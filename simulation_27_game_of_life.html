Simulation: Conway's Game of Life

A cellular automaton demonstrating emergent complexity.

Plain-Language Overview

The Game of Life, created by mathematician John Conway, is not a game in the traditional sense. It's a "zero-player game," meaning its evolution is determined by its initial state, requiring no further input. It's a world of cells on a grid, each one either "alive" or "dead." A few simple rules govern whether a cell will live, die, or be born based on how many living neighbors it has. Despite the simplicity of these rules, the Game of Life produces an astonishing variety of complex and beautiful patterns that move, grow, and interact. It's a famous example of **emergence**, where complex global behavior arises from simple local interactions.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Grid and The Cells

The simulation area is a grid of cells. Each cell can be in one of two states: **alive** (a colored square) or **dead** (the background). You can click and drag on the grid to create your own starting patterns.

2. The Rules of Life

The fate of every cell is determined at each "tick" or "generation" of the simulation. To decide if a cell will be alive or dead in the next generation, we look at its eight neighbors (horizontally, vertically, and diagonally).

  • Birth: A dead cell with **exactly 3** live neighbors becomes a live cell.
  • Survival: A live cell with **2 or 3** live neighbors survives to the next generation.
  • Death: A live cell with fewer than 2 live neighbors dies (underpopulation). A live cell with more than 3 live neighbors dies (overpopulation).

That's it! These three rules are applied to every cell on the grid simultaneously to compute the next generation.

3. Patterns of Life

From these rules, fascinating patterns emerge. Use the "Load Preset" dropdown to see some famous examples:

  • Still Lifes (e.g., Block, Beehive): These are stable patterns that do not change from one generation to the next.
  • Oscillators (e.g., Blinker, Pulsar): These patterns repeat themselves over a cycle of a few generations.
  • Spaceships (e.g., Glider, LWSS): These patterns move across the grid, eventually returning to their original shape but in a different location. The Glider is the most famous of these.
  • Complex Patterns (e.g., Gosper Glider Gun): These are larger, engineered patterns that can act as "factories," producing an endless stream of other patterns like gliders.

Try loading a "Glider" and then using the "Step" button to watch it move one generation at a time.

Micro-Experiments

  1. The Simplest Oscillator: Clear the grid. Draw a straight line of three live cells, either horizontally or vertically. Click "Step" repeatedly. You've created a "Blinker," which flips back and forth every generation.
  2. Glider Race: Load a "Glider" preset. Let it travel for a bit. Pause the simulation. Now, draw another glider somewhere else on the grid. Will they collide? Will they pass through each other? (Hint: They are just patterns, not physical objects).
  3. From Chaos comes Order: Select the "Random" preset. Run the simulation at high speed. A chaotic mess of cells will quickly boil down, leaving behind a collection of stable still lifes and simple oscillators, with a few gliders escaping the wreckage.
  4. Design a Still Life: Can you create a pattern of 4 or 5 cells that doesn't change when you click "Step"? Start with a 2x2 "Block" (which is a preset) and see if you can add a cell to it without it collapsing.
