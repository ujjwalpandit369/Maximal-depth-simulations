Nuclear Potential: The Yukawa Potential

A simulation of the potential energy between two nucleons in an atomic nucleus.

Overview

What holds the nucleus of an atom together? Protons are positively charged and should fly apart due to electrostatic repulsion. The answer is the strong nuclear force, one of the four fundamental forces of nature. This simulation explores the Yukawa potential, a simple but effective model for the strong force between two nucleons (protons or neutrons). By dragging the nucleons, you can explore the unique characteristics of this force: it's incredibly strong and attractive at short distances but vanishes almost completely at larger distances. You can also compare it to the long-range Coulomb force to see why the strong force must be so dominant within the tiny confines of the nucleus.

Beginner's Guide to the Strong Force

Let's dive into the most powerful force in the universe.

Step 1: The Nuclear Force (Yukawa Potential)

The orange curve on the graph shows the potential energy from the strong nuclear force, as modeled by the Yukawa potential. It has three key regions:

  1. The "Hard Core": At extremely close distances, the force is strongly repulsive. This acts like an impenetrable wall, preventing nucleons from crushing into each other. You can feel this if you try to push the two nucleons together.
  2. The "Attractive Well": At a slightly larger distance, the force becomes strongly attractive. This is the deep potential well that traps nucleons and binds them together to form a nucleus.
  3. The "Short-Range Tail": As the distance increases even slightly, the potential rapidly approaches zero. The force effectively vanishes. This is the most important feature of the strong force: it is powerful but has a very short range.

Step 2: The Coulomb Force - The Opponent

Protons are positively charged, so they repel each other via the electrostatic (or Coulomb) force. Check the "Show Coulomb Repulsion" box to see this potential, shown as a blue dashed line. Notice how different it is: it's always repulsive (positive potential energy) and it's long-range. It gets weaker with distance, but it never truly disappears.

Step 3: The Battle for the Nucleus

The stability of a nucleus is a battle between these two forces. For the nucleus to be stable, the attraction from the strong force must overcome the repulsion from the Coulomb force. By looking at the graph, you can see that at very short distances (inside the well), the orange curve is much more significant than the blue curve. This is why the nucleus can exist. However, if a nucleus gets too big, nucleons on opposite sides are too far apart to feel the short-range strong force, but they still feel the long-range Coulomb repulsion from all other protons, which can lead to instability.

How to Use the Simulation

  1. Drag the Nucleon: Click and drag the right-hand nucleon to change the distance between them.
  2. Watch the Graph: Observe how the white dot moves along the orange Yukawa potential curve.
  3. Feel the Forces: Try to push the nucleons very close together to feel the "hard core" repulsion. Place them in the potential well to see the most stable position.
  4. Compare Forces: Check the box to show the Coulomb potential and compare its shape and range to the Yukawa potential.

Core Theory: Yukawa vs. Coulomb

The simulation compares two potential energy functions.

1. The Yukawa Potential (Strong Nuclear Force)

U(r) = -g² * (e-r/a / r) + (Repulsive Term)

The key part is the exponential decay term, e-r/a. This term gets very small very quickly as the distance `r` increases, making the force short-range. The parameter `a` defines this range. The `-1/r` part is similar to other forces, but the exponential term overpowers it. The simulation also adds a very steep repulsive term (like `1/r¹²`) at very small `r` to model the "hard core."

2. The Coulomb Potential (Electrostatic Force)

U(r) = k * (q₁q₂ / r)

For two protons, this is a simple `1/r` potential. It is always repulsive and decreases slowly over a long range. It lacks the exponential decay term, which is why its influence is felt over much larger distances than the strong force.
