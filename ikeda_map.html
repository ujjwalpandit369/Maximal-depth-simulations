A Chaotic Butterfly in a Laser Beam

In the late 1970s, physicist Kensuke Ikeda developed a mathematical model to describe a pulse of light traveling through a non-linear optical resonator—essentially a hall of mirrors with a special material inside. He discovered that for certain parameters, the behavior of the light pulse became chaotic. The Ikeda map is a simplified version of this model.

Step 1: The Map

The simulation iterates a simple set of equations over and over:
`x_next = 1 + u * (x * cos(t) - y * sin(t))`
`y_next = u * (x * sin(t) + y * cos(t))`
Where `t` itself depends on the previous `(x, y)` point. We start with a random point and apply these rules hundreds of thousands of times, plotting each new point on the canvas.

Step 2: The Strange Attractor

Click the "Generate" button. At first, you see a scattering of points, but quickly, a definite shape begins to emerge. This shape is the system's strange attractor. It's an infinitely detailed, fractal object. The points will never land in the same place twice, and they will never settle into a simple repeating pattern, but they are forever confined to this intricate "chaotic butterfly" shape.

Step 3: Exploring the Parameters

The parameter `u` is key to the system's behavior. The default value is `u = 0.918`, which produces the classic attractor. Try slowly changing the `u` slider and clicking "Generate" again. You will see the shape of the attractor dramatically change. For some values it might become simpler, for others it might look completely different. The transition between these states is often very sudden, showcasing the sensitive nature of chaotic systems.

Step 4: Infinite Detail

Like many fractals, the Ikeda map attractor possesses self-similarity. Use your mouse wheel to zoom into one of the "feathery" parts of the attractor. As you zoom, you'll see that the thick lines resolve into more fine lines, which themselves are made of even more fine lines, and so on. This infinite complexity arising from a simple set of equations is a hallmark of chaos. You can also click and drag the mouse to pan your view around.
