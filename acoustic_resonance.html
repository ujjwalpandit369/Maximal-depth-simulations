Resonant Frequencies (Harmonics)

    Beginner's Guide — How Flutes and Organ Pipes Work

    Step 1: Waves in a Tube

    When a sound wave from the tuning fork enters the tube, it travels to the other end, reflects, and travels back. The original wave and the reflected wave then interfere with each other. Most of the time, they interfere randomly and cancel each other out. But at certain special frequencies, something magical happens.

    Step 2: Resonance and Standing Waves

    At these special "resonant" frequencies, the original and reflected waves line up perfectly to create a standing wave. A standing wave doesn't look like it's traveling; it looks like it's vibrating in place with a large amplitude. In the simulation, we visualize this by showing how far the air molecules are being displaced from their rest positions.

    • Nodes: Points where the displacement is always zero (the air isn't moving).
    • Antinodes: Points where the displacement is at its maximum (the air is moving back and forth wildly).
    When you hit a resonant frequency with the slider, the "Resonance!" indicator will light up, and you'll see the amplitude of the standing wave in the tube grow much larger. This is the principle behind all wind instruments: they are designed so that blowing into them creates sound at one of the tube's resonant frequencies, producing a loud, clear note.

    Step 3: The Importance of the Ends (Boundary Conditions)

    The type of standing wave that can form depends on what's happening at the ends of the tube. You can toggle between two types:

    1. Open at Both Ends: Air molecules can move freely at an open end, so an open end must be a displacement antinode (maximum motion). This is like a flute. For a standing wave to fit perfectly, the tube's length must be an integer multiple of half-wavelengths.
    2. Closed at One End: Air molecules cannot move at a closed end, so it must be a displacement node (zero motion). The open end is still an antinode. This is like a clarinet or an organ pipe. For a wave to fit this condition, the tube's length must be an odd integer multiple of quarter-wavelengths.

    Step 4: Harmonics

    The "Resonant Frequencies" box shows you the set of special frequencies (the "harmonics") for the current tube setup. Notice the difference:

    • The open-open tube supports all integer harmonics (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, etc.).
    • The open-closed tube only supports the odd harmonics (1st, 3rd, 5th, etc.).
    This difference in harmonic content is why instruments with different tube types have a different sound or "timbre," even when playing the same fundamental note.

    Analogy: A Guitar String. A plucked guitar string is a perfect example of a standing wave. It's fixed at both ends (two nodes). It can only vibrate at frequencies that allow a standing wave to "fit" perfectly on its length. The main note you hear is the fundamental frequency, and the richer "overtones" are the higher harmonics (2nd, 3rd, etc.), which are all present.

    Core Theory — The Mathematics of Resonance

    The resonant frequencies are determined by how many wavelengths of a standing wave can "fit" inside the tube's length `L` while satisfying the boundary conditions.

    1. Tube Open at Both Ends

    The condition is that both ends must be displacement antinodes. This allows for standing waves where the length of the tube `L` is an integer number `n` of half-wavelengths (λ).

    L = n * (λₙ / 2) (where n = 1, 2, 3, ...)

    Using the relationship `f = v / λ` (where `v` is the speed of sound, ~343 m/s), we can solve for the resonant frequencies `fₙ`:

    fₙ = n * (v / 2L)

    The first harmonic (n=1) is the fundamental frequency. The higher harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental.

    2. Tube Closed at One End

    The condition is one node (at the closed end) and one antinode (at the open end). This is only satisfied when the tube's length `L` is an odd integer number `n` of quarter-wavelengths.

    L = n * (λₙ / 4) (where n = 1, 3, 5, ...)

    Solving for the resonant frequencies `fₙ` gives:

    fₙ = n * (v / 4L)

    The fundamental frequency is `v / 4L`, which is half that of an open-open tube of the same length. The next possible resonant frequency is the 3rd harmonic (`3 * v / 4L`), then the 5th, and so on. The even harmonics are missing.
