Plain-Language Overview

The best way to understand why a scientific theory is accepted is to see how it compares to alternatives. This interactive simulation is an educational tool that demonstrates a popular "Flat Earth" model. It presents a universe where the Earth is a flat disk, with the Sun and Moon orbiting above it. The goal here is not to promote this view, but to engage in the process of science: taking a model, understanding its predictions, and comparing them to real-world observations. You can place an observer on the map, watch the Sun and Moon move, and see for yourself how the model attempts to explain day/night, seasons, and other phenomena. An explanation box will highlight where the model's predictions align with reality and, more importantly, where they fail, offering a clear lesson in how scientific theories are tested and falsified.

Beginner's Guide: Modeling a Flat Earth

Step 1: The Model

This simulation visualizes a common version of the modern Flat Earth model. Its key features are:

  • The Earth is a flat, stationary disk.
  • The North Pole is at the center of the disk.
  • Antarctica is not a continent, but a giant ice wall that encircles the disk, holding in the oceans.
  • The Sun and Moon are small spheres that orbit in circles high above the surface of the Earth.
  • The "spotlight" Sun illuminates only a portion of the disk at a time, creating day and night.
The canvas above shows a top-down view of this model. You can see the circular Earth disk, the Sun, and the Moon.

Step 2: Day, Night, and Seasons

How does this model explain day and night? The Sun acts like a moving spotlight. As it circles above the disk, it illuminates the area directly beneath it. If you are under the Sun's path, it's daytime. If you are on the other side of the disk, it's nighttime. To explain the seasons, the model proposes that the Sun's orbit changes size. In the Northern Hemisphere's summer, the Sun's circular path is smaller, centered over the Tropic of Cancer. In winter, its path is larger, centered over the Tropic of Capricorn.

Step 3: Placing an Observer

The power of a scientific model is in its ability to make testable predictions. You can participate in this by clicking anywhere on the map to place an observer (represented by a small cross). The simulation will then show you information based on what that observer should see according to the model's geometry, such as the position of the sun in their "sky."

Step 4: Testing the Model

In the controls area, you will find several buttons for "Micro-Experiments." Each one sets up the simulation to test a specific real-world phenomenon. When you click one, the simulation will run, and the "Explanation" box below will update to show you two things:

  1. The Model's Prediction: What an observer should see if the Flat Earth model were true.
  2. Observed Reality: What we actually observe in the real world.
By comparing these two, you can see where the model succeeds and where it fails. This process of comparing prediction to observation is the heart of the scientific method.

The Goal: Falsifiability

A theory isn't scientific just because it provides an explanation. It must be "falsifiable"—meaning there must be some conceivable observation that could prove it wrong. The Flat Earth model is perfectly falsifiable. It makes many concrete predictions about the world that we can go out and test. As you will see through the micro-experiments, its predictions consistently fail to match reality, which is why the scientific consensus is that the Earth is a globe.

Core Theory: The Simulated Model

The simulation is based on a common geometric version of the Flat Earth model.

  • Earth Disk: The Earth is a flat disk with a radius corresponding to the distance from the North Pole to the edge of Antarctica.
  • Sun's Path: The Sun orbits at a constant altitude. Its orbital radius changes with the seasons. We model this with a sine wave based on the day of the year.
    rsun(d) = rTropic of Cancer + (rTropic of Capricorn - rTropic of Cancer) * (1 + sin(2π(d - 81)/365))/2
  • Moon's Path: The Moon follows a similar path to the Sun but with its own period, slightly offset.
  • Observer's View: When an observer is placed on the map, we use trigonometry to calculate the apparent angle (azimuth and elevation) of the Sun and Moon in their sky.

FAQ & Falsifications

1. What is the simplest proof that the Earth is a globe?
There are many. One is watching a ship sail away from shore. The hull disappears over the horizon before the mast does. This is only possible if the surface is curved. Another is the existence of different constellations in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. On a flat Earth, everyone should see the same stars.
2. How does this model explain gravity?
It generally doesn't, which is a major weakness. Most models propose that the disk is accelerating upwards at 9.8 m/s², which would mimic the feeling of gravity. However, this leads to other problems, such as requiring the disk to eventually travel faster than the speed of light.
3. Why do lunar eclipses disprove this model?
A lunar eclipse occurs when the Earth passes between the Sun and Moon, casting a shadow on the Moon. For this to happen in the Flat Earth model, the Sun and Moon would have to be on opposite sides of the disk, with the Earth in the middle. But the model requires them to always be *above* the disk. Furthermore, the Earth's shadow on the Moon is always circular, regardless of the time of night it occurs, which is only possible if the Earth is a sphere.
4. Why do people believe this?
The reasons are complex and varied, often involving a deep distrust of conventional sources of authority like governments and scientific institutions, combined with the power of online communities to create and reinforce alternative worldviews.
