Guide: Finding Order in Chaos

The Unknowable Hamiltonian

In quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian is an operator that determines the possible energy levels of a system. For a simple system like a hydrogen atom, we can write down and solve the Hamiltonian precisely. But for a complex system like a heavy atomic nucleus, with hundreds of interacting protons and neutrons, the exact Hamiltonian is impossibly complicated and effectively unknown.

The Random Matrix Approach

Random Matrix Theory (RMT) offers a powerful alternative. Instead of trying to write down the true Hamiltonian, it proposes modeling it as a large matrix filled with random numbers, constrained only by the fundamental symmetries of the system (e.g., it must be symmetric if time-reversal symmetry holds). The incredible discovery, pioneered by Eugene Wigner, is that the statistical properties of the eigenvalues (the energy levels) of such matrices do not depend on the specific details of the random numbers, but instead follow universal laws.

Level Repulsion and the Wigner Surmise

One of the most important predictions of RMT concerns the spacing between adjacent energy levels. If the energy levels were truly random and uncorrelated, the probability of finding a very small spacing would be high (the blue Poisson curve). However, RMT predicts that for chaotic quantum systems, the energy levels actively "repel" each other, making small spacings extremely rare. This phenomenon is called level repulsion.

The distribution of these spacings is accurately described by the red Wigner surmise. Notice how this curve goes to zero as the spacing `s` goes to zero. As you generate more random Hamiltonians in the simulation, you will see the histogram of their level spacings perfectly converge to the Wigner surmise, not the Poisson curve, providing stunning evidence for the predictive power of RMT.
