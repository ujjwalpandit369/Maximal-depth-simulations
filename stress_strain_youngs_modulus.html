Welcome to the Material Tester!

Why is steel used for buildings, but rubber is used for tires? It's because they have very different properties when you pull on them. This simulation lets you be a materials scientist and test these properties.

Step 1: The Setup

You see a metal rod clamped at the top, and a graph. The graph is the most important part: a Stress-Strain Curve.

  • Stress (vertical axis): This is how much force is applied per unit of area. Think of it as how hard we're pulling on the rod, normalized. We measure it in Megapascals (MPa).
  • Strain (horizontal axis): This is how much the rod stretches, as a percentage of its original length. A strain of 0.01 means the rod has stretched by 1%.

Step 2: Choose a Material

Start with Steel, which should be selected by default. Each material has its own unique "fingerprint" in the form of its stress-strain curve.

Step 3: Pull the Rod!

Use the "Applied Stress" slider to start pulling on the rod. As you increase the stress, you'll see two things happen: the rod in the animation gets longer, and a line is drawn on the graph.

Step 4: The Elastic Region and Young's Modulus

At first, the graph is a perfectly straight line. This is the elastic region. If you were to let go of the rod here, it would snap back to its original length, just like a rubber band. The slope of this straight line is called Young's Modulus. It's a measure of the material's stiffness. Steel has a very steep slope, meaning it's very stiff—it takes a lot of stress to cause a little strain.

Step 5: The Plastic Region and Failure

Keep increasing the stress. Eventually, the line will start to curve. This is the Yield Point. Beyond this point, the material has permanently deformed. If you let go now, it would stay stretched out. This is called plastic deformation.
As you increase the stress further, you'll reach the Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS)—the maximum stress the material can handle. After this point, the material starts to "neck" (get thin in one spot) and quickly fails. The final point on the graph is the Fracture Point, where the rod breaks.

Step 6: Compare Materials

Now, click the "Reset" button and select Rubber. Try pulling on it with the slider. The curve looks completely different! It has a very low Young's Modulus (it's not stiff at all) and can stretch to enormous strains before breaking. By comparing the curves for Steel, Aluminum, and Rubber, you can build an intuition for why they are used in such different applications.

Stress (σ)

Stress is the internal force per unit area that particles of a body exert on each other. In this tensile test, it's the applied force (F) divided by the original cross-sectional area (A₀) of the rod.

Equation: `σ = F / A₀`
Units: Pascals (Pa) or Megapascals (MPa). 1 MPa = 1,000,000 Pa.

Strain (ε)

Strain is the measure of deformation. It is the change in length (ΔL) divided by the original length (L₀).

Equation: `ε = ΔL / L₀`
Units: Strain is dimensionless (e.g., m/m), but is often expressed as a percentage.

Young's Modulus (E)

Also known as the elastic modulus, this is a measure of a material's stiffness in its elastic region. It is the ratio of stress to strain in the linear portion of the curve.

Equation: `E = σ / ε` (only for elastic deformation)

Plain English: A high Young's Modulus means a material is very stiff and hard to stretch (like steel). A low value means it is flexible (like rubber).
