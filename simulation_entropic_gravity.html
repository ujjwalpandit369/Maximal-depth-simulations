Overview

Is gravity a fundamental force of nature, or is it something else entirely? Entropic gravity, a speculative idea proposed by Erik Verlinde, suggests that gravity is an emergent phenomenon, much like temperature or pressure. It arises from the universe's statistical tendency to maximize disorder, or entropy. This theory connects gravity to thermodynamics and information theory, suggesting that the "force" we feel is actually a consequence of information encoded on a "holographic screen" that surrounds us. This simulation provides a conceptual look at how the movement of a particle can alter the information on such a screen, giving rise to an entropic force that mimics gravity.

Beginner's Guide to Emergent Gravity

1. What is an Emergent Force?

Not all forces are fundamental. Temperature, for example, isn't a property of a single water molecule. It's an *emergent* property of many molecules moving around. The collective motion gives rise to the phenomenon we call temperature. Similarly, the pressure a gas exerts on a container's walls isn't a fundamental force; it's the result of countless atoms bouncing off the surface.

Entropic gravity proposes that gravity is like this. It's not a fundamental interaction between masses, but a macroscopic effect that emerges from the microscopic behavior of information and entropy.

2. An Everyday Analogy: A Polymer Chain

Imagine a long chain of molecules, like a rubber band (a polymer). If you hold the two ends, the chain can wiggle and bend in countless different ways. The crumpled, tangled-up states are far more numerous—and thus have higher entropy (disorder)—than the stretched-out, straight state.

If you pull the ends of the rubber band apart, you are forcing it into a low-entropy state. When you let go, it snaps back. This isn't because of a mysterious "attraction" between the ends. It's because the universe statistically favors states with higher entropy. The chain snaps back simply because there are overwhelmingly more ways for it to be crumpled than to be straight. This statistical tendency creates a real, measurable force—an entropic force.

3. The Holographic Screen

A key idea in modern physics is the "holographic principle," which suggests that all the information contained within a volume of space can be encoded on its boundary, like a 2D hologram storing a 3D image. In this theory, this boundary is a "holographic screen".

In our simulation, the grid of flashing pixels on the left represents this holographic screen. Each pixel is a "bit" of information. The circle on the right is a test mass (like a planet or a star). When the test mass is present, it "blots out" or "deletes" some information on the screen. The closer the mass is to the screen, the more information is lost.

4. Entropy as a Force

The universe wants to maximize entropy. When the particle moves away from the screen, it "frees up" bits of information, increasing the total entropy. This creates a statistical pull on the particle, drawing it back towards the screen to restore the lower-entropy state. This "pull" is what we perceive as gravity. In the simulation, you can see this visualized as a force arrow pointing from the particle towards the screen. The strength of the force depends on the particle's mass (how much information it represents) and its distance from the screen.

Core Theory: Information and Entropy

The theory connects thermodynamics, information, and gravity.

1. The Holographic Principle and Entropy

The entropy `S` associated with a holographic screen is proportional to its area `A`. When a particle of mass `m` is at a distance `Δx` from the screen, it changes the entropy.

ΔS = 2πk_B * (mc/ħ) * Δx

Plain English: The change in entropy (`ΔS`) is proportional to the mass of the particle (`m`) and its distance (`Δx`) from the screen. `k_B`, `c`, and `ħ` are Boltzmann's constant, the speed of light, and the reduced Planck constant, respectively.

2. The Entropic Force Law

The fundamental idea is that a change in entropy over a distance creates a force, analogous to a thermodynamic pressure. The equation is elegantly simple:

F * Δx = T * ΔS

Plain English: A force `F` acting over a distance `Δx` is equal to the temperature `T` of the holographic screen multiplied by the change in entropy `ΔS`. By substituting the Unruh temperature `T = (ħa)/(2πck_B)` (where `a` is acceleration) and the entropy formula from above, one can derive Newton's law of gravitation, `F = GmM/r²`, suggesting gravity is not fundamental but emergent.
