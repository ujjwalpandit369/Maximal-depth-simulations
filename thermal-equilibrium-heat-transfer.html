Thermal Equilibrium: Heat Transfer

An interactive simulation of two objects transferring heat until they reach the same temperature.

Overview

This simulation demonstrates the concept of thermal equilibrium. When two objects at different temperatures come into contact, heat energy flows from the hotter object to the colder one until their temperatures are equal. This balanced state is called thermal equilibrium. Here, you can set the initial temperatures and masses of two blocks, and observe how they exchange heat and reach a final, common temperature. The motion of the particles inside each block gives a visual sense of its internal kinetic energy (temperature).

Beginner’s Guide to Heat Transfer

Ever touched a hot mug of cocoa and felt the warmth spread to your hands? Or added a cold ice cube to a drink and watched it get cooler? You've experienced thermal equilibrium! Let's explore the simple rules that govern this everyday phenomenon.

Step 1: What is Temperature? (The Jiggling Particles)

Everything is made of tiny particles (atoms and molecules) that are constantly jiggling and moving. Temperature is a measure of the average energy of this jiggling motion. In our simulation, you can see this directly:

  • In a hot block (red), the particles are moving very fast and erratically.
  • In a cold block (blue), the particles are much slower and calmer.
So, "hot" just means "fast-jiggling particles," and "cold" means "slow-jiggling particles."

Step 2: What is Heat? (The Flow of Energy)

Heat is not something an object *has*; it's the transfer of energy from a hotter object to a colder one. This flow always goes in one direction: from hot to cold. It's like pouring water from a full bucket into an emptier one; the water only flows downhill.

When our two blocks touch, the fast particles in the hot block collide with the slow particles in the cold block at the boundary. In these collisions, the fast particles give some of their energy to the slow ones. This slows down the hot block's particles (cooling it down) and speeds up the cold block's particles (warming it up).

Step 3: What is Thermal Equilibrium? (The Final Balance)

This energy transfer continues until the particles in both blocks are jiggling with the same average energy. At this point, their temperatures are equal. The energy transfer doesn't stop completely—particles still bump into each other—but the energy transferred from A to B is now equal to the energy transferred from B to A. The net flow is zero. This final, stable state is called thermal equilibrium.

Step 4: Why Mass Matters (Heat Capacity)

Imagine trying to heat up a cup of water versus a swimming pool with the same small heater. The cup of water will get hot very quickly, while the swimming pool's temperature will barely change. The pool has a much larger mass and therefore a higher "heat capacity"—it takes more energy to change its temperature.

In our simulation, a more massive block requires more energy to heat up and must lose more energy to cool down. When a massive hot block touches a small cold block, the final temperature will be much closer to the hot block's original temperature, because the small block doesn't have enough "thermal pull" to change it significantly.

Worked Walkthroughs & Micro-Experiments

Walkthrough 1: The Effect of Mass

  1. Click the "Preset: Big & Small" button. This sets up a large, hot block (50kg, 100°C) and a small, cold block (5kg, 0°C).
  2. Before you begin, look at the "Final Temp" readout. The simulation calculates it will be 90.9°C. Why is it so close to 100°C? Because the massive block has more thermal inertia.
  3. Press the "Start" button and observe. The color of the large block barely changes, while the small block rapidly turns reddish-orange. The temperatures meet exactly where predicted.

Walkthrough 2: The Effect of Conductivity

  1. Click the "Reset" button for a standard setup (80°C and 20°C, equal masses).
  2. Slide the "Thermal Conductivity" slider all the way to the left (minimum). Press "Start". Notice how slowly the temperatures creep towards the middle. This is like trying to heat a room through a well-insulated wall.
  3. Now, click "Reset" again. Slide the conductivity all the way to the right (maximum). Press "Start". The temperatures equalize almost instantly. This is like the two blocks being made of copper.

Micro-Experiments to Try

  • Perfectly Balanced: Reset the simulation. The initial temperatures are 80°C and 20°C with equal 10kg masses. Predict the final temperature before looking at the readout. (Hint: It should be the exact average).
  • Ice Cube in Coffee: Set Block A (the "coffee") to be hot (90°C) and massive (30kg). Set Block B (the "ice cube") to be cold (0°C) and small (2kg). What is the final temperature? How much did the coffee's temperature actually drop?
  • The Insulator: Set up a large temperature difference (100°C vs 0°C). Set conductivity to its absolute minimum. Does heat still transfer? Yes, just very, very slowly. Perfect insulators do not exist in reality.
  • Reversing the Flow: Set Block A to 20°C and Block B to 80°C. Observe that the heat now flows from B to A. The direction is only dependent on the temperature difference.

Core Theory of Thermal Equilibrium

The concepts of heat transfer are governed by the laws of thermodynamics. Thermal equilibrium is a direct consequence of these foundational laws.

Heat Transfer by Conduction

The simulation models heat transfer via conduction. The rate at which heat (Q) flows over time (t) is proportional to the temperature difference (ΔT) and the conductivity (k) of the interface.

dQ⁄dt ∝ k × (Thot - Tcold)

This equation tells us that the bigger the temperature difference, the faster the heat will flow. As the blocks approach the same temperature, the rate of transfer slows down, eventually becoming zero at equilibrium.

Calculating the Final Temperature

When two objects exchange heat in an isolated system, the heat energy lost by the hot object is equal to the heat energy gained by the cold one. The energy required to change an object's temperature is given by q = mcΔT, where 'c' is the specific heat capacity. Assuming both blocks are made of the same material (i.e., they have the same 'c'), we can find the final temperature (Tf) with a weighted average:

Tf = (mATA, initial + mBTB, initial) ⁄ (mA + mB)

Worked Example: Using the default values, mA=10, TA=80, mB=10, TB=20.
Tf = (10kg × 80°C + 10kg × 20°C) / (10kg + 10kg)
Tf = (800 + 200) / 20 = 1000 / 20 = 50°C.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. What's the difference between heat and temperature?
Temperature is a measure of the average internal energy of an object's particles (how fast they are jiggling). Heat is the transfer of that energy from a hotter object to a colder one. An object has temperature, but it doesn't "have" heat; heat is a flow.
2. Why do the particles move?
In reality, atoms and molecules are never perfectly still (unless at absolute zero, -273.15°C). Their constant motion is a fundamental property of matter and is what we measure as temperature.
3. Why does a more massive object change temperature more slowly?
Because it has more particles. To raise the average energy (temperature) of a million particles by 1 degree, you need to add much more total energy than you would for a thousand particles. This resistance to temperature change is called heat capacity.
4. What is thermal conductivity?
It's a material property that describes how well heat can travel through it. Metals have high conductivity (a metal spoon in hot soup gets hot quickly). Styrofoam and wood have low conductivity (they are good insulators).
5. What happens if the blocks are made of different materials?
Then we would also need to consider their "specific heat capacity" (c). A material with a high specific heat (like water) requires more energy to raise its temperature than a material with a low one (like copper). The formula would become Tf = (mAcATA + mBcBTB) / (mAcA + mBcB).
