The Feigenbaum Constants

A simulation to numerically estimate the Feigenbaum constants from the logistic map.

Est. runtime: N/A | Recommended device: Desktop | Approx. size: 28 KB

Overview

In the 1970s, physicist Mitchell Feigenbaum discovered a profound and unexpected universality in the way systems transition to chaos. He found that the "period-doubling cascade"—a common route to chaos seen in the logistic map and many other systems—has a universal geometric structure. This structure is described by two constants, now named after him. This simulation allows you to explore the logistic map's bifurcation diagram and use interactive measurement tools to numerically estimate the Feigenbaum constants δ (delta) and α (alpha) for yourself, revealing the deep, hidden order within chaos.

Beginner's Guide to Universal Chaos

Step 1: The Bifurcation Diagram

The canvas shows the bifurcation diagram for the logistic map, x → r * x * (1 - x). The horizontal axis is the control parameter `r`. The vertical axis shows the long-term values that `x` settles into for a given `r`. You can see the period-doubling cascade: a single branch splits into two, then four, then eight, and so on, until it dissolves into chaos.

To Zoom: Click and drag on the diagram to select a rectangle. The view will zoom into that region. Click "Reset View" to zoom out.

Step 2: Measuring δ (Delta) - The Rate of Chaos

The constant `δ ≈ 4.669` describes the scaling of the `r` parameter. It's the ratio of the lengths of successive intervals between bifurcations. As the cascade progresses, the next split always happens 4.669... times faster than the last one.

How to measure:

  1. Click the "Measure δ" button to activate the tool.
  2. Zoom into the diagram where you can clearly see several splits.
  3. Click on three successive bifurcation points (where a branch splits in two). The simulation will mark your clicks as `r₁, r₂, r₃`.
  4. The readout will automatically calculate the ratio `(r₂ - r₁) / (r₃ - r₂)` and show you the result. See how close you can get to 4.669!

Step 3: Measuring α (Alpha) - The Shape of Chaos

The constant `α ≈ 2.502` describes the scaling of the `x` variable. It's the ratio of the width of a "tine" in a fork to the width of the smaller tine it splits into.

How to measure:

  1. Click the "Measure α" button.
  2. Zoom way in on a single, clear bifurcation "fork."
  3. Click on the main branch just before the split.
  4. Click on the upper branch just after the split. The simulation will measure the vertical distance `d₁`.
  5. Now click on the upper of the two smaller branches that split off from that one. The simulation will measure the vertical distance `d₂`.
  6. The readout will calculate the ratio `d₁ / d₂` and show you the result. See how close you can get to 2.502!
