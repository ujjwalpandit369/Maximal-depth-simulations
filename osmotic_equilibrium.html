Guide: Water's Drive to Dilute

What is Osmosis?

Osmosis is the spontaneous movement of solvent molecules (like water) through a selectively permeable membrane into a region of higher solute concentration. The goal is to equalize the solute concentrations on the two sides. It's a fundamental process in biology—it's how plant roots absorb water and how our kidneys function.

In this simulation:

  1. The U-tube is divided by a semi-permeable membrane, which allows small water molecules to pass but blocks larger solute molecules (like sugar).
  2. The right side contains pure water (solvent), while the left side contains a solution with a specific concentration of solute.
  3. Because the concentration of water is effectively lower on the left side (due to the space taken up by solute), there is a net flow of water from right to left to try and dilute the solution.

Osmotic Pressure vs. Hydrostatic Pressure

As water flows into the left arm, the liquid level rises. This creates a height difference, `h`, which results in a downward pressure due to gravity. This is called hydrostatic pressure.

  • Osmotic Pressure (Π): This is the "pulling" pressure generated by the concentration difference, driving water across the membrane.
  • Hydrostatic Pressure (Pₖ): This is the "pushing" pressure generated by the weight of the extra column of liquid, which opposes the osmotic flow.
The system reaches osmotic equilibrium when the hydrostatic pressure grows large enough to exactly balance the osmotic pressure. At this point, the net flow of water across the membrane becomes zero, and the height difference stabilizes.

Core Theory

The equilibrium state is determined by the balance of two pressures.

1. Osmotic Pressure (van 't Hoff Equation)

The pressure difference that must be applied to a solution to prevent the inward flow of its pure solvent across a semipermeable membrane is known as its osmotic pressure, `Π`.

Π = i * M * R * T
  • `i` is the van 't Hoff factor (dimensionless). For sugar (non-ionizing), `i = 1`.
  • `M` is the molar concentration of the solute (mol/L or mol/m³).
  • `R` is the ideal gas constant (8.314 J/(mol·K)).
  • `T` is the absolute temperature in Kelvin (we assume 298 K or 25°C).

2. Hydrostatic Pressure

The pressure exerted by a column of fluid due to gravity is its hydrostatic pressure, `Pₖ`.

Pₖ = ρ * g * h
  • `ρ` (rho) is the density of the fluid (for water, ~1000 kg/m³).
  • `g` is the acceleration due to gravity (~9.81 m/s²).
  • `h` is the height difference of the fluid columns.

Equilibrium Condition

Equilibrium is reached when the two pressures are equal: `Pₖ = Π`. Therefore, the final height difference is:

h = (iMRT) / (ρg)
