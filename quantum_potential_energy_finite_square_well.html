Overview

Welcome to the quantum world! This simulation explores one of the fundamental problems in quantum mechanics: a particle trapped in a "finite potential well." Unlike a classical ball that can have any amount of energy, a trapped quantum particle is only allowed to exist at specific, discrete energy levels. This simulation calculates these allowed "bound states" for you. You can change the depth and width of the well to see how many states it can hold. By selecting an energy level, you can visualize its wavefunction, which describes the probability of finding the particle at any given position. You'll witness one of the most bizarre quantum effects: the particle has a chance to be found outside the well, in a "classically forbidden" region, an effect known as quantum tunneling.

Beginner's Guide to a Quantum Well

The quantum world operates by different rules than our everyday world. This guide will help you understand the key concepts shown in the simulation.

Step 1: The Potential Well

The U-shaped black region on the diagram is the "potential well." It's a region of space that is attractive to a particle. Think of it as a valley or a hole that the particle can get "stuck" in. The depth of the well (U₀) represents how strong the attraction is. The width (L) is its size. Outside the well, the potential energy is zero.

Step 2: Quantized Energy Levels

In our classical world, a marble in a bowl can rest at any height. In the quantum world, this is not true. A particle trapped in the well can only have very specific, discrete energy levels. This is called quantization.

Everyday Analogy: A Guitar String. A guitar string can't vibrate in any random shape. It can only vibrate at specific frequencies: its fundamental tone and its harmonics. The allowed energy levels of the particle are like the allowed harmonics of the string. The simulation calculates these allowed levels and displays them as green lines.

Step 3: The Wavefunction (ψ)

In quantum mechanics, a particle isn't a tiny dot. It's described by a wavefunction, represented by the symbol ψ (psi). The wavefunction itself isn't directly observable, but its shape is crucial. The height of the wavefunction squared (ψ²) at any point tells you the probability of finding the particle at that location.

When you click on an energy level, the simulation plots its wavefunction in orange. Where the wave is biggest (positive or negative), you are most likely to find the particle. Where it's zero, you will never find the particle.

Step 4: Quantum Tunneling - The "Impossible"

Look closely at the wavefunctions. You'll notice that they don't stop at the edge of the well. They "leak" out into the regions where the particle classically shouldn't be able to go (since it doesn't have enough energy to escape). This is quantum tunneling.

Analogy: Sound Through a Wall. Imagine you're in a room and can hear a muffled conversation from next door. The sound waves are "tunneling" through the wall to reach you, even though the wall is a barrier. The particle's wavefunction does something similar, decaying exponentially the farther it gets into the forbidden region.

Step 5: Using the Simulation

  1. Use the Sliders: Adjust the "Well Depth (U₀)" and "Well Width (L)".
  2. Observe the Effect: Notice how a deeper or wider well can hold more bound states (more green lines appear). A very shallow or narrow well might not be able to hold any states at all!
  3. Select a State: Click on or near one of the green energy level lines. Its wavefunction will be plotted. Compare the shapes of the different wavefunctions. The lowest energy state (the "ground state") has the simplest shape, and they get more complex as you go up in energy.

Core Theory: Solving the Schrödinger Equation

The energy levels and wavefunctions are solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation for the potential well. By applying boundary conditions (that the wavefunction and its derivative must be continuous at the edges of the well), we arrive at two transcendental equations that must be solved numerically. In simplified units where `2m/ħ² = 1`:

Let E be the energy (a negative value), U₀ be the well depth, and L be the width. Define:

α = sqrt(-E) and k = sqrt(E + U₀)

1. For Even (Symmetric) States:

k * tan(kL/2) = α

2. For Odd (Anti-symmetric) States:

-k * cot(kL/2) = α

This simulation finds the values of `E` between `-U₀` and `0` that solve these equations. It does this by finding the intersections of the graphs of the left-hand side and right-hand side. Once an energy `E` is found, its wavefunction `ψ(x)` is plotted.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Why are the units for energy and length arbitrary?
To make the underlying equations simpler, physicists often work in "natural units" where constants like the mass of the particle (m) and Planck's constant (ħ) are set to 1. The simulation does this so we can focus on the relationships between depth, width, and energy, rather than specific physical numbers.
2. Why do more bound states appear when the well is deeper or wider?
A deeper/wider well provides more "room" for wavefunctions to "fit" inside while still satisfying the boundary conditions. A very shallow/narrow well may be too small to fit even one half-wavelength, so no bound state can exist.
3. What do the different shapes of the wavefunctions mean?
The number of "humps" in the wavefunction corresponds to its energy level. The ground state (lowest energy) always has one hump. The next state up has two, and so on. The points where the wavefunction crosses zero are called "nodes."
4. What happens if the energy is positive?
If E > 0, the particle is not trapped in the well. It's a "free particle" that can have any energy. These are called "scattering states," and they are not shown in this simulation, which focuses on the "bound states" (E < 0).
