Beginner's Guide — How Two Slits Create Many Fringes

Step 1: Light as a Wave

The most important idea in this experiment is that light behaves like a wave. Imagine ripples spreading out in a pond. In the top simulation, you can see these waves (as black and white bands) moving from left to right.

Step 2: Passing Through the Slits

The waves encounter a barrier with two narrow slits. According to Huygens' Principle, each of these slits acts like a brand new source of waves. So, on the other side of the barrier, we now have two new sets of circular waves spreading out and overlapping with each other.

Step 3: Interference - The Magic

When two waves overlap, they "interfere." This can happen in two ways:

  • Constructive Interference: If a crest from one wave meets a crest from the other wave, they add up to create a "super-crest." In the simulation, this is where a white band from one slit crosses a white band from the other. This results in a bright spot on the screen.
  • Destructive Interference: If a crest from one wave meets a trough (a dark band) from the other wave, they cancel each other out completely. This results in a dark spot on the screen.

This alternating pattern of constructive and destructive interference is what creates the series of bright and dark "fringes" that you see on the screen at the far right of the top canvas.

Step 4: The Intensity Plot

The bottom canvas shows a graph of the brightness (intensity) of the pattern on the screen. The high peaks correspond to the bright fringes (constructive interference) and the valleys at zero correspond to the dark fringes (destructive interference).

Step 5: Playing with the Parameters

This simulation lets you see how the pattern changes. Try adjusting the sliders:

  • Wavelength (λ): Increase the wavelength (move the slider to the right, towards red). Notice that the fringes on the screen get wider and more spread out.
  • Slit Separation (d): Decrease the distance between the slits. This also causes the fringes to become more spread out. It's a bit counter-intuitive, but a smaller gap produces a wider pattern!
  • Distance to Screen (L): The barrier is on the left edge of the canvas. The screen is on the right. This slider moves the barrier left and right. Moving the barrier further from the screen (a larger L) also makes the pattern wider.

Core Theory — The Mathematics of Interference

The position of the fringes can be predicted with simple geometry.

Condition for Bright Fringes (Maxima)

A bright fringe occurs when the path difference from the two slits to a point on the screen is an integer multiple (`m`) of the wavelength `λ`. The path difference is approximately `d * sin(θ)`.

d * sin(θ) = m * λ (where m = 0, ±1, ±2, ...)

Condition for Dark Fringes (Minima)

A dark fringe occurs when the path difference is a half-integer multiple of the wavelength.

d * sin(θ) = (m + 1/2) * λ (where m = 0, ±1, ±2, ...)

Position on the Screen

For a screen far away, we can use the small angle approximation: `sin(θ) ≈ y / L`, where `y` is the position on the screen and `L` is the distance to the screen. This allows us to predict the position of the bright fringes:

y_bright = (m * λ * L) / d

This simple formula is the heart of the simulation. It shows that the fringe spacing `Δy` between two adjacent bright fringes is `(λL)/d`. This is exactly what you observe when you play with the sliders.

Intensity Profile

The brightness of the pattern is not just on or off. It varies smoothly. The intensity `I` at an angle `θ` is given by:

I(θ) = I_max * cos²( (π * d * sin(θ)) / λ )

This is the formula used to draw the smooth curve in the "Intensity Pattern" plot.
