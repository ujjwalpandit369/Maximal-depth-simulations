Loops, Glue, and Topology

In a field of math called algebraic topology, we study the properties of shapes that don't change when you stretch or bend them (without tearing). One of the most powerful ideas is the fundamental group, which is essentially a collection of all the different kinds of "loops" you can draw on a shape.

Step 1: Our First Shape

Click button "1. Space U₁". The canvas shows a schematic of a torus (a donut) with a small hole in it. This is our first space, U₁. We can draw two fundamental, independent loops on this shape, labeled a and b. Any other loop can be made by combining these two. We say the fundamental group is "generated" by a and b, written as π₁(U₁) = ⟨a, b⟩.

Step 2: Our Second Shape

Click button "2. Space U₂". This shows another punctured torus, U₂, with its own loop generators, c and d. Its group is π₁(U₂) = ⟨c, d⟩.

Step 3: The Intersection

Now, imagine we are going to glue these two shapes together along the boundary of their holes. Click button "3. Intersection U₁∩U₂". The "glue" region is their intersection, which is topologically a cylinder (or, viewed end-on, a circle). A circle has only one type of loop, which we'll call f. So, π₁(U₁∩U₂) = ⟨f⟩.

Step 4: The Glued Space

Click button "4. The Union X = U₁∪U₂". We now see the resulting shape, which is a "genus-2 surface" or a double-donut. All the loops from the original spaces are still there.

Step 5: The Seifert-van Kampen Theorem

Here's the magic. Click button "5. The Final Group π₁(X)". The theorem tells us how to find the loops on the new shape. We start with all the generators from the original shapes: a, b, c, d. But we have to account for the gluing.

The loop f in the intersection corresponds to some combination of loops in U₁ and some combination in U₂. In this case, `f` is equivalent to the loop that goes around the hole in U₁, and also equivalent to the loop around the hole in U₂.

When we glue them, we are effectively saying these two loops are now the same. This creates a relation. The theorem gives us the fundamental group of the new space `X` by combining the generators and adding the relation. The visualization now shows the final set of four independent loops on our double-donut.
