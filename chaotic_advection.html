Stirring with Chaos

How do you effectively mix cream into coffee? You stir it, creating a complex, turbulent flow. But what if you could only create very simple flows? This simulation shows that you can still achieve fantastic mixing by simply alternating between two simple flows. This process is called chaotic advection.

Step 1: The Blinking Vortices

The fluid chamber is subject to a "blinking vortex" flow. This means:

  1. For a period of time, a vortex (a swirling flow) is active on the left side of the chamber.
  2. Then, it turns off, and a second vortex turns on at the right side of the chamber for the same period.
This cycle repeats. At any given moment, the flow itself is very simple, regular, and non-chaotic. The chaos emerges from the *alternation* between the two flows.

Step 2: Add the Dye

Click anywhere inside the chamber. A blob of colored dye will be placed at that location. The dye is represented by thousands of individual tracer particles that are carried along by the fluid flow (this is "advection").

Step 3: Stretch and Fold

Click the "Start/Pause" button to begin the flow. Watch the blob of dye.

The first vortex will pick up the blob and begin to stretch it out into a long filament. When the second vortex turns on, it catches this filament and begins to fold it back on itself, while stretching it even further.

As this "stretch and fold" mechanism repeats over and over, the initial blob is transformed into an incredibly complex, intricate pattern that resembles a fractal. The dye rapidly spreads and appears to mix throughout the entire chamber. This is the power of chaotic advection: it uses a simple, time-dependent process to create the complex behavior needed for efficient mixing. This principle is used in many industrial applications, from chemical reactors to food processing.
