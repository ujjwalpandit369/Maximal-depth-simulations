A Universe with "Domain Walls"

Visualizing topological defects from a phase transition.

Overview

As the incredibly hot and dense early universe expanded and cooled, it likely underwent one or more **phase transitions**, similar to steam condensing into water. **Domain walls** are hypothetical 2D "cracks" in spacetime that could have formed during such a transition if a simple symmetry was broken. This simulation visualizes this process: different regions of the universe "freeze" into different vacuum states, and the boundaries where these regions meet form a persistent network of high-energy domain walls.

Beginner's Guide: Cracks in the Cosmos

Step 1: A Symmetrical Universe

In the extreme heat of the very early universe, the fundamental forces of nature were unified, and the "vacuum" of space was in a highly symmetrical state. There was no distinction between "up" or "down," "left" or "right" in a fundamental sense.

Analogy: Imagine a perfectly still pond of water. Every point on the surface is identical to every other. The laws of physics for a water molecule are the same everywhere. This is a symmetrical state.

Step 2: The Phase Transition

As the universe expanded and cooled, it went through a **phase transition**. The underlying physics had to "choose" a new, lower-energy state, a process called **spontaneous symmetry breaking**. In our pond analogy, this is like the temperature dropping below freezing. Ice crystals begin to form. The random orientation of the first ice crystal "breaks" the perfect symmetry of the liquid water.

Step 3: Conflicting Choices

Because no signal can travel faster than light, different regions of the universe that were causally disconnected from each other had to make this choice independently. In the simulation, this choice is between the "Red Vacuum" and the "Blue Vacuum."

Press "Start Phase Transition". You will see bubbles of red and blue nucleate at random points and expand outwards at the speed of light. This is like ice crystals forming in different parts of the pond simultaneously, each with its own random orientation.

Step 4: The Domain Walls

What happens when these bubbles meet? If two red bubbles meet, they merge seamlessly. But if a red bubble meets a blue bubble, they can't. A boundary forms between them. This boundary—a "crack" between two different regions of space—is a **domain wall**. In the simulation, these are the bright yellow lines. They are stable, high-energy structures trapped between the two different vacuum states.

Step 5: The Domain Wall Problem

If stable domain walls like these had formed in our universe, they would be catastrophic. Their immense energy and gravitational pull would dominate the evolution of the cosmos, and we would see their effects everywhere. Since we don't see any, this is a serious problem for any physical theory that predicts their formation. This is one of the puzzles that the theory of cosmic inflation is thought to solve, by ensuring that any single domain grew so large that all the walls were pushed far beyond the edge of our observable universe.

Core Theory: Topological Defects

Domain walls are a type of **topological defect** that can be formed during a phase transition. The type of defect depends on the type of symmetry that is broken.

  • Domain Walls (2D): Formed from the breaking of a discrete symmetry (e.g., a choice between two distinct states, like up/down or +1/-1). This is what is shown in the simulation.
  • Cosmic Strings (1D): Formed from the breaking of an axial or cylindrical symmetry. These would be incredibly thin, massive filaments stretching across the universe.
  • Monopoles (0D): Formed from the breaking of a spherical symmetry. These would be point-like particles with a single magnetic charge (e.g., just a North pole), which have never been observed.

The study of these potential defects is a crucial way to test and constrain theories about the physics of the very early universe.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

Have we ever seen a domain wall?
No, there is no observational evidence for cosmological domain walls. Their existence is strongly disfavored by observations of the cosmic microwave background. However, similar structures (magnetic domain walls) exist and are studied in condensed matter physics, for example in ferromagnetic materials.
How does inflation solve the domain wall problem?
The theory of cosmic inflation proposes a period of hyper-accelerated expansion in the first fraction of a second. If this happened, our entire observable universe would have originated from a single, microscopically tiny region. This region would have been small enough to have settled into a single vacuum state (e.g., "blue"). Any domain walls would have been pushed so far away by the expansion that they are now far beyond our cosmic horizon, making them impossible for us to ever see.
