The Leaky Faucet (Phase Space View)

A phase space visualization for the dripping faucet experiment.

Est. runtime: Continuous | Recommended device: Desktop | Approx. size: 25 KB

Overview

A simple, dripping faucet is a surprisingly rich physical system that can exhibit complex, chaotic behavior. This simulation explores the dynamics of such a system not in real space, but in phase space—an abstract space where each point represents the complete state of the system at one instant. As the system evolves, it traces a path through this space. By adjusting a single parameter analogous to the faucet's flow rate, you can see the system's behavior change from simple and predictable (a single point or a closed loop) to chaotic. In the chaotic regime, the trajectory settles onto a beautiful, intricate, fractal object known as a strange attractor.

Beginner's Guide to Strange Attractors

Step 1: Phase Space - A Map of States

Instead of watching the faucet drip, physicists study it by plotting its state on a "phase space" diagram. For a dripping faucet, the state could be defined by the time between successive drops. For example, a point in our 3D space could be `(Time_between_drop_1_and_2, Time_between_drop_2_and_3, Time_between_drop_3_and_4)`. As the faucet continues to drip, this point moves, tracing a trajectory.

This simulation uses a well-known mathematical system (the Ikeda map) that has the same qualitative behavior as the dripping faucet.

Step 2: From Order to Chaos

The behavior of the system depends entirely on the "Flow Rate" parameter, which you can control with the slider.

  • Low Flow Rate (Periodic): At low flow rates, the faucet drips with a perfectly regular rhythm. The time between drops is always the same. In phase space, the system stays at a single point. If it alternates between two states (a "tick-tock" rhythm), the trajectory becomes a simple closed loop.
  • High Flow Rate (Chaotic): As the flow rate increases, the dripping becomes erratic and unpredictable. It never exactly repeats, but it's not completely random either. The system is chaotic.

Step 3: The Strange Attractor

In the chaotic regime, the trajectory in phase space is confined to a specific region, which it traces over and over again. This region is the strange attractor. It has several key properties:

  • Attractor: Nearby trajectories are "attracted" towards it.
  • Strange: It has a fractal structure. If you were to zoom in on any part of it, you would see smaller copies of the same overall folded and stretched structure.

As the simulation runs, you can see the trajectory building up this beautiful, complex shape. You can rotate the view by dragging the canvas to appreciate its 3D structure.

Controls

Drag the slider to change the system's parameter (analogous to flow rate). Drag the canvas to rotate the view.
