Interactive Diagram

Overview

A Penrose diagram is a clever map of spacetime that allows physicists to visualize the complex geometry around objects like black holes. It "squishes" an infinite universe into a finite, diamond-shaped picture, making it easier to understand causality—what parts of the universe can affect other parts. In this diagram, the golden rule is that all light rays travel at 45-degree angles. By drawing paths for spaceships (which must travel slower than light), you can explore the inevitable fate of anything that crosses a black hole's event horizon.

Beginner's Guide

1. A Map of an Infinite Journey

Imagine trying to draw a map that shows every single place on Earth. It's hard, but possible. Now imagine trying to draw a map that shows every place in the universe, not just in space, but also in time—from the beginning of time to the very end. This is the challenge of mapping spacetime. It's infinitely large and lasts for an infinitely long time.

A Penrose diagram is the solution. It's a type of "conformal transformation," a fancy mathematical technique for taking an infinite spacetime and squeezing it into a finite shape, like the diamond you see here. The shapes of small things are preserved, but vast distances are compressed. This allows us to see the entire history and structure of a universe containing a black hole at a single glance.

2. The Golden Rule: 45 Degrees is Law

On a normal map, the speed limit changes everywhere. On a Penrose diagram, the speed limit is the same everywhere and for everyone: the speed of light. To make this easy to see, the diagram is drawn so that light rays always travel at perfect 45-degree angles.

  • A vertical line represents something standing still in space.
  • A horizontal line represents a single instant in time across all of space.
  • Anything that moves through space, like a spaceship, must travel on a path that is more vertical than it is horizontal (a slope steeper than 45 degrees). This is because nothing can travel faster than light.
Try it yourself! Select "Draw Spaceship" and try to draw a path that is more horizontal than vertical. The line won't follow your cursor, because you are trying to break the laws of physics.

3. The Four Regions of Spacetime

This diagram (for a simple, non-rotating black hole) is divided into four regions:

  1. Our Universe (Bottom Quadrant): This is where we are. You can only start drawing your path here. From here, you can travel to other places in our universe, or you can travel towards the black hole.
  2. The Black Hole (Top Quadrant): If you cross the boundary—the Event Horizon—you enter the black hole region. Notice the top edge of this region is a jagged red line. That is the Future Singularity. It's not a place in space, but a moment in time. Once you are in the black hole, every possible future path you can take ends at the singularity. It is as inevitable as "next Monday" is for us.
  3. The White Hole (Left Quadrant): This is the time-reversed version of a black hole. Nothing can enter it; things can only come out. The jagged red line at its bottom is the Past Singularity, a moment in the past from which everything in this region emerged. It's a mathematical curiosity that most physicists believe doesn't exist in our real universe.
  4. Other Universe (Right Quadrant): The full mathematics of a simple black hole suggest it could be a bridge (an "Einstein-Rosen bridge" or wormhole) to another, separate universe. Like the white hole, this is a mathematical feature that is not expected to be physically real, as it would be incredibly unstable.

Core Theory

Causal Structure of Spacetime

The most important feature of a Penrose diagram is that it makes the causal structure of spacetime obvious. Causality is the principle that an effect cannot happen before its cause. On the diagram, this is built into the geometry.

The Future Light Cone

From any point in the diagram, the set of all points that can be reached is called the "future light cone." On this diagram, the light cone is always a 90-degree wedge pointing upwards. Any path you draw must stay within the confines of this cone.

  • Timelike paths (spaceships): Stay inside the 45-degree boundaries of the light cone.
  • Lightlike paths (light rays): Travel exactly on the 45-degree boundaries of the light cone.
  • Spacelike paths: Paths outside the light cone (more horizontal than vertical). These are forbidden, as they would require traveling faster than light.

Inside the event horizon, the entire light cone, including all possible future paths, is pointed towards the singularity. This is the geometric representation of "no escape."

Parameters & Controls

This simulation has two main drawing modes that determine the kind of path you can trace on the diagram.

ModeWhat it RepresentsPhysical Rule
Draw Spaceship The worldline of a massive object, like a person, planet, or spaceship. The path must be "timelike," meaning it travels slower than light. On the diagram, this means its slope must always be less than 45° from the vertical.
Draw Light Ray The worldline of a massless particle, like a photon of light. The path must be "lightlike" (or null), meaning it travels at exactly the speed of light. On the diagram, this means its slope is exactly 45°.

Worked Walkthroughs

Journey into the Black Hole

  1. Select the "Draw Spaceship" mode.
  2. Click near the bottom of the "Our Universe" quadrant to start your journey.
  3. Drag your cursor upwards and to the right, crossing the dashed line that marks the Event Horizon. You are now inside the black hole.
  4. Now, try to move your cursor back out. Drag it to the left, away from the singularity. Notice how the drawn path continues to move upwards, even if you move your mouse downwards? This is because time always moves forward (up the diagram).
  5. No matter where you move your cursor inside the top quadrant, your path will inevitably end on the jagged red line at the top. You have reached the singularity.

Sending a Signal to a Friend

  1. Click "Clear Path".
  2. Select "Draw Spaceship" and draw a path for an "Observer" who stays in the right-hand side of the "Our Universe" quadrant. This is Alice.
  3. Now, select "Draw Light Ray". Start a path somewhere to the left of Alice's path, but also in "Our Universe". This is Bob, sending a light signal.
  4. Draw the light ray's path at a 45-degree angle upwards and to the right, so that it intersects Alice's path. This shows that Bob can successfully send a message to Alice.
  5. What if Alice is inside the black hole? Clear the paths, draw Alice's path so it crosses the event horizon. Now try to draw a light ray from Bob (outside) to Alice (inside). It works!
  6. Now try the reverse. Can Alice, once inside the black hole, send a light signal back out to Bob? Try drawing a 45-degree light ray path starting from inside the black hole and aiming back out. It's impossible.

Advanced Notes & Extensions

What about Real Black Holes?

The diagram shown here is for a Schwarzschild black hole, which is the simplest kind: it is eternal, non-rotating, and uncharged. Real-world black holes are formed from collapsing stars, and they rotate.

  • Collapsing Star: A Penrose diagram for a black hole formed from a collapsing star would not have the "White Hole" or "Other Universe" regions. The past singularity would be replaced by the diagram of the star itself.
  • Rotating Black Holes (Kerr): A rotating black hole has a much more complicated Penrose diagram. It has two event horizons and the singularity is not a line, but a ring. In theory, it might be possible to travel through the ring without being destroyed, entering other regions of spacetime. This is an active area of theoretical research.

Are Wormholes Real?

The "Other Universe" region is connected to ours by an Einstein-Rosen Bridge, more popularly known as a wormhole. While it is a valid mathematical solution, physicists have shown that such a wormhole would be incredibly unstable. It would collapse shut the instant even a single photon tried to travel through it, and it would require exotic matter with "negative energy" to hold it open.

Analogies & Intuition

1. The Mercator Map of Spacetime (Map Analogy)

A Penrose diagram is to spacetime what a Mercator map is to the Earth. A Mercator map is famous for distorting the size of landmasses (making Greenland look as big as Africa) but has a very useful property: any straight line drawn on the map is a line of constant compass bearing. This made it invaluable for navigation.

  • Analogy: A Penrose diagram distorts infinite distances but has a useful property: light rays are always straight 45-degree lines. This makes "causal navigation" easy.
  • Limitation: Like the Mercator map, it gives a false impression of distances. The singularity is not "close" in space, but it is "soon" in time.

2. The Compressed Movie Timeline (Visualization Analogy)

Imagine the entire history of the universe is a movie that is trillions of hours long. You want to see the overall plot. You could use a video editor to compress the entire movie into a one-minute clip. You can now see the beginning, middle, and end all at once, but you've lost all sense of the real duration of events.

  • Analogy: A Penrose diagram compresses the infinite timeline of the universe into a single, viewable graphic.
  • Limitation: This analogy captures the time compression well but misses the spatial compression and the geometric rules about light cones.

3. A Change of Coordinates (Computational Analogy)

In computer graphics or data analysis, it's often useful to change your coordinate system. For example, switching from Cartesian coordinates (x, y) to polar coordinates (radius, angle) can make a problem involving circles much simpler. A Penrose diagram is the result of a very clever change of coordinates for spacetime.

  • Analogy: The original coordinates (time `t`, and radial distance `r`) are messy near a black hole. The new coordinates (`u`, `v`, which are combinations of `t` and `r`) result in a much cleaner and more well-behaved picture where light travels in simple straight lines.
  • Limitation: This is the most technically accurate analogy but also the least intuitive if you're not familiar with coordinate transformations.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Why is it diamond-shaped?
The diamond shape is the result of the mathematical "squishing" process. It's a square rotated by 45 degrees, which is the most convenient shape for ensuring that light rays travel at 45-degree angles on the page.
2. If I can't escape, can I at least see out of the black hole?
Yes! Light from the outside universe can still enter the black hole and reach you. Try drawing a light ray starting in "Our Universe" and ending on a spaceship path inside the event horizon. You would be able to see the stars and the rest of the universe, but they would appear increasingly distorted and blueshifted as you approach the singularity.
3. What *is* the singularity?
It is a moment in time, not a place in space. It is the end of spacetime as we know it. According to General Relativity, at the singularity, the curvature of spacetime becomes infinite, and the density of matter is infinite. Our current laws of physics break down there.
4. What is the event horizon?
The event horizon is not a physical barrier. It is a one-way door in spacetime. It is the precise boundary beyond which the gravitational pull is so strong that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light. Once you cross it, your future inevitably lies within the black hole.
5. Why can't I draw a path that is very horizontal?
A horizontal line represents all of space at a single instant in time. To travel along a horizontal line would mean being in multiple places at once. To travel on a path that is more horizontal than vertical would mean you are traveling faster than light, which is forbidden by the laws of physics.
6. Can I start my path in the "Other Universe"?
No. This simulation restricts you to starting in our universe. The "Other Universe" is causally disconnected from ours, meaning you can't travel there through normal space. While the diagram shows a "bridge," it's not a traversable one.
