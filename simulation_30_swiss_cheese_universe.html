The "Swiss Cheese" Universe Model

A simple model for studying our lumpy, inhomogeneous cosmos.

Plain-Language Overview

The standard model of cosmology assumes the universe is perfectly smooth and uniform. While this is a good approximation on the largest scales, we know that up close, the universe is lumpy, with matter gathered into galaxies and vast empty voids between them. The "Swiss Cheese" model is a classic tool used by cosmologists to study the effects of this lumpiness. It imagines the universe as a block of cheese (the smooth, uniform part) with holes carved out of it (the empty voids where matter has collapsed). This simulation lets you explore such a universe, showing how light rays travel through the lumpy cosmos and how their paths are bent by the cheese and holes.

Beginner's Guide — A Gentle Introduction

Step 1: The Smooth Universe Assumption

To make the math manageable, the standard model of the Big Bang—called the FLRW model—makes a powerful simplifying assumption: that the universe is perfectly "homogeneous" (the same everywhere) and "isotropic" (the same in every direction). This smooth model has been incredibly successful at describing the average properties and expansion of the universe on the largest possible scales.

Step 2: The Lumpy Reality

But we know this isn't the whole truth. When we look at the sky, we don't see a uniform fog. We see points of light (stars) gathered into galaxies, which are gathered into clusters, which are arranged in long filaments surrounding vast, nearly empty regions called voids. The universe is smooth on average, but lumpy up close. A key question in cosmology is: do these lumps and voids affect our measurements of the universe, like its expansion rate?

Step 3: The Swiss Cheese Model

This is where the Swiss Cheese model comes in. It's a simple, elegant way to put lumps into the smooth model. Here's how it works:

  1. Start with the cheese: Begin with a block of cheese representing the perfectly smooth, expanding FLRW universe.
  2. Carve out the holes: Cut out spherical regions from the cheese. These represent areas where the matter has pulled together under gravity, leaving behind an empty void.
  3. Fill the holes (or not): In the simplest model, the holes are completely empty (a vacuum). In more complex versions, you can place a condensed mass like a galaxy or a black hole at the center of each hole. The spacetime geometry inside the hole is different from the geometry of the cheese.
By doing this, you create an exact solution to Einstein's equations that is lumpy, not smooth. This allows cosmologists to study how this lumpiness affects things that travel through it, like light.

Step 4: Bending Light Rays

In a perfectly smooth universe, light travels in a "straight line" (called a geodesic). But in a Swiss Cheese universe, its path is more complicated. As a ray of light passes the edge of a hole, its path is bent by the gravity of the surrounding cheese. This effect, called gravitational lensing, is what this simulation demonstrates. You can fire photons through the model to see how the voids deflect their paths, giving us a different view of the cosmos than we'd get from a perfectly uniform model.

The FLRW and Schwarzschild Metrics

The Swiss Cheese model works by stitching two different solutions of Einstein's equations together.

  • The Cheese (Exterior): The spacetime of the uniform, expanding universe is described by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric. This metric has a "scale factor," `a(t)`, that grows with time, representing the overall cosmic expansion.
  • The Hole (Interior): The spacetime inside the empty voids is often described by the Schwarzschild metric. This is the same metric that describes the spacetime around a non-rotating, uncharged black hole. It is a static, vacuum solution.

Junction Conditions

You can't just arbitrarily cut and paste spacetimes together. The genius of the model is that it's an exact solution, meaning the geometry is perfectly smooth at the boundary where the hole meets the cheese. This is achieved by carefully enforcing the "Israel-Darmois junction conditions." In essence, this means that the mass removed from the FLRW "cheese" to create the void must be exactly equal to the mass of the central object placed within the Schwarzschild void (often, this is zero for a pure vacuum hole). This ensures there are no nasty physical discontinuities at the boundary.

Effect on Light: Gravitational Lensing

The simulation shows a simplified version of gravitational lensing. According to General Relativity, the path of light is bent by the curvature of spacetime, which is caused by mass-energy. In our model, the "cheese" has a uniform density, while the "holes" are empty. This density difference at the edge of the holes acts like a lens. A light ray passing near the edge of a hole is deflected. The amount of deflection in this simulation is exaggerated for visual effect, but it captures the essence of how inhomogeneities can bend light paths.

Backreaction and the Hubble Constant

A major motivation for studying models like this is the "backreaction" problem. Does the average expansion of a lumpy universe behave in the same way as the expansion of a perfectly smooth universe with the same average density? The answer is not necessarily yes. The non-linear nature of Einstein's equations means that the average of the evolution is not the same as the evolution of the average. The Swiss Cheese model allows physicists to study this effect. Some have proposed that backreaction could help explain the "Hubble Tension"—the fact that measurements of the universe's expansion rate today (the Hubble constant) seem to disagree depending on whether they are measured from the early universe (the CMB) or the late universe (supernovae).

Limitations of the Model

The Swiss Cheese model is a "toy model" and has significant limitations. The real universe is not made of perfectly spherical voids. The large-scale structure is more like a "cosmic web" of filaments, clusters, and sheet-like walls surrounding voids. More sophisticated (and much more complex) models like Lemaître-Tolman-Bondi (LTB) models, which allow for non-uniform density profiles, or full N-body numerical simulations are needed to capture this greater complexity.
