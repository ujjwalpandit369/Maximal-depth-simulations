Specific Heat Capacity

An interactive comparison of how different materials heat up.

Overview

Why does a metal spoon in hot soup heat up almost instantly, while the soup itself takes much longer to cool down? Why is the beach sand scorching hot on a sunny day, while the water is still cool? The answer is Specific Heat Capacity (c). It is an intrinsic property of a substance that describes how much heat energy is required to raise its temperature. This simulation allows you to heat up two blocks of identical mass but different materials, demonstrating visually how a low specific heat capacity leads to a rapid temperature change, while a high specific heat capacity means the temperature changes much more slowly.

The Formula for Heating

The relationship between heat, mass, specific heat, and temperature change is given by:

Q = m * c * ΔT

Rearranging this to solve for the temperature change (ΔT), we get:

ΔT = Q / (m * c)
  • ΔT is the change in temperature (what we observe).
  • Q is the heat energy added (determined by the Heater Power).
  • m is the mass of the substance (assumed to be 1 kg for both blocks in this simulation).
  • c is the specific heat capacity of the material.

This second formula is the key. Since Q and m are the same for both blocks, it shows that the temperature change (ΔT) is inversely proportional to the specific heat (c). A big 'c' means a small 'ΔT', and a small 'c' means a big 'ΔT'.

Specific Heat of Common Materials (J/kg·K)

  • Water: 4186 (Extremely High)
  • Aluminum: 900 (High for a metal)
  • Iron: 450 (Typical for a metal)
  • Gold: 129 (Extremely Low)

Try comparing Gold and Water in the simulation. For the same amount of heat from the heater, the gold's temperature skyrockets, while the water's temperature barely budges. This is why water is used as a coolant in engines and power plants—it can absorb a huge amount of heat without its temperature increasing too much.
