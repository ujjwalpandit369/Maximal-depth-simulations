Relative Velocity: River Crossing

A classic physics problem demonstrating vector addition.

Overview

If a boat points straight across a river, it won't land directly on the other side; the current will carry it downstream. To travel straight across, the boat must point partially upstream to counteract the river's flow. This is a classic problem of relative velocity, where the boat's final path is the vector sum of its own velocity relative to the water and the water's velocity relative to the ground. This simulation lets you explore this concept by setting the boat's speed, heading, and the river's current, then visualizing the resulting path and the underlying vectors.

Vector Addition in Action

The boat's motion is governed by a simple vector equation:

v_ground = v_boat + v_current
  • v_boat (blue vector) is the velocity of the boat with respect to the water. Its direction is the "heading" you set with the slider.
  • v_current (gray vector) is the velocity of the water with respect to the ground. It always points downstream.
  • v_ground (red vector) is the velocity of the boat with respect to the ground. This is the vector sum of the other two, and it represents the boat's actual path.

Micro-Experiments

  1. Go Straight Across: Set the boat speed to 5 m/s and the river speed to 3 m/s. What heading angle (θ) do you need to set so the boat travels straight across the river with zero downstream drift? (Hint: The upstream component of the boat's velocity must cancel out the river's velocity).
  2. Fastest Crossing: At what heading angle does the boat cross the river in the shortest amount of time? Does the river's speed affect this? (Hint: The time to cross depends only on the component of the boat's velocity that is perpendicular to the banks).
  3. Upstream Battle: Set the boat speed to 4 m/s and the river speed to 5 m/s. Is it possible to travel upstream (i.e., land at a point to the left of where you started)? Why or why not?
