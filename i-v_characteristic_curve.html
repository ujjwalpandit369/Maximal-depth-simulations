One-Paragraph Overview

Every electronic component has a unique "fingerprint" that describes how it behaves when electricity passes through it. This fingerprint is its Current-Voltage (I-V) characteristic curve. For some components, like a simple resistor, this relationship is a straight line: double the electrical "pressure" (voltage), and you get double the "flow" (current). For others, like a diode, the relationship is highly non-linear, acting like a one-way street for current. This simulation lets you sweep the voltage across these components and watch their I-V curves get traced out, revealing the fundamental physics that governs every electronic circuit.

Beginner’s Guide — The Rules of Electrical Flow

The Water Analogy: Pressure, Flow, and Constriction

Let's imagine electricity as water flowing through pipes.

  • Voltage (V): This is like the water pressure. Higher voltage means more "push" behind the electricity. Measured in Volts (V).
  • Current (I): This is the rate of water flow—how much water passes a point per second. Higher current means more charge is flowing. Measured in Amperes (A), or often milliamps (mA, 1/1000th of an Amp).
  • Resistance (R): This is like a narrow section or a clog in the pipe that restricts the flow. Higher resistance means it's harder for current to pass through. Measured in Ohms (Ω).

Component 1: The Ohmic Resistor (A Simple Pipe)

An Ohmic resistor is the most basic component. It's like a straight, simple pipe. Its resistance is constant. If you double the water pressure (voltage), the flow rate (current) exactly doubles. This simple, linear relationship is called Ohm's Law: V = I × R.

On the I-V graph, this creates a perfect straight line that passes through the origin (0V, 0A). The slope of this line is related to the resistance. A steeper line means lower resistance (more current for the same voltage), and a shallower line means higher resistance.

Component 2: The Diode (A One-Way Valve)

A diode is a semiconductor device that acts like a one-way street for current. It's like a check valve or a turnstile in our water pipe analogy.

  • Forward Bias (Positive Voltage): When you apply a positive voltage in the "correct" direction, the valve opens up. After a small "turn-on" voltage (around 0.7V for silicon diodes), the resistance drops to almost zero, and a large current can flow easily. This is the vertical part of the "hockey stick" curve.
  • Reverse Bias (Negative Voltage): When you try to push current through the wrong way with a negative voltage, the valve slams shut. Almost no current can flow (just a tiny, negligible leakage current). This is the flat, horizontal part of the curve along the axis.
This non-linear behavior is crucial for everything from converting AC to DC power to protecting circuits from being connected backwards.

Static vs. Dynamic Resistance

In the readout, you'll see two types of resistance.

  • Static Resistance (R = V/I): This is the simple resistance calculated by Ohm's law at a single point. For a resistor, it's constant. For a diode, it changes dramatically depending on the voltage.
  • Dynamic Resistance (r = dV/dI): This is the resistance to a *small change* in voltage. It's the slope of the I-V curve at a specific point. For a resistor, it's the same as the static resistance. For a diode in its "on" state, the dynamic resistance is very small, because a tiny change in voltage causes a huge change in current. This is a more advanced concept used in designing circuits with AC signals.

Core Theory & Equations

1. Ohm's Law (Resistors)

For an ideal Ohmic resistor, the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance is linear and constant.

I = V / R

Plain English: The current (I) flowing through a resistor is directly proportional to the voltage (V) across it and inversely proportional to its resistance (R).
Numeric Example: If you apply a voltage `V = 2 V` across a resistor with `R = 100 Ω`, the current will be `I = 2 V / 100 Ω = 0.02 A` or `20 mA`.

2. Shockley Diode Equation

The I-V relationship for a diode is exponential, described by the Shockley diode equation.

I = I_s * (e^(V / (n * V_t)) - 1)

Plain English: The current (I) through a diode depends on the applied voltage (V) in an exponential way.

  • `I_s`: The reverse saturation current, a very small constant (e.g., 10⁻⁹ A). It's the tiny leakage current that flows when the diode is reverse-biased.
  • `V_t`: The thermal voltage, a constant related to temperature (`kT/q`). At room temperature, it's about `25.85 mV`.
  • `n`: The ideality factor, a constant usually between 1 and 2.
When `V` is positive, the `e^V` term dominates and the current grows exponentially. When `V` is negative, the `e^V` term becomes nearly zero, and `I` is approximately `-I_s` (a very small negative current).
Numeric Example: For `V = 0.7 V` (and typical parameters), `V / (n*V_t)` is large, so `e^(V / (n*V_t))` is huge, leading to a large forward current. For `V = -2 V`, `e^(V / (n*V_t))` is practically zero, so `I ≈ I_s * (0 - 1) = -I_s`, a tiny leakage current.
