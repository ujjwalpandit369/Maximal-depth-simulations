Relativistic Doppler Effect

A simulation comparing the classical and relativistic Doppler effect for light.

Plain-Language Overview

The Doppler effect for sound is familiar: an approaching ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched. The same happens for light, but with a twist from Special Relativity. The relativistic Doppler effect includes an extra factor due to time dilation, leading to a unique prediction: a "transverse" Doppler effect. Even when a light source moves purely sideways, there is still a redshift observed. This simulation lets you compare the classical and relativistic predictions and see this strange effect for yourself.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Setup

The visualization shows a stationary Observer (the eye) and a Light Source moving with a velocity indicated by the arrow. You control the speed and direction.

2. The Big Difference: Classical vs. Relativistic

The results box shows the frequency an observer would measure, assuming the source emits at a frequency of 1. Notice how the classical and relativistic predictions differ at high speeds.

3. The Smoking Gun: Transverse Doppler Effect

Set the Angle of Motion to exactly 90°. Classically, there should be no frequency shift. But relativity predicts a redshift! This is a direct consequence of time dilation—the moving source's "clock" ticks slower, so it emits wave crests less frequently. This effect has been experimentally verified.

Core Theory

The classical Doppler effect for light is f' = f₀ / (1 - (v/c)cosθ). The relativistic formula adds the time dilation factor: f' = f₀ * √(1 - v²/c²) / (1 - (v/c)cosθ). At θ=90°, the classical formula gives `f'=f₀`, while the relativistic formula gives `f' = f₀ * √(1 - v²/c²)`, correctly predicting a redshift.
