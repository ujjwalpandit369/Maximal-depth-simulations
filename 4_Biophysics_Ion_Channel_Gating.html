Biophysics: Voltage-Gated Ion Channel

A simulation of the molecular gates that power your nervous system.

Plain-Language Overview

Every thought you have and every move you make is controlled by electrical signals firing through your neurons. These signals are made possible by tiny, sophisticated molecular machines called **ion channels**. These channels act as gates embedded in the cell's membrane, opening and closing to allow specific ions (charged atoms) to rush in or out. Many of these channels are "voltage-gated," meaning they open and close in response to changes in the electrical voltage across the cell membrane. This simulation provides a simplified view of how one of these gates works, showing how a change in voltage causes the channel protein to change its shape, opening a pore and allowing ions to flow, which is the fundamental event that creates a nerve impulse.

Beginner's Guide: The Neuron's Electrical Gates

Step 1: The Cell Membrane

Every living cell is enclosed in a membrane, which acts as a barrier between the inside and the outside of the cell. This membrane maintains a small electrical voltage, called the **membrane potential**. In a resting neuron, this is typically around -70 millivolts (mV).

Step 2: Ion Channels - The Gates in the Wall

The membrane is studded with proteins that act as channels or pores. The one shown in the simulation is a **voltage-gated ion channel**. It's a highly specialized gate that is usually closed, blocking the flow of ions (like the blue sodium ions). You can control the membrane voltage with the slider.

Step 3: The Voltage Sensor

How does the gate "know" what the voltage is? The channel protein has parts that are electrically charged, called **voltage sensors** (represented by the small paddles with '+' signs).

  • At rest (-70mV), the inside of the cell is negative, which pulls these positive sensors *down* and holds the gate *closed*.
  • When the membrane voltage becomes more positive (an event called **depolarization**), the electrical force changes. The positive inside now repels the positive sensors, pushing them *up*.
This upward movement of the sensors triggers a conformational change in the rest of the protein, causing the central gate to snap open.

Step 4: The Flow of Ions

Once the gate is open, ions are free to move through the pore. They will flow down their electrochemical gradient—in this case, from the high concentration outside the cell to the low concentration inside. This sudden influx of positive ions is what creates the "spike" of an action potential, or nerve impulse.

Step 5: The Conductance Plot

The graph on the right shows the channel's **conductance**, which is its ability to let ions flow. It's essentially a plot of the probability that the channel is open. Notice that it's not a simple on/off switch; it's an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve. At very negative voltages, the probability of opening is near zero. As the voltage crosses a certain threshold, the probability rapidly increases until, at positive voltages, the channel is almost certain to be open. The point on the curve shows the probability for the current voltage you have set.
