Joule Expansion (Free Expansion)

What happens to temperature when a gas expands into nothing?

Estimated Runtime: Interactive | Recommended Device: Desktop | Approx. HTML Size: 140 KB

Plain-Language Overview

Imagine a container split into two parts: one filled with a gas, the other a perfect vacuum. What happens if you suddenly remove the divider? The gas rushes to fill the empty space. This is called Joule expansion or free expansion. The surprising and counter-intuitive result, which this simulation demonstrates, is that for an "ideal" gas, the temperature does not change at all. This experiment is a cornerstone of thermodynamics, revealing a deep connection between temperature, energy, work, and the nature of gases themselves.

Beginner's Guide — The First Law in Action

Step 1: The Setup

Our experiment takes place in a perfectly insulated box. This is important because it means no heat can get in or out from the surroundings. The box is divided by a partition. On the left, we have a gas, which is a collection of particles whizzing around and bouncing off each other and the walls. On the right, we have nothing—a complete vacuum.

Step 2: The Expansion

The simulation starts when we instantly remove the partition. The gas particles, no longer confined to the left side, are now free to move into the right side. They quickly spread out until they are, on average, evenly distributed throughout the entire box.

Step 3: The Big Question - Does the Temperature Change?

Our everyday intuition might suggest the gas should cool down. When you use a can of compressed air, the can gets cold. This is because the expanding air does work on the outside atmosphere to push it away. But in our experiment, the situation is different. To figure out what happens, we turn to the First Law of Thermodynamics.

Step 4: The First Law of Thermodynamics (`ΔU = Q - W`)

This law is a statement of energy conservation. It says that the change in a system's internal energy (`ΔU`) is equal to the heat added to the system (`Q`) minus the work done *by* the system (`W`). Let's look at each term for our expanding gas:

  • Heat (Q): The box is insulated. No heat can get in or out. So, Q = 0.
  • Work (W): A system does work when it expands against an opposing force or pressure. Our gas is expanding into a vacuum, where the pressure is zero. It doesn't have to push anything out of the way. Therefore, the gas does no work. So, W = 0.
  • Internal Energy (ΔU): Since `ΔU = 0 - 0`, the change in the internal energy of the gas is zero. ΔU = 0.

For an ideal gas, the internal energy is just a measure of the total kinetic energy of its particles, which is directly proportional to its temperature. If the internal energy doesn't change, the temperature doesn't change either! The particles just have more room to move around in.

Step 5: What about Entropy?

Even though the temperature and energy are constant, something has clearly changed in a fundamental way. The gas is now more disordered, and it will never spontaneously go back into the left half of the box. This increase in disorder is measured by a quantity called entropy. The entropy of the gas sharply increases during free expansion, showing that the process is irreversible.

Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

An ideal gas is defined as one where the particles are point-like and have no interactions with each other (no attractive or repulsive forces). Therefore, the internal energy `U` of the gas is purely the sum of the kinetic energies of all its particles. This kinetic energy is directly proportional to the absolute temperature `T`.

U = (f/2) nRT

Where `f` is the degrees of freedom (3 for simple point particles), `n` is the number of moles, and `R` is the ideal gas constant. The key takeaway is `U ∝ T`.
Since the Joule expansion process involves `ΔU = 0`, it must be that `ΔT = 0` for an ideal gas.

Entropy Change

Entropy (`S`) is a measure of the disorder or, more formally, the number of microscopic arrangements (microstates) that correspond to a given macroscopic state. When the volume available to the gas doubles, the number of possible positions for each particle doubles. For N particles, the number of available microstates increases by a factor of `2^N`.

The change in entropy for an ideal gas expanding from volume `V₁` to `V₂` is given by:

ΔS = nR * ln(V₂ / V₁)

Since `V₂ > V₁`, the term `ln(V₂ / V₁)` is positive, meaning the entropy always increases (`ΔS > 0`). This is consistent with the Second Law of Thermodynamics, which states that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease. This is why the process is irreversible.

What about Real Gases?

The "no temperature change" result is only strictly true for a hypothetical ideal gas. Real gas molecules do have forces between them. At most distances, there are weak, attractive forces called "van der Waals forces."

When a real gas expands, the average distance between the molecules increases. To pull the molecules away from each other against these attractive forces, the gas must do "internal work." Where does the energy for this work come from? It comes from the kinetic energy of the particles themselves.

Since the kinetic energy of the particles decreases, the temperature of a real gas does drop slightly during a free expansion. This cooling effect is known as the Joule effect. In the simulation, you can toggle to "Real Gas" mode to see this: after the expansion, the particles will slow down, and the thermometer will show a small decrease in temperature.

Distinction from Joule-Thomson Expansion

It is important not to confuse free expansion with the Joule-Thomson (or throttling) process. In a throttling process, a gas is forced from a high-pressure region to a low-pressure region through a valve or porous plug. In this case, work is done on and by the gas as it moves, and the temperature change can be significant. This effect is used in liquefaction of gases and is the primary mechanism in the expansion valve step of the refrigeration cycle.
