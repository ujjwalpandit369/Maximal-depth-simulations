A Different View of Reality

What is reality made of? We usually think of it as points in spacetime. But what if the fundamental objects were not points, but light rays? This is the core idea of Twistor Theory, a radical and beautiful approach to physics developed by Sir Roger Penrose.

The Two Spaces

The simulation shows two linked panels:

  • Minkowski Spacetime: On the left is our familiar spacetime, simplified to one dimension of space (x) and one of time (t). The diagonal grid lines represent the paths that light can travel (we set the speed of light, c, to 1).
  • Twistor Space: On the right is a representation of the corresponding "twistor space." While technically a 3D complex space, we are looking at a 3D real "slice" of it.

The Correspondence

Twistor theory proposes a deep, magical correspondence between these two spaces. This simulation demonstrates the most famous part of that correspondence.

Click and drag in the Spacetime panel. Your drag action defines a yellow line. The simulation forces this line to have a slope of +1 or -1, meaning it represents a null line—the path a ray of light would take.

As you define this light ray, watch the Twistor Space panel. A single, unique yellow point appears. This is the correspondence:

Every light ray in spacetime is represented by a single point in twistor space.
Try moving the light ray around in the spacetime panel. Notice how the corresponding point moves in twistor space. The entire history of a photon, a path through spacetime, is encoded as a single, static point in this other world.

Why is this useful?

Penrose and others hoped that by reformulating physics in twistor space, some difficult problems (like quantum gravity) might become simpler. For example, interactions between particles that are complex processes in spacetime can sometimes be described by the geometry of intersecting lines and surfaces in twistor space. While it hasn't yet solved everything, twistor theory has led to powerful new mathematical tools and provides a profound new perspective on the nature of reality.
