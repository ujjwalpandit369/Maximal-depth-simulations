Welcome to the Spring Lab!

Have you ever stretched a rubber band or a spring? You might have noticed that the more you pull, the harder it pulls back. A scientist named Robert Hooke studied this and came up with a famous rule, now known as Hooke's Law. Let's discover it for ourselves!

Step 1: The Setup

On the left, you see a spring hanging from a support. On the right is a graph. This graph will plot the Force we apply to the spring versus how much it stretches (its extension).

Step 2: Change the Spring

The "Spring Constant (k)" slider in the controls changes how stiff the spring is. A high 'k' value means a very stiff spring (like from a car's suspension). A low 'k' value means a very loose, stretchy spring (like a Slinky).

Step 3: Hang a Mass

In the controls, click on one of the mass buttons, like "100 g". You'll see a 100-gram weight appear on the end of the spring, and the spring will stretch downwards. The force pulling the spring is the weight of the mass. Weight is calculated as `mass × gravity`.

Step 4: Take a Measurement

Now that the spring is stretched, click the orange "Add Data Point to Graph" button. A dot will appear on the graph. This dot's position represents the force from the 100g mass and the resulting extension of the spring.

Step 5: Collect More Data

This is how science works! One data point isn't enough. Now, click the "200 g" mass button. The spring will stretch more. Click "Add Data Point" again. A new dot appears on the graph. Repeat this for 300g, 400g, and 500g.

Step 6: The Big Reveal - The Straight Line

Look at the graph. The points you plotted should form a nearly perfect straight line! This is Hooke's Law in action: the force and the extension are directly proportional. Double the force, and you double the extension.

The simulation automatically draws a "best-fit line" through your points. The slope (the steepness) of this line is incredibly important. It tells you the spring constant 'k'. A steeper slope means a stiffer spring. Look at the "Calculated Spring Constant" in the results box. It should be very close to the "true" value you set with the slider! You've just experimentally measured a physical property of the spring.

Hooke's Law

Hooke's Law describes the restoring force (Fₛ) exerted by a spring when it is displaced from its equilibrium position by a distance (x).

Equation:

Fₛ = -k · x

Plain English: The force the spring uses to pull back is proportional to how far you've stretched it. The negative sign indicates the force is in the opposite direction of the displacement (it tries to return to its original position).

In our experiment: We apply a downward force (the weight of the mass, Fₚ) which, at equilibrium, is equal and opposite to the spring's restoring force. So, `Fₚ = -Fₛ = kx`.

Weight as a Force

The force applied to the spring is the weight of the mass due to gravity.

Equation:

Fₚ = W = m · g

Where `m` is mass in kilograms and `g` is the acceleration due to gravity (approximated as 9.8 m/s²).

Numeric Example: For a 200g mass:

m = 200 g = 0.2 kg
Fₚ = 0.2 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 1.96 Newtons (N)

Slope of the F-vs-x Graph

Since `F = kx`, this is in the form of a linear equation `y = mx + b`, where `y = F`, `m = k`, `x = x`, and the y-intercept `b` is 0. Therefore, the slope of the Force vs. Extension graph is the spring constant `k`.

Equation:

k = ΔF / Δx

Plain English: The spring constant is the "change in force" divided by the "change in extension." It's a measure of stiffness in Newtons per meter (N/m).
