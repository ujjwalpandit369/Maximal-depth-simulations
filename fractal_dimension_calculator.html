More than 1D, Less than 2D?

We're used to objects having integer dimensions: a line is 1D, a square is 2D, a cube is 3D. But what about fractals? A fractal like the Sierpinski triangle seems to be more than a line, but it's full of holes, so it's less than a full 2D square. A fractal dimension is a way to measure this "in-between" dimension, quantifying a fractal's complexity and how much space it fills.

Step 1: The Box-Counting Method

This simulation uses the box-counting method, a straightforward way to estimate fractal dimension. The idea is simple:

  1. Cover the fractal shape with a grid of boxes of a certain size, `ε`.
  2. Count how many boxes, `N(ε)`, actually contain a piece of the fractal.
  3. Repeat this process with smaller and smaller boxes.

Step 2: Run the Analysis

First, choose a shape to analyze. The "Line" (1D) and "Filled Square" (2D) are included as simple controls. The "Sierpinski Triangle" is our main fractal example.

Click the "Start Box-Counting" button. The simulation will perform the box-counting method for several different grid sizes.

Step 3: The Log-Log Plot

The right panel shows the results on a log-log plot. The horizontal axis is `log(1/ε)` (related to how small the boxes are) and the vertical axis is `log(N(ε))` (the number of boxes counted).

For a fractal, these points will form a nearly straight line. The slope of this line is the estimated fractal dimension!

The simulation automatically fits a line to these points and displays the calculated slope in the "Estimated Fractal Dimension" box.

  • For the Line, the dimension will be very close to 1.0.
  • For the Filled Square, the dimension will be very close to 2.0.
  • For the Sierpinski Triangle, you should get a dimension of approximately 1.585, a true fractional dimension!
Use the "Grid Size" slider to see the different grids overlaid on the fractal in the left panel, and the corresponding data point highlighted in the plot on the right.
