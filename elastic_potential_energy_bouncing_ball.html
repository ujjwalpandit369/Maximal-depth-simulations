Elastic Potential Energy: The Bouncing Ball

A simulation of a bouncing ball, focusing on energy transformations.

Est. runtime: Continuous | Recommended device: Desktop/Mobile | Approx. size: 25 KB

Overview

Ever wonder where the "bounce" in a bouncing ball comes from? This simulation reveals the physics behind it by tracking the ball's energy. As the ball falls, its energy of position—Gravitational Potential Energy—is converted into energy of motion, or Kinetic Energy. Upon impact, the ball deforms, and this energy is momentarily stored as Elastic Potential Energy, like in a compressed spring. The ball then springs back, converting this stored energy back into kinetic energy to launch itself upwards. By adjusting the ball's "bounciness," you can see how energy is lost in this process, explaining why a real ball never bounces back to its original height.

Beginner's Guide to Energy in a Bounce

Let's break down the journey of a bouncing ball, one form of energy at a time. The key idea is that energy isn't created or destroyed, just transformed from one type to another.

Step 1: The Three Main Energies

Our simulation tracks three key types of energy, which are visualized in the pie chart:

  • Gravitational Potential Energy (GPE): This is energy stored due to the ball's height. The higher the ball, the more GPE it has. Think of it as the "potential" to fall.
  • Kinetic Energy (KE): This is the energy of motion. The faster the ball moves, the more KE it has. It's zero at the very peak of its bounce when it momentarily stops.
  • Elastic Potential Energy (EPE): This is energy stored in a deformed object, like a stretched rubber band or a compressed spring. For our ball, this energy appears only during the instant it's squashed against the ground.

There is also a fourth category: Lost Energy. In a real bounce, some energy is converted into heat and sound, which is why the ball doesn't bounce forever. This is shown as the gray part of the pie chart.

Step 2: The Energy Transformation Cycle

Watch the pie chart as the simulation runs. You'll see a repeating cycle:

  1. At the top (start): The ball is held still at its maximum height. It has no motion (zero KE) and is not compressed (zero EPE). The pie chart is 100% GPE.
  2. Falling: As the ball falls, its height decreases, so its GPE is converted into KE. It speeds up. You'll see the GPE slice shrink and the KE slice grow.
  3. Just before impact: The ball is at its lowest height (almost zero GPE) and moving at its fastest speed. The pie chart is nearly 100% KE.
  4. Maximum compression: The ball hits the ground and squashes. For a split second, it stops moving (zero KE). All its energy is stored in its deformed shape. The chart briefly flashes to 100% EPE. This is the "source" of the bounce!
  5. Rising: The ball springs back into shape, pushing off the ground. The stored EPE is converted back into KE, launching it upwards.
  6. Back to the top: As the ball rises, it slows down, converting its KE back into GPE. It reaches a new, slightly lower peak, and the cycle repeats.

Step 3: The "Bounciness" Slider (Coefficient of Restitution)

This slider controls how much energy is lost during the bounce. A value of 1.0 represents a "perfectly elastic" collision—no energy is lost, and the ball would bounce forever. A value of 0.0 represents a "perfectly inelastic" collision, where the ball hits the ground and doesn't bounce at all (like a lump of clay). A typical bouncy ball might have a value around 0.8.

Step 4: Using the Simulation

Simply use the sliders to set the initial Drop Height and the Bounciness, then press Play. Watch the ball and the pie chart to see the energy transformations in action. Use the Reset button to try new values.

Worked Example: Energy Loss

The Coefficient of Restitution (COR) relates the ball's speed just before and just after impact: v_after = v_before * COR.

Since Kinetic Energy is KE = 0.5 * m * v², the kinetic energy after the bounce is:

KE_after = 0.5 * m * (v_before * COR)² = (0.5 * m * v_before²) * COR² = KE_before * COR²

If a ball with 100 Joules of KE hits the ground with a COR of 0.8, the KE it has immediately after bouncing is:

KE_after = 100 * 0.8² = 100 * 0.64 = 64 Joules. 36 Joules of energy were lost to heat and sound.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Where does the "lost" energy go?
In the real world, it's converted mostly into heat (the ball and the ground get slightly warmer) and sound waves (you hear the "thump" of the bounce).
2. Why does the pie chart flash red (Elastic PE) so quickly?
The process of the ball compressing and decompressing happens incredibly fast, typically in just a few milliseconds. This simulation slows it down slightly so you can see it.
3. What is a "coefficient of restitution"?
It's a number between 0 and 1 that measures the "bounciness" of a collision. It's the ratio of the final to initial relative speed between two objects after they collide.
4. Can the bounciness ever be 1.0 in real life?
No. Every real-world collision loses some energy. A bounciness of 1.0 (a perfectly elastic collision) is a theoretical ideal, like a frictionless surface. Some things get close, like the collision between two steel ball bearings.
