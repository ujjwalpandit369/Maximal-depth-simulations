Stacked Ball Drop

A surprising demonstration of momentum and energy transfer.

Estimated Runtime: Interactive | Recommended Device: Desktop | Approx. HTML Size: 150 KB

Plain-Language Overview

What happens if you stack a small, light ball on top of a large, heavy ball and drop them together? The result is surprisingly dramatic. Instead of a normal bounce, the top ball is launched upwards with incredible speed, reaching a height many times greater than where it started. This simulation animates this classic physics demonstration, often called a "supernova toy," showing how the conservation of momentum and energy in a sequence of rapid collisions can lead to a massive transfer of energy to the smaller object.

Beginner's Guide — The Collision Sequence

Step 1: The Drop

We start with a large ball (like a basketball) and a small ball (like a tennis ball) held in the air, with the small ball resting on top. When we drop them, gravity pulls them both down equally. They fall together, accelerating to the same downward velocity just before impact.

Step 2: The First Collision (Big Ball vs. Ground)

The large ball hits the ground first. Assuming a perfectly elastic ("bouncy") collision, it rebounds almost instantly. Its velocity flips direction—it is now moving **upward** with the same speed it had when it was moving downward.

Step 3: The Second Collision (Big Ball vs. Small Ball)

This is the crucial moment, and it happens in a fraction of a second. The small ball is still moving **downward** when it collides with the large ball, which is now moving **upward**. From the small ball's perspective, it's like running into a very heavy object that is moving towards it at high speed.

Step 4: The Launch

In this collision, both momentum (`mass × velocity`) and kinetic energy (`½ × mass × velocity²`) are conserved. Because the large ball is so much more massive, it can transfer a huge amount of its momentum and energy to the much lighter small ball. The small ball rebounds with a velocity that is much greater than its initial impact speed. This high launch velocity sends it flying high into the air, far above its original drop height. In the ideal case where the large ball is infinitely massive compared to the small one, the small ball can rebound with up to three times its impact velocity, reaching nine times the original height!

Analogy: The Trampoline Boost

Imagine you are on a trampoline with a very large, heavy friend. If you both jump at the same time, you'll get a normal bounce. But if you time it just right, so that you are coming down just as your heavy friend is on their way up from a big bounce, the result is very different. As the trampoline mat springs up, driven by your friend's weight, it will hit you and launch you into the air with far more energy than you could ever get on your own. The basketball acts as a mobile "trampoline" for the tennis ball, boosting it with the energy from its own rebound.

1. Free Fall Velocity

Ignoring air resistance, an object dropped from a height `h` will have a velocity `v` just before impact given by the laws of kinematics:

v = sqrt(2 * g * h), where `g` is the acceleration due to gravity (~9.8 m/s²).

2. Elastic Collision

The interaction between the two balls is modeled as a 1D perfectly elastic collision. In such a collision, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. For two objects with masses `M` and `m` and initial velocities `V_i` and `v_i`, their final velocities `V_f` and `v_f` are given by:

V_f = ((M-m)/(M+m)) * V_i + (2m/(M+m)) * v_i

v_f = (2M/(M+m)) * V_i - ((M-m)/(M+m)) * v_i

In our scenario, the large ball has just rebounded from the ground, so its initial velocity is `V_i = +v`. The small ball is still falling, so its initial velocity is `v_i = -v`. Substituting these into the equation for the small ball's final velocity `v_f` gives:

v_f = (2M*v / (M+m)) - (-(M-m)*v / (M+m)) = v * (2M + M - m) / (M+m) = v * (3M - m) / (M+m)

3. The Ideal Limit

Consider the ideal case where the large ball is much, much heavier than the small one (`M >> m`). In this limit, `m` becomes negligible. The equation simplifies to:

v_f ≈ v * (3M / M) = 3v

The small ball leaves the collision with three times the speed it had coming in!

4. Final Height

The maximum height an object reaches after being launched upwards with velocity `v_f` is `h_f = v_f² / (2g)`. If `v_f = 3v`, then:

h_f = (3v)² / (2g) = 9 * v² / (2g)

Since the original height `h = v² / (2g)`, we find that h_f = 9h. In the ideal limit, the small ball bounces to nine times its original height.
