Visualizing The Space of Rotations: SO(3)

An interactive exploration of the geometry of 3D rotations.

Recommended device: Desktop

Plain-Language Overview

Every possible way you can rotate an object in 3D space can be thought of as a "point" in an abstract "space of all rotations." This simulation visualizes this abstract space as a solid ball. A point inside this ball represents a unique 3D rotation: the direction from the center to the point gives the axis of rotation, and the distance from the center gives the angle of rotation. On the left, drag the point inside the "Rotation Ball" to define a rotation. On the right, see a 3D object instantly perform that rotation. This provides an intuitive feel for the strange geometry of the rotation group SO(3).

Beginner's Guide

1. The Axis and Angle of Rotation

Any 3D rotation can be described by an axis to rotate around (like a skewer through an apple) and an angle of how much to turn. The "Rotation Ball" on the left is a direct picture of this. Imagine a line from the center of the transparent ball to the red point you control. That line is the axis of rotation. The length of that line is the angle of rotation. The maximum angle is π (180 degrees), which is the radius of the ball.

2. The Strange Surface of the Ball

A rotation by +180 degrees is the exact same thing as a rotation by -180 degrees around the same axis. In our ball visualization, this means that two points on opposite sides of the sphere—called antipodal points—represent the same rotation. When you drag the control point to the surface, you will see its "ghost" appear on the opposite side to represent this identification. This means the space of rotations isn't really a ball; it's a more complex object called Real Projective Space (RP³).

3. Order Matters! (Non-Commutativity)

Unlike adding numbers (where 2+3 = 3+2), the order of rotations matters. Try the demo buttons: reset, then click "Apply X then Y". Note the orientation. Reset again, then click "Apply Y then X". The object ends up in a completely different orientation! This property, that A * B ≠ B * A, is called non-commutativity and is fundamental to rotation group theory.

Core Theory: SO(3) and Quaternions

The set of all possible 3D rotations forms a mathematical structure called a Lie Group, specifically the Special Orthogonal group SO(3). To implement rotations smoothly, this simulation uses quaternions, 4D numbers that efficiently represent rotations. A point v = (x, y, z) inside our parameter ball is converted to a unit quaternion q as follows: First, the angle `θ = ||v||` and the axis `n = v / θ`. Then, the quaternion `q = (cos(θ/2), sin(θ/2) * n_x, sin(θ/2) * n_y, sin(θ/2) * n_z)`. This quaternion is then used to generate a rotation matrix for the 3D object.
