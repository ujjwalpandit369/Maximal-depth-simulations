Damped and Driven Harmonic Oscillator

An interactive demonstration of resonance.

Plain-Language Overview

From pushing a child on a swing to the design of bridges and the tuning of a radio, the principle of resonance is everywhere. This simulation explores this fundamental concept using a simple system: a mass attached to a spring. You can control the properties of the system, including a damping force that slows it down and an external driving force that pushes it. By changing the frequency of the external push, you'll discover that when the driving frequency matches the system's own "natural" frequency, the oscillations can grow dramatically—a powerful phenomenon known as resonance.

Beginner's Guide — Pushing a Swing

The best way to understand this system is to think about pushing a child on a swing.

Step 1: The Natural Frequency

A swing, left to its own devices, will swing back and forth at a specific, natural frequency. This frequency depends on the length of its chains. Our mass-spring system is the same; it has a natural frequency determined by its mass (`m`) and the stiffness of the spring (`k`).

Step 2: Damping

A swing won't oscillate forever. Air resistance and friction in the hinges will gradually slow it down and make the swings smaller. This is a **damping** force. In our simulation, this is represented by the dashpot (like a small shock absorber), and its strength is controlled by the damping coefficient (`b`).

Step 3: The Driving Force

Now, you start pushing the swing. You are the **driving force**. You can choose how hard you push (the `Driving Force Amplitude, F₀`) and how often you push (the `Driving Frequency, ωd`).

Step 4: Resonance!

How should you push the swing to get it to go really high? You don't need to push hard. You need to push smart. You instinctively time your pushes to match the swing's natural frequency. You give a small push at just the right moment in each cycle, adding energy efficiently.

This is **resonance**. When the driving frequency (`ωd`) is equal or very close to the natural frequency (`ω₀`), the system's amplitude grows dramatically. In the simulation, you can find the resonance point by adjusting the `Driving Frequency` slider. Watch the "Amplitude vs. Driving Frequency" graph to see the peak, and see the effect on the oscillating block. If you drive the system at a very different frequency (too fast or too slow), you will be pushing at the wrong times, often against the system's own motion, and the amplitude will be small.

Step 5: The Role of Damping in Resonance

In a system with zero damping, the amplitude at resonance would grow to infinity! In any real system, including our simulation, the damping force limits the peak amplitude. The drag force gets stronger as the block moves faster, eventually balancing the energy being put in by the driving force. A higher damping value (`b`) will result in a lower, wider resonance peak.

Equation of Motion

The system is described by a second-order linear differential equation, which is Newton's Second Law (`F=ma`) applied to the block. The total force is the sum of the spring force, the damping force, and the external driving force.

m * a(t) + b * v(t) + k * x(t) = F₀ * cos(ωd * t)

Where `x` is position, `v` is velocity, and `a` is acceleration.

Key Frequencies and Parameters

  • Natural Frequency (ω₀): The frequency at which the system would oscillate if there were no damping or driving. ω₀ = sqrt(k/m).
  • Driving Frequency (ωd): The frequency of the external periodic force. This is the main variable you control to find resonance.

Steady-State Amplitude

After any initial transient behavior dies out, the system settles into a "steady-state" oscillation at the driving frequency `ωd`. The amplitude `A` of this steady-state oscillation depends on how close the driving frequency is to the natural frequency, and on the amount of damping. The formula is:

A(ωd) = (F₀/m) / sqrt( (ω₀² - ωd²)² + (b*ωd/m)² )

The "Amplitude vs. Driving Frequency" graph in the simulation is a plot of this equation. You can see that the denominator becomes smallest when `ωd` is close to `ω₀`, which makes the amplitude `A` very large. This is the resonance peak.

Phase Shift

An interesting feature of driven oscillators is the phase shift between the driving force and the block's motion.

  • Well below resonance (ωd << ω₀): The block moves in phase with the driver. The driver pushes right, the block moves right.
  • At resonance (ωd ≈ ω₀): The block's velocity is in phase with the driver's force. This means the block's position lags the driver by 90 degrees (a quarter cycle). The driver is pushing hardest when the block is moving fastest through its equilibrium point, which is the most efficient way to transfer energy.
  • Well above resonance (ωd >> ω₀): The block moves almost completely out of phase (180 degrees) with the driver. The driver pushes right, but the block is moving left. The system cannot keep up with the rapid driving force.

Applications and Dangers

Resonance is a critical concept in many areas of science and engineering.

  • Electrical Engineering: RLC circuits (containing resistors, inductors, and capacitors) are direct electrical analogs of this mechanical system. Resonance is used to tune radios and filters to select a specific frequency.
  • Music: The body of a guitar or violin is designed to resonate at specific frequencies, amplifying the sound produced by the strings.
  • Structural Engineering: Engineers must carefully calculate the natural frequencies of bridges, buildings, and aircraft wings to ensure that external forces (like wind, earthquakes, or engine vibrations) do not match them. The catastrophic collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 is a famous example of destructive resonance caused by wind.
