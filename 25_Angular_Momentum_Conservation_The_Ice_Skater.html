Plain-Language Overview

One of the most elegant demonstrations of a physics principle is an ice skater performing a spin. They start spinning slowly with their arms outstretched, and then, by simply pulling their arms in, they can dramatically increase their speed without any external help. This isn't magic; it's the law of **conservation of angular momentum**. This simulation lets you explore this principle directly. You can control a simple model of a spinning skater and toggle their arms between "in" and "out." As you do, you'll see how changing the distribution of mass instantly affects the rate of spin, all while the total angular momentum—the fundamental quantity of rotation—remains perfectly constant.

Beginner's Guide: The Physics of a Spin

Step 1: Angular Momentum (L)

Just like a moving object has linear momentum (mass × velocity), a spinning object has **angular momentum**. It's a measure of how much "rotational motion" an object has. A key law of physics states that for a system with no external twisting forces (torques), its total angular momentum cannot change. It is conserved.

Step 2: Moment of Inertia (I)

The angular momentum of an object depends on two things: how fast it's spinning (its angular velocity, ω) and how its mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation. This mass distribution is called the **moment of inertia (I)**. It's a measure of "rotational laziness"—how hard it is to get the object spinning. Mass that is far from the axis of rotation contributes much more to the moment of inertia than mass that is close to the axis.

Step 3: The Skater's Secret

The relationship between these three quantities is simple: L = I × ω. Since angular momentum (L) must be conserved, if a skater changes their moment of inertia (I), their angular velocity (ω) *must* change in the opposite way to keep the product constant.

  • Arms Out: When the skater's arms are outstretched, their mass is distributed far from their spin axis. This gives them a **large moment of inertia (I)**. To maintain a certain angular momentum L, their spin speed ω must be relatively **low**.
  • Arms In: When the skater pulls their arms in close to their body, they are moving mass closer to the axis of rotation. This **decreases their moment of inertia (I)**. Since L must stay constant, their spin speed ω must **increase** to compensate.
This is the entire trick: Iout × ωout = Iin × ωin. You can see this relationship directly in the bar chart in the simulation.

Step 4: Using the Simulation

The simulation starts with the skater spinning slowly with arms out.

  1. Note the initial values for I, ω, and L in the bar chart.
  2. Click the **"Pull Arms In"** button.
  3. Watch as the skater's arms move inward and their spin speed (ω) dramatically increases.
  4. Now look at the bar chart again. The bar for I has gone down, the bar for ω has gone up, but the bar for the total angular momentum L has remained exactly the same!
You can use the sliders to change the mass of the arms and their positions to see how this affects the magnitude of the change in speed.

Core Theory: Conservation of L

1. Moment of Inertia (I)

The moment of inertia for our simplified skater model is the sum of the inertia of their body (a cylinder) and their two arms (point masses).

I = Ibody + 2mr²

Where Ibody is a constant, m is the mass of one arm, and r is the current distance of the arms from the center.

2. Angular Momentum (L)

Angular momentum is the product of the moment of inertia and the angular velocity, ω.

L = Iω

3. Conservation Law

When the skater pulls their arms in, there are no external torques on the system, so the total angular momentum must be conserved.

Linitial = Lfinal

Iinitialωinitial = Ifinalωfinal

This allows us to predict the final angular velocity:

ωfinal = ωinitial * (Iinitial / Ifinal)

Since pulling the arms in makes Ifinal smaller than Iinitial, the ratio (Iinitial / Ifinal) is greater than 1, and the final spin speed must be higher.
