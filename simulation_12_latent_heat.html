Latent Heat and Phase Change

Visualizing the "hidden" energy required to melt ice and boil water.

Estimated Runtime: 1-5 minutes | Recommended Device: Desktop | Approx. HTML Size: 150 KB

Plain-Language Overview

If you put a pot of ice on a stove and turn on the heat, you might expect its temperature to rise steadily until it's all steam. But that's not what happens. The temperature gets stuck at 0°C (32°F) while the ice is melting, and gets stuck again at 100°C (212°F) while the water is boiling. Why? This simulation explores the concept of "latent heat"—the significant amount of energy that must be absorbed by a substance to change its phase (e.g., from solid to liquid) without changing its temperature at all. You'll see how adding heat can either raise a substance's temperature or change its phase, but not both at the same time.

Beginner's Guide — A Step-by-Step Heating Curve

Phase 1: Heating the Ice

We start with a block of ice below its freezing point. As we add heat from the hot plate, the water molecules in the ice crystal lattice vibrate more and more vigorously. This increased vibration is what we measure as a rise in temperature. The graph shows a steady increase in temperature for the heat we've added.

Phase 2: The Melting Plateau (Latent Heat of Fusion)

When the ice reaches 0°C, a remarkable thing happens. Even though we are still adding heat, the temperature stops rising. The graph shows a flat plateau. Where is the energy going? It's being used to break the strong hydrogen bonds that hold the water molecules in their rigid ice structure. This energy is called the latent heat of fusion. "Latent" means hidden, because it doesn't show up on a thermometer. The beaker shows a mix of ice and water, and the microscopic view shows some molecules breaking free from the lattice. The temperature will not budge from 0°C until every last bit of ice has melted.

Phase 3: Heating the Water

Once all the ice has turned into liquid water, the added heat once again causes the temperature to rise. The now-free water molecules move around faster and faster, which we measure as an increase in temperature. The graph shows the temperature climbing again.

Phase 4: The Boiling Plateau (Latent Heat of Vaporization)

At 100°C, we hit another plateau. The temperature again stops rising. This time, the added heat energy is used to overcome the weaker forces that keep the water molecules together as a liquid, allowing them to escape into the air as a gas (steam). This energy is the latent heat of vaporization. In the microscopic view, you can see molecules flying off from the surface. The temperature will remain locked at 100°C until all the water has boiled away.

Phase 5: Heating the Steam

After all the water has become steam, any further heat added will increase the temperature of the steam, making the gas molecules move even faster.

Analogy: The Deconstruction Crew

Imagine a neatly stacked, frozen block of bricks representing ice.

  1. Heating Ice: You shake the entire block. The more you shake, the more it vibrates (temperature rises).
  2. Melting: A deconstruction crew arrives. They use their energy (latent heat) to pry the bricks apart from each other and turn the neat stack into a jumbled pile (liquid). While the crew is working, all their energy goes into demolition, not shaking. The overall vibration level (temperature) stays constant until the block is fully disassembled.
  3. Heating Water: With the bricks in a pile, you start shaking the whole pile. The vibrations increase again (temperature rises).
  4. Boiling: A new crew arrives with forklifts. Their job is to take each individual brick and launch it into the air (gas). All their energy (latent heat) goes into lifting and launching, not shaking the pile. The vibration level of the pile stays constant until the last brick is gone.

Specific Heat Capacity

When there is no phase change, the heat added (Q) is related to the temperature change (ΔT) by the specific heat capacity (c) and the mass (m) of the substance.

Q = m * c * ΔT

The specific heat capacity is the amount of energy required to raise 1 gram of a substance by 1 degree Celsius. It's different for ice, water, and steam.

  • c (ice) ≈ 2.09 J/g°C
  • c (water) ≈ 4.18 J/g°C
  • c (steam) ≈ 2.01 J/g°C
Example: To heat 10g of ice from -20°C to 0°C (a ΔT of 20°C), you need:
Q = 10g * 2.09 J/g°C * 20°C = 418 J

Latent Heat

During a phase change, the heat added (Q) does not change the temperature. Instead, it's used to change the phase, and is related to the mass (m) and the specific latent heat (L).

Q = m * L

The specific latent heat is the amount of energy required to change the phase of 1 gram of a substance at a constant temperature.

  • L (fusion) for water = 334 J/g
  • L (vaporization) for water = 2260 J/g
Example: To melt 10g of ice at 0°C, you need:
Q = 10g * 334 J/g = 3340 J
Notice this is much more energy than was needed to heat the ice by 20°C! This is why the melting plateau is long. The boiling plateau is even longer because the latent heat of vaporization is so large.
