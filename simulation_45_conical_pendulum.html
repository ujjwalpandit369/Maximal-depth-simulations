The Conical Pendulum

A demonstration of uniform circular motion and centripetal force.

Estimated Runtime: Interactive | Recommended Device: Desktop | Approx. HTML Size: 150 KB

Plain-Language Overview

A conical pendulum is a weight fixed to the end of a string, but instead of swinging back and forth, it revolves in a horizontal circle at a constant speed, with the string sweeping out the shape of a cone. For this stable circular motion to be possible, the forces acting on the weight must be perfectly balanced. This simulation visualizes the pendulum and its free-body diagram, showing how the tension in the string provides both the upward force needed to counteract gravity and the inward "centripetal" force needed to keep the weight moving in a circle.

Beginner's Guide — The Balance of Forces

Step 1: The Setup

Imagine a ball tied to a string, which is attached to the ceiling. If you give the ball a push sideways, it will start to revolve in a horizontal circle. The string will trace out a cone shape, which is where the name "conical pendulum" comes from.

Step 2: The Two Real Forces

There are only two fundamental forces acting on the ball:

  1. Gravity (`F_g`): A constant downward pull from the Earth. This is shown as a red arrow.
  2. Tension (`T`): The pulling force from the string, which acts along the string towards the pivot point. This is shown as a solid blue arrow.

Step 3: Breaking Down the Tension

The key to understanding the motion is to resolve the diagonal tension force into two perpendicular components (shown as dashed blue arrows):

  • A vertical component (`T_y`) that points straight up.
  • A horizontal component (`T_x`) that points inward, toward the center of the circle.

Step 4: The Force Balance

For the pendulum to move in a stable horizontal circle, these forces must be perfectly balanced in a specific way:

  • In the vertical direction: The ball is not moving up or down, so the forces must cancel out. The upward vertical component of tension must exactly equal the downward force of gravity. `T_y = F_g`.
  • In the horizontal direction: There is an unbalanced force! The horizontal component of tension, `T_x`, is pointing toward the center of the circle. This is the **centripetal force**. It is the net force that constantly pulls the ball away from a straight path and forces it to move in a circle.
The simulation allows you to change the radius of the circle. You will see that to maintain a wider circle, the ball must travel faster, and the angle of the string must increase to provide the larger centripetal force required.

Force Balance Equations

By resolving the tension `T` into components using the angle `θ` from the vertical, we get:

Vertical Balance: T * cos(θ) = m * g

Horizontal (Centripetal) Force: T * sin(θ) = m * v² / r

Solving for Physical Quantities

We can manipulate these two equations to solve for the required speed and other properties.

  1. Angle `θ`: From the geometry of the pendulum, we can see that `sin(θ) = r / L`. Therefore, the angle is determined directly by your choice of radius and string length: θ = asin(r / L).
  2. Tension `T`: From the vertical balance equation, we can solve for tension: T = mg / cos(θ).
  3. Speed `v`: If we divide the horizontal equation by the vertical equation, `T` cancels out:
    (T*sin(θ))/(T*cos(θ)) = (mv²/r) / (mg)
    tan(θ) = v² / (rg)
    Solving for `v` gives: v = sqrt(r * g * tan(θ)).
  4. Period `T_p`: The period of one revolution is the circumference of the circle divided by the speed: T_p = 2πr / v.
