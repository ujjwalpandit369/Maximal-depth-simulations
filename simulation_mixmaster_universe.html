Overview

The standard model of cosmology describes a Big Bang that was incredibly smooth and uniform (isotropic). The Mixmaster model, a solution to Einstein's equations known as a Bianchi Type IX spacetime, explores a radically different possibility: a chaotic, anisotropic beginning. In this model, the universe doesn't expand equally in all directions. Instead, it might expand rapidly along one axis while contracting along the other two, with these directions chaotically switching over time. This simulation visualizes a patch of such a universe as a 3D box, allowing you to watch it erratically change shape as it evolves.

Beginner's Guide to a Chaotic Cosmos

1. Isotropic vs. Anisotropic

Imagine blowing up a perfectly round balloon. As it inflates, it expands equally in every direction. This is isotropic expansion. The standard model of the Big Bang assumes the early universe expanded this way—smoothly and uniformly in all directions.

Now, imagine squeezing a water balloon. It might bulge out in the middle while getting thinner at the ends. If you were to do this repeatedly and erratically, the shape would change in a complex way. This is anisotropic behavior—changing differently in different directions. The Mixmaster model proposes the very fabric of space behaved this way near the Big Bang.

2. An Everyday Analogy: A Squash Ball in a Corner

A great analogy for the Mixmaster universe is a squash ball being hit into the corner of a court.

  • When the ball hits one wall, it compresses along that direction and expands along the other two.
  • It then ricochets off another wall, compressing along a new direction and expanding along the others.
  • The result is a chaotic, tumbling, shape-changing motion.
The Mixmaster universe does something similar as it approaches the initial singularity (the "corner"). Gravity causes space to collapse along one direction, which in turn causes it to expand along the other two. This "bounce" repeats infinitely, with the axes of expansion and contraction switching chaotically.

3. What the Simulation Shows

This simulation visualizes this concept using a box and a graph.

  • Universe Shape (Top Canvas): The 3D box represents a patch of the universe. Its side lengths correspond to the "scale factors" (`a₁, a₂, a₃`) in the three spatial directions. When you run the simulation, you'll see the box rapidly and chaotically stretch and squeeze along its different axes.
  • Scale Factor Evolution (Bottom Canvas): This plot shows the values of the three scale factors (color-coded) over time. Time on this graph is logarithmic, meaning it gets infinitely dense as it approaches the "Big Bang" at time zero on the left. Notice the wild oscillations, where one factor grows while the others shrink, which is the hallmark of Mixmaster dynamics.

Press Run Simulation to watch the chaotic evolution unfold. The key idea is that instead of a smooth, predictable start, the universe might have had a violent, chaotic beginning before eventually settling into the isotropic expansion we see today.
