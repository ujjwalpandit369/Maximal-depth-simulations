Orbits as Geodesics in Curved Spacetime

Visualizing why a planetary orbit is considered a "straight line."

Plain-Language Overview

We typically think of a planet's orbit as a curved path, caused by the "force" of gravity from its star. General Relativity offers a revolutionary alternative: gravity is not a force. Instead, the star's mass curves the fabric of spacetime around it. The planet is simply moving in the "straightest possible line" through this now-curved spacetime. A straight line on a curved surface is called a geodesic. This simulation shows this duality. On the left, you see the familiar curved orbit in 3D space. On the right, you see the planet's path (its "worldline") traced on the curved spacetime created by the star, showing how this same motion can be viewed as a geodesic.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Two Views

This simulation presents two different ways of looking at the exact same physical event: a planet orbiting a star.

  • 3D Space View (Left): This is the view we are familiar with. It shows the star at the center and the planet moving along a curved, elliptical path. This is the orbit as it would appear if you were observing it from afar in space.
  • Spacetime View (Right): This is a more abstract but more fundamental view. It shows the "rubber sheet" of spacetime being curved by the star's mass. The path drawn here is the planet's worldline—its trajectory through both space and time. Notice how this path follows the natural "groove" or contour of the curved spacetime. This is the geodesic.

2. What is a Geodesic?

A geodesic is the shortest and "straightest" possible path between two points on a given surface. On a flat surface like a piece of paper, a geodesic is a simple straight line. But on a curved surface, a geodesic can look curved to an outside observer.

Analogy: An Airline Flight. Imagine you want to fly from New York to Rome. If you look at a flat world map, the flight path looks like a long, pronounced arc, not a straight line. Why? Because the pilots are flying the shortest possible route over the curved surface of the Earth. That arc *is* the geodesic on the sphere. A planetary orbit is the exact same concept, but for a path through curved 4D spacetime instead of a curved 2D surface.

3. Gravity as Geometry

This leads to the core insight of General Relativity. The planet is not being "pulled" by a force. It feels no force at all; it is in a state of perfect free-fall. It is simply following its natural tendency to move in a straight line. The "illusion" of a force only appears because the spacetime it is moving through has been curved by the mass of the star. In Einstein's view, gravity is geometry. The simulation shows that the "curved" path on the left and the "straight" path on the right are just two different ways of describing the same geodesic motion.

The Geodesic Equation

In General Relativity, the path of a freely falling particle is described by the geodesic equation. In its full form, it involves Christoffel symbols (Γ) which encode all the information about the curvature of spacetime.

d2xμ/dτ2 + Γμαβ (dxα/dτ)(dxβ/dτ) = 0

This equation essentially says that the acceleration of an object along its worldline is zero, when accounting for the effects of curvature. It is the relativistic equivalent of Newton's first law (an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by a force). In General Relativity, gravity is not considered a force, so an orbiting planet is simply following its inertial, "straightest" path. Our simulation uses a much simpler Newtonian gravity calculation to generate the orbit, but the path it generates is a very good approximation of a true geodesic in the weak gravity of a star.
