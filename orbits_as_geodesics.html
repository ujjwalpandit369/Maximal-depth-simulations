Orbits as Geodesics in Curved Spacetime

Visualizing why a planetary orbit is considered a "straight line."

Plain-Language Overview

We typically think of an orbit as a curved path caused by the "force" of gravity. General Relativity offers a revolutionary alternative: gravity is not a force. Instead, a star's mass curves spacetime. The planet is simply moving in the "straightest possible line" through this curved spacetime. A straight line on a curved surface is called a geodesic. This simulation shows this duality. On the left is the familiar curved orbit. On the right, the planet's path (its "worldline") is traced on the curved spacetime, showing how this motion can be viewed as a geodesic.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Two Views

This simulation shows two ways of looking at a planet orbiting a star. The 3D Space View (Left) is the familiar view of a curved orbit. The Spacetime View (Right) is more abstract. It shows the "rubber sheet" of spacetime being curved by the star's mass. The path drawn here is the planet's worldline—its trajectory through space and time. Notice how this path follows the natural "groove" of the curved spacetime.

2. What is a Geodesic?

A geodesic is the straightest possible path on a curved surface. An airline flight path looks like an arc on a flat map, but it's the shortest route on the curved Earth. A planetary orbit is the same concept, but for a path through curved 4D spacetime.

3. Gravity as Geometry

The planet is not being "pulled" by a force. It is in free-fall, following its natural tendency to move in a straight line through a spacetime that has been curved by the star. In Einstein's view, gravity is geometry.

Core Theory

In General Relativity, the path of a freely falling particle is described by the geodesic equation: d²xμ/dτ² + Γμαβ (dxα/dτ)(dxβ/dτ) = 0. This is the relativistic equivalent of Newton's first law. Gravity is not a force, so an orbiting planet is simply following its inertial, "straightest" path through curved spacetime.
