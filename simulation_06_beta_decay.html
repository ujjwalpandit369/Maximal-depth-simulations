Plain-Language Overview

Beta decay is a common form of radioactivity where an unstable atomic nucleus changes its identity. For example, Carbon-14 (used in carbon dating) decays into stable Nitrogen-14. This process is governed by the weak nuclear force, one of the four fundamental forces of nature. This simulation provides a two-level view of beta decay: you'll see the nucleus transform, and then "zoom in" to a Feynman diagram showing how, at a deeper level, a single quark changes its flavor, emitting an electron and an elusive antineutrino. By running many decays, you will also recreate the evidence that first pointed to the neutrino's existence.

Beginner's Guide — A Change from Within

Step 1: The Unstable Nucleus

We start with a nucleus of Carbon-14, which contains 6 protons and 8 neutrons. This particular combination is unstable; the nucleus has too many neutrons for its number of protons and wants to reach a more stable, lower-energy state. It does this via beta decay.

Step 2: The Nuclear Transformation

During beta decay, one of the neutrons inside the nucleus spontaneously turns into a proton. This changes the chemical element! With 7 protons and 7 neutrons, our atom is no longer Carbon—it has become Nitrogen-14, which is a very stable nucleus. To conserve electric charge (since a neutral neutron became a positive proton), a negative particle must be created: an electron. This electron is ejected from the nucleus at high speed and is also known as a "beta particle."

Step 3: The Energy Puzzle and the "Ghost" Particle

When scientists first studied beta decay, they found a puzzle. The energy difference between a C-14 nucleus and an N-14 nucleus is a fixed, specific amount. They expected the ejected electron to always carry away this amount of energy. Instead, they observed that the electrons came out with a continuous spectrum of energies, from nearly zero up to the maximum expected value. Where was the missing energy going?

In 1930, Wolfgang Pauli proposed a radical solution: an undetected *third particle* was also being emitted, carrying away the missing energy. Because this particle was electrically neutral and had to be very light, it was incredibly difficult to detect. Enrico Fermi later named it the **neutrino** (or in this case, an **antineutrino**). The total energy is shared between the electron and the antineutrino, which is why the electron's energy can vary. In the simulation, each time you click "Decay," you add a point to the electron energy graph, slowly building up this characteristic spectrum.

Step 4: The Fundamental View - Quarks and the Weak Force

What's happening at an even deeper level? Protons and neutrons are made of smaller particles called quarks. A neutron is made of one "up" and two "down" quarks (udd), while a proton is two "up"s and one "down" (uud). Beta decay is the fundamental process where a **down quark transforms into an up quark**. This change is mediated by the **weak nuclear force**. The `d` quark emits a force-carrying particle, a `W⁻` boson, which then instantly decays into the electron and the antineutrino. The Feynman Diagram panel in the simulation visualizes this fundamental interaction.

Nuclear and Particle Reactions

The overall nuclear reaction is written as:

¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + ν̅ₑ

The subscripts are the number of protons (atomic number) and the superscripts are the total number of protons and neutrons (mass number). `e⁻` is the electron and `ν̅ₑ` is the electron antineutrino.

The underlying fundamental process is the decay of a down quark (`d`) into an up quark (`u`):

d → u + W⁻

Followed immediately by the decay of the virtual W⁻ boson:

W⁻ → e⁻ + ν̅ₑ

Energy Spectrum

The total energy released in the decay, called the Q-value, is fixed by the mass difference between the initial and final nuclei: `Q = (m_C14 - m_N14)c²`. This energy is shared as kinetic energy between the electron and the antineutrino (the recoil of the heavy nucleus is negligible).

Q ≈ KE_electron + KE_antineutrino

Because this energy can be shared in any proportion between the two outgoing light particles, the electron's energy `KE_electron` can range from 0 (if the antineutrino takes all the energy) to `Q` (if the antineutrino takes no energy). This results in a continuous spectrum of electron energies, not a single sharp peak. The exact shape of the spectrum is determined by quantum mechanical phase space factors, and for a simple allowed decay like this, it can be approximated by:

N(E) ∝ pE(Q-E)² where `p` and `E` are the electron's momentum and energy.
