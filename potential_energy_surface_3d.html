Potential Energy Surface for a Chemical Reaction

A visualization of the energy landscape that governs a chemical reaction.

Overview

A chemical reaction is more than just an arrow in an equation; it's a dynamic journey across an energy landscape. This simulation visualizes that landscape, called a Potential Energy Surface (PES), for a simple reaction. The surface's "valleys" represent stable molecules (reactants and products), while the "mountain pass" between them is the high-energy transition state. You can launch a particle, representing the reacting system, with a certain amount of kinetic energy and watch its trajectory. Will it have enough energy to climb the pass and form products, or will it fail and roll back? This simulation provides an intuitive, topographical view of what it takes for a chemical reaction to succeed.

Beginner's Guide to Reaction Landscapes

Imagine a chemical reaction as a journey through a mountainous region. The PES is the map of this region.

Step 1: The Landscape (PES)

The axes of our map represent the "geometry" of the molecules, for example, the distances between the atoms. The "height" at any point on the map is the potential energy for that specific arrangement of atoms.

  • Valleys: The deep blue areas are low-energy valleys. These are stable configurations. One valley holds the Reactants, and the other holds the Products.
  • Mountains: The red and yellow areas are high-energy mountains, representing unstable arrangements of atoms.
  • The Saddle Point: To get from the reactant valley to the product valley, the easiest path is not over the highest peak, but through the lowest "mountain pass." This pass is called the saddle point or the transition state. It's the highest energy point along the ideal reaction path.

Step 2: The Trajectory

A chemical reaction is not a slow crawl along the minimum energy path. Reacting molecules have kinetic energy (they are moving and vibrating). A reaction is a "trajectory"—a path a particle takes as it moves across this surface. The simulation shows this trajectory as an orange line.

Step 3: How to Use the Simulation

  1. Choose a View: Use the buttons to switch between a 2D Contour Map (like a topographical map) and a pseudo-3D Wireframe view. The contour map is often better for seeing the path clearly.
  2. Set Initial Velocity: Click and hold your mouse in the "Reactant Valley" (usually the bottom-left). An arrow will appear. Drag your mouse to set the direction and speed (initial kinetic energy) for your particle.
  3. Launch!: Release the mouse to launch the particle on its trajectory.
  4. Observe the Outcome:
    • Successful Reaction: Did the particle have enough energy, aimed in the right direction, to get over the saddle point and into the product valley?
    • Failed Reaction: Did it not have enough energy and just "roll" back into the reactant valley? Or did it have the wrong angle and just bounce off a "mountain wall"?
  5. Reset: Click the "Reset" button to clear the old trajectory and launch a new one.

Controls

Click and drag in the reactant valley (bottom-left) to set the initial velocity, then release to launch.
