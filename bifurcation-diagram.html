Overview

This simulation explores how a very simple system can display surprisingly complex behavior as we change a single setting. The system is the logistic map, a basic model of population growth. The setting is the growth rate `r`. The main panel, the Bifurcation Diagram, is a map of the system's long-term behavior. The horizontal axis is the growth rate `r`, and the vertical axis shows the population levels the system eventually settles on. By watching how the plot splits and branches, you can see the system's "route to chaos." The live "Cobweb Plot" on the left shows what the system is doing for the specific `r` value you've chosen with the slider.

Beginner's Guide

1. A Simple Model of Population

Imagine a species of fish in a pond. Their population next year depends on their population this year. This relationship is modeled by the logistic map: xnext = r * xcurrent * (1 - xcurrent).

  • x is the population, scaled to be a number between 0 (extinction) and 1 (maximum possible population).
  • r is the growth rate. A low `r` means the fish reproduce slowly; a high `r` means they reproduce quickly.
The `(1 - x)` term represents a limit to growth—as the population `x` gets closer to 1, the pond gets too crowded, and the growth slows down. It's a simple feedback loop.

2. Two Ways to See the Behavior

This simulation has two linked panels:

  1. The Cobweb Plot (Left): This is a live view for a single value of `r` that you control with the slider. It shows how the population `x` changes from one year to the next. The red line "cobweb" starts at an initial population, goes up to the blue parabola (to calculate next year's population), then horizontally to the `y=x` line (to set up the next calculation). Watching this plot shows you the system's short-term dynamics.
  2. The Bifurcation Diagram (Right): This is the main event. It's a summary of the system's long-term behavior across a whole range of `r` values. To create it, the computer picks an `r`, runs the simulation for 1000 "years" to let it settle, then plots the population values for the next 1000 years as a single vertical line of dots. It then moves to the next `r` and repeats. Click "Generate Diagram" to see this process.

3. The "Route to Chaos"

The magic happens when you look at the bifurcation diagram.

  • For low `r` (e.g., 2.8), the system settles on a single, stable population. The plot is one thin line.
  • At `r=3.0`, the line splits in two. This is a bifurcation. The population no longer settles on one value but oscillates between two values (a 2-year cycle).
  • At `r=3.45`, it splits again into a 4-year cycle. Then it splits into an 8-cycle, then 16, and so on, faster and faster. This is called a period-doubling cascade.
  • Finally, around `r=3.57`, the system becomes chaotic. The population never settles down and never repeats. It can take on a huge range of values, which appear as a solid block of dots. Within this chaos, however, there are strange windows of stability—white stripes where the system temporarily becomes orderly again.
This transition from order to chaos is a common pattern found in many real-world systems, from fluid dynamics to electrical circuits.

Core Theory

The Logistic Map

The entire simulation is based on iterating a single, simple quadratic function. The state of the system in the next time step, xn+1, is determined solely by its state in the current time step, xn, and the parameter r.

xn+1 = r * xn * (1 - xn)

Despite its simplicity, this equation is one of the most famous examples of a system that can exhibit chaotic behavior.

Fixed Points and Stability

A "fixed point" is a value of `x` where the population stops changing. This happens when xn+1 = xn. For `r` values between 1 and 3, the system has one stable fixed point. The cobweb plot will show the path spiraling into this point.

Period-Doubling Bifurcations

As `r` increases past 3, the fixed point becomes unstable. It splits (bifurcates) into two new points, and the system now oscillates between them. This is called a 2-cycle. As `r` increases further, this 2-cycle becomes unstable and each of its points bifurcates, leading to a 4-cycle. This cascade of period-doubling bifurcations is a universal route to chaos. The ratio of the distances between successive bifurcations approaches a universal constant called the Feigenbaum constant (δ ≈ 4.669).

Worked Walkthroughs

Finding the First Bifurcation

  1. Use the slider to set r = 2.9. Watch the cobweb plot on the left. The red lines spiral into a single point. This is a stable system.
  2. Slowly drag the slider to the right. As you pass r = 3.0, you'll see the cobweb plot change. It no longer spirals to a point but settles into a small square box. This is the first bifurcation! The system now has two states it alternates between.
  3. Continue increasing `r` to 3.2. The box in the cobweb plot gets larger.
  4. Now find the next bifurcation. Slowly increase `r` from 3.2. Around r = 3.45, you'll see the cobweb plot change again, now settling into a pattern with four distinct values. You've found the second bifurcation.

Advanced Notes & Extensions

Universality and the Feigenbaum Constant

The period-doubling route to chaos is not unique to the logistic map. A huge number of different mathematical functions exhibit the exact same behavior. Furthermore, the ratio of the distances between successive bifurcations always converges to the same number, the Feigenbaum constant δ ≈ 4.669.... This discovery of universality was a major breakthrough in chaos theory, showing that the transition to chaos has deep, underlying mathematical rules.

Self-Similarity

If you were to "zoom in" on a small part of the chaotic region of the bifurcation diagram, you would find a distorted, miniature copy of the entire diagram within it. This property of self-similarity is a hallmark of fractals and is intimately connected to the period-doubling cascade.

Analogies & Intuition

1. A Dripping Faucet

Think of a faucet that you are opening very, very slowly.

  • Low `r` (Stable): At first, the water drips out one drop at a time, at a perfectly regular interval. Drip... drip... drip. This is like the stable fixed point.
  • Medium `r` (Period-Doubling): As you open the tap a bit more, you might find a new rhythm. Drip-drip... drip-drip... This is a 2-cycle. A bit more, and it might become a four-drip pattern. This is the period-doubling cascade.
  • High `r` (Chaos): Open it further, and the dripping becomes irregular and turbulent. You can't predict when the next drop will fall. This is chaos.
Limitation: A real faucet is much more complex, but the analogy for the *transition* from simple, periodic behavior to complex, aperiodic behavior is very strong.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. What is this diagram actually showing?
It's a map of behavior. For any given setting of the "growth rate" `r` on the horizontal axis, the black dots show the long-term values of the population `x` on the vertical axis. If you see one dot, the population is stable. If you see two dots, it's oscillating between two values.
2. Why does it take a while to generate?
To draw each vertical slice of the diagram, the computer must simulate thousands of time steps to be sure it has found the "long-term" behavior. Doing this for every pixel across the canvas is computationally intensive.
3. What are the white stripes in the chaotic area?
These are "islands of stability." They are small windows of the `r` parameter where the system suddenly snaps back from chaos into a stable, periodic cycle (like a 3-cycle or 6-cycle). These windows are a fascinating and complex feature of chaotic systems.
4. What is a "cobweb plot"?
It's a graphical way to see an iterated function in action. You start at a value `x` on the horizontal axis, move vertically to the function's curve (the parabola), and then move horizontally to the `y=x` line. The new point on the horizontal axis is your next value of `x`. Repeating this process traces out the system's trajectory.
