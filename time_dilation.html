The Light Clock and Time Dilation

A classic thought experiment demonstrating why time slows down for moving objects.

Plain-Language Overview

Einstein's theory of special relativity predicts that time is not absolute. A moving clock ticks more slowly than a stationary one. This isn't a mechanical defect; it's a fundamental property of spacetime. To understand why, we use a "light clock," which consists of two mirrors with a photon bouncing between them. Each bounce is one "tick." This simulation shows two such clocks: one at rest, and one on a fast-moving spaceship. By observing them, you can see for yourself that because the speed of light is constant for everyone, the moving clock must tick slower.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Two Principles of Special Relativity

You only need to accept two principles: 1) The laws of physics are the same for all observers in uniform motion. 2) The speed of light in a vacuum (c) is the same for all observers, regardless of their motion.

2. The Light Clock at Rest

The stationary clock's photon bounces straight up and down. The time for one tick is simply time = distance / speed. This is its "proper time."

3. The Light Clock in Motion (View from Earth)

From our stationary perspective, the moving clock's photon must travel a longer, diagonal path. But since its speed is still `c`, it must take a longer time to complete its trip. Therefore, the moving clock ticks slower.

4. The View from the Ship

Click "View from Ship." Now we are at rest relative to that clock. From our perspective, its photon goes straight up and down, while the "Earth" clock is the one moving, and *its* clock appears to run slow. Both views are correct! This is the essence of relativity.

Core Theory: Deriving Time Dilation

We can derive the formula using the Pythagorean theorem on the moving clock's light path. Let t' be the time for one half-tick on the moving clock and t₀ be the time for a half-tick on the rest clock. The sides of the right triangle are: the vertical path c*t₀, the horizontal path v*t', and the diagonal path c*t'.

(ct')² = (ct₀)² + (vt')². Solving for t', we get: t' = t₀ / √(1 - v²/c²). The term 1 / √(1 - v²/c²) is the Lorentz factor, γ. So, t' = γt₀. Since γ ≥ 1, the moving clock's time interval is always longer.
