Falling into a Black Hole

Comparing the views of a falling astronaut and a distant observer.

Plain-Language Overview

What would it be like to fall into a black hole? For Alice, the astronaut taking the plunge, the journey across the event horizon—the point of no return—is surprisingly uneventful and takes a finite amount of her time. But for Bob, watching from a safe distance, her fall appears to take an eternity. He sees her slow down as she approaches the event horizon, her image getting redder and dimmer, until she seems to freeze. This simulation shows both perspectives side-by-side.

Beginner's Guide

1. Bob's Perspective: The Eternal Fall

The left panel shows what Bob sees. Due to Gravitational Time Dilation, Alice's clock appears to tick slower and slower, eventually freezing as she reaches the event horizon. Due to Gravitational Redshift, light from her suit loses energy, shifting from white to red and fading to black. To Bob, she never crosses.

2. Alice's Perspective: A Quick Crossing

The right panel shows Alice's view. Her own clock ticks normally. She feels weightless as she's in free-fall. Crossing the event horizon is completely unremarkable. The main effect she sees is the bizarre distortion of starlight into a bright window in front of her.

Core Theory

The relationship between the proper time of the falling observer (dτ, Alice's time) and the coordinate time of the distant observer (dt, Bob's time) is given by the Schwarzschild metric: dτ = √(1 - Rₛ/r) dt, where `Rₛ` is the Schwarzschild radius and `r` is the distance. As `r` approaches `Rₛ`, `dt` approaches infinity for any finite `dτ`.
