Falling into a Black Hole

Comparing the views of a falling astronaut and a distant observer.

Plain-Language Overview

What would it be like to fall into a black hole? The answer depends entirely on who you ask. For Alice, the astronaut taking the plunge, the journey across the event horizon—the point of no return—is surprisingly uneventful and takes a finite amount of her time. But for Bob, her partner watching from a safe distance, her fall appears to take an eternity. He sees her slow down as she approaches the event horizon, her image getting redder and dimmer, until she seems to freeze in time, never quite crossing. This simulation shows both perspectives side-by-side, illustrating the extreme effects of gravitational time dilation and redshift near a black hole.

A Tale of Two Travelers

1. Bob's Perspective: The Eternal Fall

The left panel shows what Bob sees from his spaceship at a safe distance. As Alice falls towards the black hole, the intense gravity warps spacetime.

  • Gravitational Time Dilation: From Bob's perspective, time itself runs slower for Alice the deeper she gets into the gravity well. Her clock appears to tick slower and slower, eventually seeming to stop completely as she reaches the event horizon. Bob will never see her cross.
  • Gravitational Redshift: Light from Alice's suit has to climb out of the gravity well to reach Bob. This costs it energy, which shifts its wavelength towards the red end of the spectrum. As Alice approaches the horizon, the light becomes so redshifted that it fades beyond visibility. In the simulation, you will see her suit turn from white to red and then fade to black.

To Bob, Alice's fall is an infinite, fading process where she is seemingly "pasted" onto the event horizon forever.

2. Alice's Perspective: A Quick and Uneventful Crossing

The right panel shows what Alice sees. For her, time is passing normally—her own clock ticks at a steady rate. She is in free-fall, so she feels no forces. The moment she crosses the event horizon is... completely unremarkable. There is no sign, no wall, no special sensation. She simply passes the point of no return without realizing it.

  • Distortion of Starlight: What she *does* see is the sky of distant stars becoming bizarrely distorted. The stars in her forward view appear blueshifted and concentrated into a small, bright window due to an effect called "aberration of light." Behind her, the light is redshifted and the sky expands.
  • The Inevitable End: Once inside the event horizon, all possible future paths lead to the singularity at the center. The curvature of spacetime is so extreme that even to move "outwards" is to move forward in time towards the center.

The simulation shows her clock ticking normally all the way through. Her journey from the start to the singularity is finite in her own experience.

Time Dilation and Redshift Near a Black Hole

The physics is governed by the Schwarzschild metric. The relationship between the proper time of the falling observer (dτ, Alice's time) and the coordinate time of the distant observer (dt, Bob's time) is:

dτ = √(1 - Rs/r) dt

Where Rs is the Schwarzschild radius (2GM/c²) and `r` is Alice's distance from the center. As r approaches Rs, the term (1 - Rs/r) approaches zero. This means a finite interval of Alice's time (dτ) corresponds to an infinitely long interval of Bob's time (dt). This is why Bob never sees her cross.

Similarly, the gravitational redshift is given by:

λobserved = λemitted / √(1 - Rs/r)

As r approaches Rs, the denominator goes to zero, and the observed wavelength goes to infinity, shifting the light completely out of the visible spectrum.
