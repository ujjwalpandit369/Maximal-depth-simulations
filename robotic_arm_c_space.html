Welcome to Robot Abstract-o-Land!

On the left, you see a simple robotic arm with two joints, living on the surface of a sphere. This is its Workspace—the physical world it moves in. On the right is a square. This is its "mind," its Configuration Space (C-Space). This abstract map contains every single possible pose the robot arm can make.

Step 1: The Two Worlds

The robot's pose is completely defined by its two joint angles, `θ₁` and `θ₂`.

  • `θ₁` controls the rotation of the first link around the sphere's pole (like longitude).
  • `θ₂` controls the angle of the second link relative to the first.
The C-space on the right is a map of these two angles. The horizontal axis is `θ₁` (from 0° to 360°), and the vertical axis is `θ₂` (from -180° to 180°). The green dot shows the arm's current `(θ₁, θ₂)` configuration.

Step 2: Linked Motion

Try dragging the green dot around in the C-space square. As you do, watch the physical arm on the sphere move. Every point in the C-space corresponds to exactly one unique pose of the arm. Now try the sliders—they do the same thing!

Step 3: The Torus

Drag the green dot off the right side of the square. It reappears on the left! Now drag it off the top—it reappears on the bottom. Why? Because angles wrap around. 360° is the same as 0°. This means the C-space isn't really a square; it's a torus (a donut shape). The square is just a "flattened" map of this donut.

Step 4: Obstacles

This is where C-space becomes incredibly useful for robot motion planning. Click on the sphere on the left to place a circular obstacle (a "no-go zone").

Instantly, a complex shape appears in the C-space on the right. This is the forbidden region. Any green dot placed inside this region corresponds to a robot arm pose that would collide with the obstacle. The robot is not allowed to enter this part of its "mind."

Planning a path for the robot in the physical world can be very hard. But planning a path in C-space is much easier: just find a path for the green dot from its start to its goal that doesn't enter the forbidden region. This is the core idea behind modern robotics path planning.
