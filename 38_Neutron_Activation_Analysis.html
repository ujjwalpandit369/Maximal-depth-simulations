Neutron Activation Analysis (NAA) Simulator

A non-destructive technique for identifying the elemental composition of a sample.

Plain-Language Overview

How can scientists determine the precise elements inside a valuable artifact without destroying it? One powerful method is Neutron Activation Analysis. The process involves two main steps: first, the sample is safely bombarded with neutrons, which "activates" some of the atoms inside, turning them into unstable radioactive isotopes. Second, the sample is moved to a detector that listens for the unique "fingerprint" of radiation emitted as these unstable atoms decay. Each element emits gamma rays with a characteristic set of energies, allowing scientists to identify the original composition with extreme precision. This simulation walks you through this two-step process, from activation to detection and identification.

Beginner's Guide: Making Atoms Talk

Neutron Activation Analysis is a clever way to force atoms to reveal their identity. It's a three-part story: activate, decay, and detect.

Step 1: Activation

First, we need to make the atoms in our sample "active." We do this by placing the sample in a beam of neutrons. Most atoms in the sample won't be affected, but occasionally an atom will absorb a neutron into its nucleus. This doesn't change what element it is (since the number of protons is the same), but it creates a new, heavier **isotope** of that element. This new isotope is often radioactive.

Click the **"Place Sample in Beam"** button to see this. An animation will show the sample being irradiated, with a nucleus absorbing a neutron and becoming "activated" (it will start to glow).

Step 2: Decay and Detection

The newly created radioactive isotope is unstable and wants to get back to a more stable state. To do this, it undergoes radioactive decay, releasing its excess energy. This energy is often emitted in the form of **gamma rays**, which are very high-energy photons.

Crucially, each type of radioactive isotope emits gamma rays at a very specific, well-known set of energies. This set of energies is like a unique fingerprint for that isotope. After activating the sample, click **"Move to Detector"**. The sample is moved to a gamma-ray detector, which measures the energy of every gamma ray that hits it.

Step 3: The Spectrum and Identification

The detector builds up a **gamma-ray spectrum**, which is a graph of how many gamma rays were detected at each energy. You will see sharp peaks appear on the graph at the characteristic energies of the isotopes in the sample. By looking up which isotopes emit gamma rays at these energies, we can identify what was in the original sample.
After the spectrum is generated, click **"Identify Peaks"**. The simulation will label the peaks on the graph, revealing the elemental composition of the sample you chose.
