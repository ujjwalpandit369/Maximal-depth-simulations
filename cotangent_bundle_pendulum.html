The Cotangent Bundle: Phase Space as a Manifold

Overview

In advanced physics, the state of a system is described not just by its position, but by its position and momentum together. This combined space is called "phase space." This simulation demystifies the modern geometric view of phase space, which is described as the cotangent bundle of the system's configuration manifold. We use a simple pendulum as our example. Its configuration space is a circle, and we show how its phase space naturally becomes a cylinder. By adjusting the pendulum's energy, you can see how its real-world motion (swinging vs. rotating) creates different kinds of trajectories on this cylindrical phase space manifold.

Beginner's Guide to Phase Space

Step 1: Configuration Space - Where the Pendulum Can Be

The panel on the left shows a simple pendulum. Its position can be described by a single number: its angle. Since the angle wraps around, the space of all possible positions—its "configuration space"—is a circle. Mathematicians call this `S¹`.

Step 2: Phase Space - Position AND Momentum

To fully describe the pendulum's state at any instant, we need its position (angle, θ) and its momentum (p). The space of all possible positions and momenta is called phase space. How can we visualize this?

The Cotangent Bundle Idea: Imagine taking the circle of possible positions. At each and every point (angle) on that circle, we can attach a vertical line. This line represents all the possible momenta the pendulum could have at that exact angle (it could be moving fast to the right, fast to the left, or be momentarily still). "Gluing" a line to every point on a circle creates a cylinder. This cylinder is the phase space of the pendulum.

The panel on the right shows this cylindrical phase space. The circular direction is the angle θ, and the vertical direction is the momentum p.

Step 3: Trajectories in Phase Space

As the pendulum moves, its state `(θ, p)` changes, tracing a path on the surface of the cylinder. By changing the pendulum's total energy with the slider, you can create three distinct types of trajectories:

  • Low Energy (Oscillation): The pendulum swings back and forth but doesn't go over the top. In phase space, this creates a simple, closed loop—a "librational" orbit.
  • High Energy (Rotation): The pendulum has enough energy to spin continuously in one direction. In phase space, this creates a wavy line that wraps all the way around the cylinder—a "rotational" orbit.
  • Critical Energy (The Separatrix): At the exact energy needed to just reach the top, the pendulum's motion is special. In phase space, this creates a sharp, figure-eight-like path that separates the oscillation region from the rotation region. This path is called the separatrix.
