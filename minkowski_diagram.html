Spacetime Diagram (Minkowski Diagram) Explorer

An interactive tool for exploring the geometry of spacetime.

Plain-Language Overview

A spacetime diagram is a fundamental tool in special relativity. It's a graph that plots time on one axis and space on another, allowing us to visualize the motion of objects as paths, called "worldlines," through a unified "spacetime." This interactive explorer lets you create events (points in spacetime) and draw worldlines for objects. The most important feature is the "Boost Velocity" slider, which allows you to see how the diagram—and the very concepts of space and time—transform from the perspective of a moving observer. You'll see the axes of space and time pivot and "scissor" together, visually demonstrating the core effects of relativity like the relativity of simultaneity.

Beginner's Guide to Spacetime Diagrams

1. Reading the Diagram

The diagram plots time (as ct, where c is the speed of light) on the vertical axis and one dimension of space (x) on the horizontal axis.

  • An Event is a single point on the diagram, representing something happening at a specific place and a specific time. Use the "Plot Event" tool to create them.
  • A Worldline is the path of an object through spacetime. A stationary object has a vertical worldline (its position x doesn't change as time t increases). A moving object has a tilted worldline. Use the "Draw Worldline" tool to create one.
  • The Light Cone (the yellow X) represents the path of light starting at the origin. Its lines are at 45 degrees because we plot ct vs x. Since nothing can travel faster than light, no worldline can ever have a slope flatter than the light cone.

2. The Boost Slider: Changing Your Point of View

The "Boost Velocity" slider is the most important control. It simulates changing your reference frame to one that is moving with velocity v relative to the original frame. As you move the slider, you'll see the new axes, x' and ct', appear.

Notice how they "scissor" together. This is the geometric representation of the Lorentz transformations. From the perspective of the moving frame, the x' axis is their "space at a single moment in time," and the ct' axis is their "time at a single location."

3. Visualizing Relativity

You can use this tool to see the concepts from the previous simulations in a new way:

  • Relativity of Simultaneity: Create two events with the same vertical (ct) coordinate. They are simultaneous in the stationary frame. Now, use the boost slider. You will see that the two events do *not* lie on a single line of constant t' (a line parallel to the new x' axis). They are no longer simultaneous.
  • Time Dilation: Draw a vertical worldline for a stationary clock. Its "ticks" are regular intervals along the ct axis. In the boosted frame, that worldline is now tilted. To measure the time between the same two ticks in the new frame, you have to project them onto the new ct' axis. You will find that the time interval is longer.
  • Length Contraction: This is harder to see, but it's related to how the worldlines of the front and back of a moving object are represented in the diagram.

The Geometry of Lorentz Transformations

The "scissoring" of the axes is a direct result of the Lorentz transformation equations:

x' = γ(x - βct)
ct' = γ(ct - βx)

Where β = v/c and γ = 1/√(1-β2).

  • The x' axis is defined as the set of all points where t' = 0. Plugging this into the second equation gives ct = βx. This is the equation of a line with slope β, which is exactly the tilted x' axis you see.
  • The ct' axis is defined as the set of all points where x' = 0. Plugging this into the first equation gives x = βct, or ct = x/β. This is the equation of a line with slope 1/β, which is the other tilted axis.
Notice that as v → c, β → 1, and the two axes close in on the 45-degree light cone line, which is an invariant.
