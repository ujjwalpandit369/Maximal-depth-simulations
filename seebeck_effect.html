The Seebeck Effect

An animation of a thermocouple generating voltage from heat.

Overview

Can you create electricity directly from a temperature difference? The Seebeck effect makes it possible. Discovered in 1821 by Thomas Johann Seebeck, this phenomenon is the direct conversion of heat into electrical energy at the junction of two different conductors. A device that utilizes this effect is called a thermocouple, and it forms the basis for many common digital thermometers. This simulation visualizes how charge carriers (electrons) in two different metals diffuse at different rates from a hot region to a cold region, creating a net voltage that can be measured.

How It Works: Electron Diffusion

The process behind the Seebeck effect is surprisingly simple and is based on the behavior of electrons in a metal.

  1. Hot Electrons Move More: At the hot junction of the thermocouple, the electrons in the metal have higher thermal energy. They vibrate more and move around faster than the electrons at the cold junction.
  2. Diffusion from Hot to Cold: Just like a drop of ink spreading out in water, the energetic electrons at the hot end tend to diffuse or spread out towards the cold end, where there are fewer energetic electrons. This happens in both metal wires.
  3. The Key Difference: The two metals are different (e.g., Copper and Constantan). In one metal, the electrons diffuse more readily than in the other. This means one wire will build up a larger negative charge at its cold end compared to the other wire.
  4. A Net Voltage: This difference in charge accumulation between the two wires creates a net potential difference, or voltage, across the circuit. This voltage is small (microvolts per degree Celsius), but it is stable and can be measured accurately by a voltmeter.

What to Observe in the Simulation

  • The top wire (Copper) and bottom wire (Constantan) form a circuit.
  • The red and blue blocks represent the hot and cold reservoirs.
  • The small white circles are electrons. Notice their random motion is more vigorous near the hot junction.
  • Watch the net drift of electrons from left to right. This drift is stronger in the bottom (Constantan) wire.
  • This differential drift leads to a charge imbalance, which is registered as a voltage on the voltmeter. The larger the temperature difference, the stronger the diffusion, and the higher the voltage.

The Seebeck Coefficient

The relationship between the temperature difference and the generated voltage is described by a simple formula:

V = S * (Tₕ - T꜀)
  • V is the generated voltage, measured in Volts.
  • (Tₕ - T꜀) is the temperature difference between the hot and cold junctions, in Kelvin or Celsius.
  • S is the Seebeck Coefficient for the pair of materials. It's a measure of how much voltage is produced for each degree of temperature difference. It's measured in Volts per Kelvin (V/K).

For the Copper-Constantan (Type T) thermocouple shown in this simulation, the Seebeck coefficient is approximately 41 µV/K (microvolts per Kelvin) at room temperature. This means for every 1°C of temperature difference, it generates about 41 millionths of a volt.
