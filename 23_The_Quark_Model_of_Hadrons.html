The Quark Model of Hadrons

An interactive builder to explore how quarks combine to form protons, neutrons, and other particles.

Plain-Language Overview

The protons and neutrons that make up the atoms in our bodies are not fundamental particles. They are themselves made of even smaller, more fundamental particles called **quarks**. The Quark Model describes the rules for how these quarks can combine to form the particles we observe. This interactive simulation is a "hadron builder" that lets you play with these rules. You can drag different quarks into the workspace and try to combine them. The simulation will check your combination against the rules of the Standard Model—like the strange requirement for "color charge" to be neutral—and tell you if you've successfully built a known particle like a proton, or if your combination is forbidden by the laws of physics.

Beginner's Guide: The Rules of Particle Building

Step 1: The Quarks

There are six "flavors" of quarks, but all normal matter is made from just the two lightest: the **Up** quark and the **Down** quark. Quarks have fractional electric charges. An Up quark has a charge of +2/3, while a Down quark has a charge of -1/3. The palette on the right contains these quarks and their corresponding **antiquarks** (which have the opposite charge).

Step 2: The "Color" Charge

In addition to electric charge, quarks carry a different kind of charge called **color charge**. It has nothing to do with visible colors; it's just a whimsical name for the charge associated with the Strong Nuclear Force. There are three colors: red, green, and blue. There are also three corresponding anti-colors for the antiquarks.

Step 3: The Rule of Confinement

A fundamental rule of the Strong Force is that quarks can never exist alone; they are always "confined" inside composite particles. These composite particles must be **"color neutral"** or "white." There are two known ways to make a color-neutral combination:

  1. Baryons (three quarks): By combining one red, one green, and one blue quark. The mix of the three primary "colors" is considered white. Protons and neutrons are baryons.
  2. Mesons (one quark, one antiquark): By combining a quark of a certain color with an antiquark of the corresponding anti-color (e.g., a red quark and an anti-red antiquark).
The simulation will only allow you to form valid, color-neutral groups.

Step 4: Build a Proton!

Let's try to build the most famous baryon, a proton.

  1. From the palette, drag an **Up quark (u)** with a red color dot into the workspace.
  2. Drag another **Up quark (u)**, but this time with a green color dot.
  3. Drag a **Down quark (d)** with a blue color dot.
  4. Click the **"Validate Combination"** button.
The info panel will confirm you have built a Proton! It will also calculate its charge for you: (+2/3) + (+2/3) + (-1/3) = +1. Now try building a Neutron (udd).
