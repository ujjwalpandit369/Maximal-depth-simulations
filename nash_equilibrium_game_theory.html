Guide: The Prisoner's Dilemma and Nash Equilibrium

The Story

Imagine two partners in crime are arrested and held in separate interrogation rooms. They can't communicate. The prosecutor offers each the same deal:

  • If you betray your partner (Defect) and they stay silent (Cooperate), you go free (5 points) and they get a long sentence (0 points).
  • If you both stay silent (Cooperate), you both get a short sentence (3 points each).
  • If you both betray each other (Defect), you both get a medium sentence (1 point each).
What should you do? This is the Prisoner's Dilemma.

Rational Choice and Nash Equilibrium

Let's think about it from your perspective, without knowing what your partner will do:

  • Case 1: Your partner Cooperates. Your best move is to Defect (you get 5 points instead of 3).
  • Case 2: Your partner Defects. Your best move is still to Defect (you get 1 point instead of 0).
In both cases, your "rational" self-interested choice is to Defect. Since your partner is also rational, they will reason the same way. The inevitable result is that you both Defect and end up with a measly 1 point each.

This outcome, (Defect, Defect), is the Nash Equilibrium. It's a state where no player can improve their outcome by *unilaterally* changing their choice. If you are both defecting, and you decide to switch to Cooperate, your outcome gets worse (from 1 to 0). The paradox is that this "stable" equilibrium is worse for both of you than if you had both cooperated.

The Iterated Game: The Emergence of Cooperation

What if you play the game repeatedly with the same opponent? Things change. Now, your actions have consequences for future rounds. This is where strategies like Tit-for-Tat shine.

  • Tit-for-Tat: This strategy is simple: 1. Cooperate on the first round. 2. On every subsequent round, do whatever your opponent did on the *previous* round.
Tit-for-Tat is considered a "nice" strategy because it starts by cooperating, "retaliatory" because it immediately punishes defection, and "forgiving" because it will return to cooperation as soon as the opponent does. Try playing against the Tit-for-Tat AI. You'll find that mutual cooperation is often the most profitable long-term strategy.
