1D Collisions: Elastic vs. Inelastic

Exploring conservation of momentum and kinetic energy in one-dimensional collisions.

Plain-Language Overview

When two objects collide, what happens? The outcome depends on two fundamental principles: conservation of momentum and conservation of energy. This interactive simulation lets you crash two objects together on a one-dimensional track to see how these principles work. You can control the mass and initial velocity of each object, as well as the "elasticity" of the collision—from a perfectly "bouncy" elastic collision to a "sticky" inelastic one. By observing the objects' motion and the detailed data tables, you will discover that total momentum is *always* conserved, but kinetic energy is only conserved in perfectly elastic collisions. This is a key concept for understanding everything from billiard ball physics to car crashes.

Beginner's Guide: The Physics of Crashing

Step 1: Two Conservation Laws

To understand collisions, we need two key concepts:

  • Momentum (p): A measure of an object's "mass in motion," calculated as p = mass × velocity. It is a vector, meaning it has a direction. In a closed system, the **total momentum is always conserved**—the total amount before the collision is the same as the total amount after.
  • Kinetic Energy (KE): The energy of motion, calculated as KE = ½ × mass × velocity². Unlike momentum, kinetic energy is **not always conserved**. In most real-world collisions, some of it is converted into other forms, like heat and sound.

Step 2: Elastic vs. Inelastic Collisions

The "Coefficient of Restitution" (e) slider lets you control the "bounciness" of the collision.

  • Perfectly Elastic (e=1): A perfectly bouncy collision, like two ideal billiard balls hitting. In this special case, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.
  • Perfectly Inelastic (e=0): A perfectly "sticky" collision. The two objects stick together after they collide and move as a single mass. In this case, momentum is still conserved, but the maximum possible amount of kinetic energy is lost.
  • Inelastic (0 < e < 1): This represents all real-world collisions. The objects bounce off each other, but not perfectly. Some kinetic energy is lost.

Step 3: Running Experiments

Use the sliders to set up different scenarios and observe the results in the data tables.

  • Experiment 1 (Elastic): Set e=1. Make both masses equal and give Object 2 a velocity of 0. Fire Object 1 at it. You'll see they perfectly trade velocities! Check the tables to confirm that both total momentum and total KE are conserved.
  • Experiment 2 (Inelastic): Set e=0. Use the same masses and velocities. Now when they collide, they stick together. Look at the tables: total momentum is the same before and after, but the total kinetic energy has decreased significantly.
  • Experiment 3 (The "Big Guy"): Make Object 1 very heavy and Object 2 very light. Fire the heavy object at the light, stationary one. The heavy object barely slows down, while the light one shoots off at high speed.

Core Theory: Solving the Collision

To find the final velocities (v₁' and v₂'), we must solve a system of two equations with two unknowns.

1. Conservation of Momentum

The total momentum before the collision must equal the total momentum after.

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'

2. Coefficient of Restitution (e)

This equation relates the relative speed of separation to the relative speed of approach.

e = (v₂' - v₁') / (v₁ - v₂)

Rearranging this gives a second equation: e(v₁ - v₂) = v₂' - v₁'

3. The Solution

By solving these two equations simultaneously for v₁' and v₂', we get the final velocities, which the simulation uses to calculate the outcome of the collision instantly:

v₁' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ - m₂e(v₁ - v₂)) / (m₁ + m₂)

v₂' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ + m₁e(v₁ - v₂)) / (m₁ + m₂)
