Plain-Language Overview

The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) is the oldest light in the universe, a faint afterglow from the Big Bang that fills all of space. By studying this light, we can learn immense amounts about the early universe. One intriguing question is whether the universe was born with a large-scale magnetic field. While we see magnetic fields in galaxies today, we don't know if they are primordial or formed later. This simulation shows how such an ancient magnetic field would leave a subtle, smoking-gun signature in the polarization of the CMB light—a specific type of swirling pattern known as a "B-mode." You can turn a hypothetical magnetic field on and off to see how it twists the fabric of the ancient light.

Beginner's Guide — A Gentle Introduction

Step 1: The Baby Picture of the Universe

The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) is often called the "baby picture of the universe." It's a snapshot of what the universe looked like when it was only 380,000 years old (today it's 13.8 billion years old!). Back then, it wasn't transparent. It was a hot, dense soup of charged particles (a plasma) and light. As the universe expanded and cooled, protons and electrons finally combined to form neutral hydrogen atoms. At this moment, the light was set free to travel across the cosmos, and we can still see it today as a faint microwave glow in all directions.

Step 2: Polarized Light - More Than Just Brightness

Light is a wave, and its waves can vibrate in different directions. Most light sources, like a lightbulb, are "unpolarized," meaning the waves vibrate in all directions randomly. Polarized light has a preferred direction of vibration. Think of shaking a rope: you can shake it up-and-down, or side-to-side. Polarizing sunglasses work by blocking light with a certain polarization (the kind that reflects off roads as glare). The light from the CMB is slightly polarized, and this polarization contains a treasure trove of extra information.

Step 3: E-Modes vs. B-Modes - Ripples and Whirlpools

Any pattern of polarization on the sky can be mathematically separated into two distinct types of patterns, called "E-modes" and "B-modes."

  • E-Modes: These patterns are "gradient-like." They either point away from a center or form concentric circles around it. They have no "curl" or "swirl."
  • B-Modes: These patterns are "curl-like." They have a characteristic swirling, vortex pattern, like cream stirred into coffee.
Analogy: Water in a Pond. Imagine the polarization pattern is like the motion of water on a pond's surface. If you drop a pebble in, the ripples move straight out from the center. This is like an E-mode. If you stir the water with a stick to create a whirlpool, the swirling vortex is like a B-mode.

This distinction is critically important because standard physics of the early universe (like density fluctuations) creates E-modes almost exclusively. Finding B-modes means you've found something exotic, like a whirlpool in your pond.

Step 4: The Magnetic Field's Smoking Gun

A primordial magnetic field would have been woven into the hot plasma of the early universe. Through complex effects like Faraday rotation, this magnetic field would have "stirred" the plasma, generating a distinctive, swirling B-mode pattern in the polarization of the light as it was released. The standard model of cosmology does not produce such a signal. Therefore, detecting this specific kind of B-mode would be a smoking gun for primordial magnetism. This simulation lets you be the astronomer: you can add a magnetic field and see the B-mode whirlpools appear in the CMB data.

Describing Polarization

The polarization of light at a certain point is described by two numbers, the Stokes parameters Q and U. These describe the strength of the polarization along the horizontal/vertical axes (Q) and along the 45-degree diagonal axes (U). From Q and U, we can define a polarization "vector" with a magnitude and an angle. The glyphs on the map represent this vector.

From Q/U to E/B

E-modes and B-modes are derived from the spatial patterns of the Q and U fields across the sky. In Fourier space (where we analyze patterns by their wavelength), the decomposition is clean. A Fourier mode `(Q̃, Ũ)` at a certain wavevector `k` can be uniquely split into an E-mode amplitude and a B-mode amplitude. In real space, this corresponds to:

  • E-mode: A pattern that can be described as the gradient of some scalar potential field, `P_E = ∇Φ`. This means it has no curl (`∇ × P_E = 0`).
  • B-mode: A pattern that can be described as the curl of some vector potential, `P_B = ∇ × Ψ`. This means it has no divergence (`∇ ⋅ P_B = 0`).
Our simulation generates a random E-mode field and a random B-mode field using this principle, and then adds them together based on the magnetic field strength slider: `P_total = P_E + B_strength ⋅ P_B`.

Power Spectra

Cosmologists analyze the CMB by computing the "power spectrum," which shows how much fluctuation there is at different angular scales (or wavelengths) on the sky. They compute spectra for Temperature (TT), E-mode polarization (EE), and B-mode polarization (BB). A primordial magnetic field would add a characteristic bump to the BB power spectrum at specific angular scales, which is what experiments are designed to look for.

The Hunt for B-Modes

Detecting primordial B-modes is one of the biggest challenges in modern cosmology. There are two main theorized sources:

  1. Inflation: A period of exponential expansion in the first fraction of a second, which would have produced gravitational waves. These waves would also generate a B-mode pattern. This is often considered the "holy grail" of CMB research.
  2. Primordial Magnetic Fields: As explored in this simulation.
The main difficulty is that these primordial signals are expected to be incredibly faint.

Galactic Foregrounds

The biggest challenge is contamination from our own Milky Way galaxy. Dust within our galaxy is aligned by the galactic magnetic field, and when this dust glows, it produces polarized light. This "foreground" emission has a strong B-mode component that can easily drown out a faint primordial signal. Cosmologists use sophisticated techniques, like observing the sky at many different frequencies (since the foreground and CMB signals have different color spectra), to try and clean this contamination from their maps. So far, all detected B-modes have been consistent with being from galactic dust, placing very tight limits on the possible strength of any primordial magnetic field.

Current Experiments

A host of ground-based experiments are dedicated to this search, including the BICEP/Keck Array at the South Pole, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT), the South Pole Telescope (SPT), and upcoming, more powerful observatories like the Simons Observatory and CMB-S4.
