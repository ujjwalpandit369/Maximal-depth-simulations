Gravitational Time Dilation

Why clocks run slower in stronger gravity.

Plain-Language Overview

General Relativity predicts that gravity warps time itself. Clocks deeper inside a "gravity well" (closer to a massive object) tick more slowly than clocks farther away. This is a real, measurable effect essential for technologies like GPS. This simulation shows two clocks: one at the base of a tower on a massive planet, and one at the top. As time passes, you will see the higher clock pull ahead of the lower clock.

Beginner's Guide

1. Gravity as Curved Spacetime

General Relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime. The more massive an object, the more it curves spacetime, creating a "gravity well." The grid in the simulation visualizes this curvature.

2. Observing the Simulation

You can see two clocks: Clock A at the bottom and Clock B at the top. As the simulation runs, Clock B, being in a weaker gravitational field, will run faster than Clock A. Increasing the Planet Mass or Tower Height will make this difference more extreme.

Real-World Application: GPS Satellites

This isn't just a theory. GPS satellites orbit in a weaker gravitational field than we are on the surface, making their clocks run faster by about 38 microseconds per day. If this relativistic effect were not corrected, GPS navigation would accumulate errors of about 10 kilometers every single day!

Core Theory

The rate at which time passes (proper time, dτ) for a stationary clock at a distance `r` from a mass `M`, relative to a distant clock (`dt`), is given by the Schwarzschild metric: dτ = √(1 - 2GM/rc²) dt. The term 2GM/c² is the Schwarzschild radius (Rₛ). Since (1 - Rₛ/r) is always less than 1, proper time dτ in the gravitational field is always less than the time dt far away. The closer you get to the mass (smaller `r`), the slower time passes.
