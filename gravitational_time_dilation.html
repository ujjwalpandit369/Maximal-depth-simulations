Gravitational Time Dilation

Why clocks run slower in stronger gravity.

Plain-Language Overview

One of the most mind-bending predictions of Einstein's General Relativity is that gravity warps not just space, but time itself. Clocks that are deeper inside a "gravity well" (i.e., closer to a massive object) will tick more slowly than clocks that are farther away. This isn't a theory; it's a real and measurable effect that is essential for technologies like GPS to function. This simulation shows two clocks: one at the base of a very tall tower on a massive planet, and one at the top. As the clocks run, you will see the time on the higher clock literally pull ahead of the time on the lower clock, demonstrating gravitational time dilation in action.

Beginner's Guide

1. Gravity as Curved Spacetime

As we saw in previous simulations, General Relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. The more massive an object, the more it curves the spacetime around it, creating what we call a "gravity well." The background of the simulation shows a visualization of this concept. The planet sits in a dip it has created in the fabric of spacetime.

2. Time is Part of Spacetime

The crucial insight is that time is not separate from space. They form a single, four-dimensional continuum. When gravity curves spacetime, it curves the time dimension as well as the spatial dimensions. For an observer far away from any gravitational influence, a clock might tick by one second. But for a clock deep inside a gravity well, that same "amount" of spacetime progression will be experienced as less than one second of its local, "proper" time. In essence, a portion of its journey through spacetime is "used up" by moving through curved space, leaving less for moving through time.

3. Observing the Simulation

In the simulation, you can see two clocks.

  • Clock A (Bottom): This clock is at the base of the tower, deeper in the planet's gravity well.
  • Clock B (Top): This clock is at the top of the tower, farther away from the planet and in a weaker gravitational field.
As the simulation runs, you will notice the numbers on Clock B's display increasing faster than the numbers on Clock A's display. This is a direct visualization of gravitational time dilation.

Use the sliders to see how the effect changes:

  • Increasing the Planet Mass will make the gravity well deeper, causing the difference in the clock rates to become more extreme.
  • Increasing the Tower Height will increase the separation between the clocks, also increasing the difference in their rates.

Real-World Application: GPS Satellites

Gravitational time dilation is not just a theoretical curiosity; it's a daily engineering problem. The Global Positioning System (GPS) relies on a network of satellites orbiting the Earth, each with a highly precise atomic clock. These satellites are both moving very fast (which causes special relativistic time dilation, making their clocks run slow) and are in a weaker gravitational field than we are on the surface (which causes gravitational time dilation, making their clocks run fast).

The effect of gravity is stronger than the effect of their speed. GPS satellite clocks run faster than ground clocks by about 38 microseconds per day. This may not sound like much, but GPS works by measuring the time it takes for signals to travel from satellites to a receiver. If this relativistic effect were not accounted for, GPS navigation would accumulate errors of about 10 kilometers every single day, making the system completely useless! Engineers must build the corrections for both special and general relativity directly into the system for it to work.

The Schwarzschild Metric

For a simple, non-rotating, uncharged massive object, the curvature of spacetime is described by the Schwarzschild metric. A key part of this metric describes the rate at which time passes (the proper time, dτ) for a stationary clock at a distance r from the center of a mass M, relative to a clock infinitely far away (where time passes at rate dt).

dτ = √(1 - 2GM/rc2) dt

Where G is the gravitational constant and c is the speed of light. The term 2GM/c² is the Schwarzschild radius (Rs). Since (1 - Rs/r) is always less than 1, this equation shows that proper time dτ for the clock in the gravitational field is always less than the coordinate time dt of the distant observer. The closer you get to the mass (smaller `r`), the smaller the term in the square root, and the slower time passes.
