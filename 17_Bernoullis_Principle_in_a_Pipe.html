Plain-Language Overview

Why does an airplane wing generate lift? How does a curveball curve? The answer to both involves Bernoulli's Principle, a fundamental concept in fluid dynamics. This principle states that for a moving fluid, there is an inverse relationship between its speed and its pressure: where the fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower. This simulation provides a clear demonstration of this idea. You can see a fluid flowing through a pipe that narrows in the middle. To get through the narrow section, the fluid must speed up. As it does, you can see from the pressure gauges (manometers) that its pressure drops, only to rise again when the pipe widens and the fluid slows down.

Beginner's Guide: The Garden Hose Effect

Step 1: The Continuity Equation

Imagine water flowing through a pipe. The amount of water passing any point per second must be constant. If the pipe suddenly gets narrower, the water has to speed up to get the same volume through that smaller opening in the same amount of time. This is the "garden hose effect": when you put your thumb over the end of a hose, you make the opening smaller, and the water shoots out faster. This principle is called the **Continuity Equation**. In the simulation, you can see particles representing the fluid speed up in the narrow central section.

Step 2: Bernoulli's Principle

Daniel Bernoulli discovered in the 18th century that a fluid's speed and pressure are related. His principle states that, for a horizontal pipe, **where the speed is high, the pressure is low**, and where the speed is low, the pressure is high. This can seem counterintuitive, but it's a direct consequence of the conservation of energy for the fluid.

Step 3: Visualizing Pressure

The simulation has three vertical tubes, called **manometers**, sticking out of the top of the pipe. These are simple pressure gauges. The pressure of the fluid inside the pipe pushes the fluid up into the tube. A higher fluid level in the manometer means higher pressure inside the pipe at that point.

Step 4: Putting It All Together

Now watch the whole system in the simulation:

  1. In the first wide section, the fluid moves at a low velocity, and the pressure is high (the manometer level is high).
  2. As the fluid enters the narrow section, the Continuity Equation forces it to speed up.
  3. Because the fluid has sped up, Bernoulli's Principle tells us its pressure must drop. You can see this directly: the fluid level in the central manometer is much lower.
  4. As the fluid enters the final wide section, it slows down again, and its pressure rises back to the initial high value.
You can use the sliders to change the pipe's radii and the inlet velocity to see how these changes affect the speeds and pressures throughout the system.

Core Theory: Continuity and Bernoulli's Equations

1. Continuity Equation

For an incompressible fluid, the product of the cross-sectional area (A) and the fluid velocity (v) is constant at all points along a pipe.

A₁v₁ = A₂v₂

Since the area of a circular pipe is A = πr², we can find the velocity in the constricted section (v₂) from the velocity in the wide section (v₁):

v₂ = v₁ * (A₁/A₂) = v₁ * (r₁/r₂)²

2. Bernoulli's Equation

Bernoulli's equation is a statement of the conservation of energy for a moving fluid. For a horizontal pipe (where the potential energy due to height does not change), it is written as:

P + ½ρv² = constant

Where P is the pressure, ρ (rho) is the fluid density (we assume 1000 kg/m³ for water), and v is the fluid velocity. This means the sum of the static pressure and the dynamic pressure is constant. By applying this between section 1 and section 2, we get:

P₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ½ρv₂²

We can rearrange this to solve for the pressure in the constricted section (P₂):

P₂ = P₁ + ½ρ(v₁² - v₂²)

Since v₂ > v₁, the term in the parenthesis is negative, which correctly shows that P₂ must be less than P₁.
