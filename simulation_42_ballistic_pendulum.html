The Ballistic Pendulum

Using conservation laws to measure the speed of a bullet.

Estimated Runtime: Interactive | Recommended Device: Desktop | Approx. HTML Size: 150 KB

Plain-Language Overview

How can you measure the speed of a bullet using only a block of wood, some string, and a ruler? The ballistic pendulum is a classic physics experiment that does just that. It's a beautiful demonstration of two different fundamental conservation laws working in sequence. First, a bullet embeds itself in a suspended block in a collision where **momentum is conserved**. Second, the block and bullet swing upwards like a pendulum in a process where **mechanical energy is conserved**. By measuring the final height of the swing, you can work backwards to find the bullet's initial speed.

Beginner's Guide — A Two-Part Problem

The key to understanding the ballistic pendulum is to break the process down into its two distinct parts.

Part 1: The Collision (Conserve Momentum!)

The first event is the bullet striking and embedding itself in the block. This is a **perfectly inelastic collision**.

  • What's Conserved? In any collision, the total momentum of the system is conserved (as long as there are no external forces). Momentum is `mass × velocity`.
  • What's NOT Conserved? Kinetic energy is *not* conserved in an inelastic collision. A huge amount of energy is lost to heat, sound, and the work done to splinter the wood and deform the bullet.
So, we can use the law of conservation of momentum to find the velocity of the combined block-and-bullet system immediately after the collision.

Part 2: The Swing (Conserve Energy!)

The second event is the block-and-bullet system swinging upwards.

  • What's Conserved? During this swing, we can assume that mechanical energy is conserved (ignoring air resistance). The kinetic energy the block has just after the collision is converted into gravitational potential energy as it rises.
  • What's NOT Conserved? Momentum is *not* conserved during the swing, because there is now an external force on the system (the tension in the strings).
We use the law of conservation of energy to relate the velocity after the collision to the maximum height `h` that the pendulum reaches.

Working Backwards

By running the simulation, you are doing what a physicist would do in the lab:

  1. You observe the final height `h` of the swing.
  2. From `h`, you can calculate the kinetic energy the block had just after the collision.
  3. From that kinetic energy, you can find the velocity `V` it had just after the collision.
  4. Using that velocity `V` and the conservation of momentum from the collision, you can calculate the original, unknown speed of the bullet.

Part 1: Inelastic Collision

We apply conservation of momentum. Let `m_b` and `v_b` be the bullet's mass and velocity, and `M_B` be the block's mass. The final velocity of the combined system is `V_f`.

Momentum_initial = Momentum_final

m_b * v_b + M_B * 0 = (m_b + M_B) * V_f

Solving for the velocity just after the collision:

V_f = (m_b * v_b) / (m_b + M_B)

Part 2: Energy Conservation in the Swing

We apply conservation of mechanical energy. The kinetic energy just after the collision is converted into gravitational potential energy at the peak of the swing.

KE_initial = PE_final

(1/2) * (m_b + M_B) * V_f² = (m_b + M_B) * g * h

The `(m_b + M_B)` terms cancel, and we can solve for the maximum height `h`:

h = V_f² / (2g)

Example: Using default values: `m_b = 0.01 kg`, `v_b = 400 m/s`, `M_B = 5 kg`, `g = 9.8 m/s²`.
1. Find `V_f`: `V_f = (0.01 * 400) / (0.01 + 5) = 4 / 5.01 ≈ 0.798 m/s`.
2. Find `h`: `h = (0.798)² / (2 * 9.8) = 0.637 / 19.6 ≈ 0.0325 m` (or 3.25 cm).
