The Standard Model of Particle Physics

An interactive chart of the fundamental building blocks of the universe.

Plain-Language Overview

Everything in the universe, from the stars to ourselves, is made from a small number of fundamental building blocks called elementary particles. The "Standard Model" is our current best theory describing these particles and the forces through which they interact. It's like the periodic table, but for the most fundamental level of reality we know. This interactive chart lets you explore this "particle zoo." You can click on any particle—like the familiar electron, the exotic top quark, or the famous Higgs boson—to learn about its properties, such as its mass, charge, and how it fits into the grand cosmic puzzle.

Beginner's Guide: The Universe's LEGO Set

Step 1: Two Kinds of Particles

The Standard Model organizes particles into two main families, which you can see in the chart:

  • Fermions (The Bricks): These are the particles that make up all the matter we see. They are like the LEGO bricks of the universe. Fermions are divided into two groups: **Quarks** and **Leptons**.
  • Bosons (The Forces/Rules): These particles are responsible for the fundamental forces of nature. They are like the rules that describe how the LEGO bricks can stick together. They include the **Gauge Bosons** (force carriers) and the **Scalar Boson** (the Higgs boson).

Step 2: Fermions - The Matter Particles

Click on the different fermion tiles to explore them.

  • Quarks: These particles feel the strong nuclear force and are always "confined" inside larger composite particles like protons and neutrons. There are six "flavors" of quarks: Up, Down, Charm, Strange, Top, and Bottom.
  • Leptons: These particles do not feel the strong nuclear force. The most famous lepton is the **Electron**. The other charged leptons are the Muon and the Tau, which are like heavier, unstable cousins of the electron. There are also three **Neutrinos**, which are extremely light, have no electric charge, and barely interact with other matter at all.
Notice that the fermions are arranged in three columns, called **generations**. All the matter we see around us is made of just the first generation (Up quarks, Down quarks, and Electrons). The second and third generation particles are much heavier and unstable, and are only seen in high-energy environments like particle accelerators.

Step 3: Bosons - The Force Carriers

The forces of nature work by exchanging force-carrying particles.

  • The **Photon** carries the electromagnetic force, which governs light, electricity, and magnetism.
  • The **Gluon** carries the strong nuclear force, which "glues" quarks together inside protons and neutrons.
  • The **W and Z Bosons** carry the weak nuclear force, which is responsible for certain types of radioactive decay.
The chart also includes the famous **Higgs Boson**. The Higgs is special; it's not a force carrier. It's an excitation of the "Higgs field," which permeates all of space and is responsible for giving the fundamental particles their mass.

Step 4: Using the Chart

Click on any particle to see its properties in the info panel below. Notice what happens when you click on a particle: others of the same type (quark, lepton, etc.) are highlighted. This helps you see the family relationships within the Standard Model. This chart is a map of our deepest understanding of the physical world.
