Nuclear Isomers and Gamma Decay

How an excited nucleus relaxes by emitting a high-energy photon.

Plain-Language Overview

Just like an atom can have its electrons in an "excited" energy state, an atomic nucleus can also be in an excited state, often after a previous radioactive decay or a nuclear reaction. This excited nucleus is unstable and will eventually relax to its stable "ground state." To do so, it must release its excess energy. It does this by emitting a very high-energy particle of light called a **gamma ray (γ)**. This process is called gamma decay. This simulation visualizes this transition, showing the nucleus relaxing while a gamma ray is emitted, and the corresponding drop on an energy level diagram.

Beginner's Guide — The Nucleus Calming Down

Step 1: The Excited Nucleus

A nucleus can be left in an excited state, which we denote with an asterisk (e.g., Technetium-99m, or `⁹⁹ᵐTc*`), for many reasons. Most commonly, it is the product of a previous alpha or beta decay. This excited state has excess energy, similar to a compressed spring or a vibrating bell. In the simulation, we represent this as a visibly "agitated" nucleus.

Step 2: The Decay to Ground State

A system in nature always seeks its lowest possible energy state. For the excited nucleus, this is the "ground state." To get there, it must shed its excess energy. Unlike alpha or beta decay, this process does not change the identity of the nucleus—the number of protons and neutrons remains the same. The nucleus simply calms down.

Step 3: The Gamma Ray

The energy is released in the form of a single, high-energy photon. A photon is a particle of light, and photons in this high-energy range are called **gamma rays**. The energy of the emitted gamma ray is precisely equal to the energy difference between the excited state and the ground state. Because nuclear energy levels are separated by a large amount of energy (typically thousands or millions of times more than atomic electron levels), gamma rays are extremely energetic.

Step 4: Nuclear Isomers

Most excited states decay almost instantly (in less than a trillionth of a second). However, some excited states are "metastable," meaning they can exist for a much longer time—seconds, minutes, hours, or even years! These long-lived excited nuclei are called **nuclear isomers**. They are important in many applications, particularly in nuclear medicine. Technetium-99m, for example, is an isomer with a half-life of 6 hours, making it ideal for medical imaging procedures.

The Decay Equation

Gamma decay is represented by a simple equation. Let `X` be a nucleus with mass number `A` and atomic number `Z`. An excited state is denoted by an asterisk `*`.

ᴬ_Z X* → ᴬ_Z X + γ

Notice that `A` and `Z` do not change. The nucleus does not transmute into a different element. It only loses energy.

Energy of the Emitted Photon

The energy levels within a nucleus are quantized, meaning they have specific, discrete values. Let the energy of the initial excited state be `E_initial` and the energy of the final ground state be `E_final`. The energy of the emitted gamma ray photon, `E_γ`, is exactly equal to the difference between these two levels.

E_γ = E_initial - E_final = ΔE

Because the energy levels are precisely defined for each isotope, the gamma rays emitted by a substance have a characteristic, discrete energy spectrum. This allows scientists to perform "gamma-ray spectroscopy" to identify the exact isotopes present in a sample.
