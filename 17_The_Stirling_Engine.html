Plain-Language Overview

Unlike the familiar internal combustion engine in a car, a Stirling engine is an "external" combustion engine. This means the heat source is outside the engine, which allows it to run on almost any form of heat—from burning fuel to concentrated sunlight. It works by cyclically heating and cooling a fixed amount of gas, shuttling it between a hot end and a cold end to drive a piston. This simulation shows the clever mechanics of a Stirling engine, including its two pistons and a crucial component called a "regenerator" that dramatically improves its efficiency. By watching the physical motion on the left and the corresponding Pressure-Volume diagram on the right, you can understand how this ingenious engine converts heat into work.

Beginner's Guide: The Engine That Can Run on Sunshine

Step 1: The Components

The simulation shows a common type of Stirling engine. Let's identify the key parts in the left-hand diagram:

  • Working Gas: The colored gas trapped inside the engine.
  • Hot End & Cold End: The engine is heated on one side (red) and cooled on the other (blue).
  • Displacer Piston (Gray): This is the large piston inside the main cylinder. Its job is not to produce power, but to shuttle the working gas back and forth between the hot and cold ends. It is loose-fitting, so gas can flow around it.
  • Power Piston (Dark Gray): This is the piston in the smaller cylinder. It moves up and down to compress the gas or be pushed by the expanding gas. This is where the useful work is done.
  • Regenerator (Mesh): This is a porous material (like a metal sponge) that sits between the hot and cold ends. Its job is to temporarily store heat, and it is the secret to the Stirling engine's high efficiency.

Step 2: The Four-Stage Cycle

The engine operates in a continuous cycle with four overlapping stages. Let's start with the gas at its most compressed state (both pistons are "up").

  1. Heating & Expansion: The displacer moves down, shuttling most of the gas to the hot end. The gas heats up rapidly, its pressure increases, and it expands, pushing the power piston down. This is the power stroke.
  2. Transfer to Cold End: The displacer moves up, pushing the hot gas through the regenerator to the cold end. As the gas passes through the regenerator, it gives up much of its heat, which is stored in the regenerator material. The gas arrives at the cold end, having cooled down significantly, which causes the pressure to drop.
  3. Cooling & Compression: With most of the gas now in the cold end, the power piston moves up, easily compressing the low-pressure gas. The remaining heat is expelled to the environment through the cold end.
  4. Transfer to Hot End: The displacer moves down again, pushing the cool gas back through the regenerator. The regenerator, still hot from step 2, gives its stored heat back to the gas. The gas arrives at the hot end already pre-heated, ready to start the power stroke again.

Step 3: The Magic of the Regenerator

The regenerator is the key. In an ideal engine, the heat stored in the regenerator in step 2 is exactly the amount needed to pre-heat the gas in step 4. This means the only external heat the engine needs is during the power stroke (step 1), and the only waste heat is during the compression stroke (step 3). This "recycling" of heat is what makes the Stirling engine so efficient in theory. Use the "Regenerator Efficiency" slider to see its effect. At 0%, the engine is very inefficient. At 100%, its efficiency approaches the theoretical maximum (the Carnot efficiency).

Step 4: The P-V Diagram

The diagram on the right plots the pressure of the gas versus the total volume inside the engine. The cycle traces a rounded, tilted-oval shape. The area inside this loop represents the net work done by the engine in one cycle. Notice how changing the temperatures or regenerator efficiency changes the shape and area of this loop, and thus the power output of the engine.

Core Theory: The Ideal Stirling Cycle

The ideal Stirling cycle consists of four distinct processes acting on a fixed amount of gas:

  1. Isothermal Expansion: The gas expands from V1 to V2 at a constant high temperature TH. Heat QH is absorbed from the hot source.
  2. Isochoric Cooling: The gas is cooled from TH to TC at a constant volume V2. Ideally, the heat Qregen is stored in the regenerator.
  3. Isothermal Compression: The gas is compressed from V2 to V1 at a constant low temperature TC. Waste heat QC is expelled.
  4. Isochoric Heating: The gas is heated from TC to TH at a constant volume V1. Ideally, the heat Qregen is recovered from the regenerator.

Net Work: The work done during the constant volume steps is zero. The net work is the sum of the work from the isothermal steps:

Wnet = nR TH ln(V2/V1) + nR TC ln(V1/V2) = nR(TH - TC)ln(V2/V1)

Efficiency: The total heat input from the external source is QH plus any heat not recovered by the regenerator. With a perfect regenerator, the only heat input is QH.

η = Wnet / QH = (nR(TH - TC)ln(V2/V1)) / (nR TH ln(V2/V1)) = 1 - TC/TH

This shows that the ideal Stirling engine with perfect regeneration has the same efficiency as a Carnot engine, the maximum possible.
