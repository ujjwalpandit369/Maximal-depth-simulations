The Refrigerator Cycle (Vapor-Compression)

How to use physics to move heat from a cold place to a hot place.

Plain-Language Overview

A refrigerator seems to defy a basic law of nature: heat is supposed to flow from hot to cold, but a fridge makes its inside cold while warming up the room outside. It's a "heat pump," a machine that uses energy to move heat against its natural direction of flow. This simulation illustrates the clever four-step process, called the vapor-compression cycle, that makes this possible. It relies on a special fluid (a refrigerant) that can be forced to boil at very low temperatures (absorbing heat) and condense at high temperatures (releasing heat), all by changing its pressure.

Beginner's Guide — The Four-Step Cycle

The secret to refrigeration is changing the boiling point of a fluid. Water boils at 100°C, but if you lower the pressure (like on a mountaintop), it boils at a lower temperature. Refrigerators use a special fluid and a pump to manipulate this effect.

Step 1: The Evaporator (Inside the Fridge)

A cold, low-pressure liquid refrigerant flows into a network of coils inside the refrigerator. Because the pressure is so low, its boiling point is also very low (e.g., -30°C). Even in a cold fridge, it's warm enough to make the refrigerant boil and turn into a gas. To do this, it must absorb a large amount of "latent heat" from the food and air inside the fridge, making them colder. This is the cooling step.

Step 2: The Compressor

The now gaseous, low-pressure refrigerant is sucked out of the evaporator and into the compressor. The compressor is a powerful pump that does work on the gas, squeezing it into a much smaller volume. This compression dramatically increases the gas's pressure and, as a result, its temperature. It is now a very hot, high-pressure gas.

Step 3: The Condenser (Outside the Fridge)

This hot, high-pressure gas is pushed into a set of coils on the back or bottom of the refrigerator. These coils are exposed to the air in your kitchen. Since the gas is much hotter than the room, heat flows from the gas to the air. As the gas cools, it condenses back into a high-pressure liquid, releasing its latent heat into the room. This is the heat rejection step. (This is why the back of your fridge feels warm).

Step 4: The Expansion Valve

The now high-pressure liquid refrigerant is pushed through a very narrow nozzle called an expansion valve. As it passes through, its pressure plummets. This sudden drop in pressure causes an equally dramatic drop in temperature (a phenomenon called the Joule-Thomson effect). The fluid is now a very cold, low-pressure liquid, ready to flow back into the evaporator and start the cycle all over again.

Analogy: The "Heat Sponge"

Think of the refrigerant as a magical, reusable "heat sponge."

  1. Inside the fridge: The sponge is cold and "dry." It soaks up heat from its surroundings (cooling the fridge) and becomes "wet" with heat (it evaporates).
  2. To the compressor: The "wet" sponge is carried to a wringing machine (the compressor).
  3. At the compressor: The machine does work, twisting and squeezing the sponge with great force. This makes the sponge itself very hot.
  4. Outside the fridge: The hot, sopping-wet sponge is held out in the room, where it radiates its heat and drips out its "water" (condenses), becoming a cool, "dry" sponge again, ready to repeat the process.

The First Law of Thermodynamics

For a complete cycle, the energy must balance. The total energy ejected from the system must equal the total energy put into it. In this case, the heat rejected to the hot room (`Q_H`) must equal the heat absorbed from the cold fridge (`Q_C`) plus the energy added by the pump to run the cycle (`W`).

Q_H = Q_C + W

This shows that a refrigerator always adds more heat to the room than it removes from its interior, which is why you can't cool your kitchen by leaving the fridge door open.

Coefficient of Performance (COP)

The efficiency of a refrigerator is measured by its Coefficient of Performance, or COP. It's the ratio of what you want (cooling) to what you pay for (work).

COP = Desired Output / Required Input = Q_C / W

A higher COP means a more efficient refrigerator. Unlike the efficiency of a heat engine, the COP can be greater than 1. A typical household fridge has a COP between 2 and 4.

The Ideal (Carnot) Limit

The maximum possible COP for any refrigerator operating between a cold temperature `T_C` and a hot temperature `T_H` is given by the Carnot COP. Temperatures must be in an absolute scale like Kelvin (K = °C + 273.15).

COP_Carnot = T_C / (T_H - T_C)

Example: For our default `T_C = -10°C = 263.15 K` and `T_H = 30°C = 303.15 K`:
COP_Carnot = 263.15 / (303.15 - 263.15) = 263.15 / 40 = 6.58
No real refrigerator can ever be this efficient, but it provides a theoretical upper limit.

Real Refrigerants and P-h Diagrams

The simulation uses a simplified model, but real refrigeration cycles are analyzed using Pressure-Enthalpy (P-h) diagrams specific to the refrigerant being used (like R-134a or R-600a). Enthalpy is a measure of the total energy of a thermodynamic system. The P-h diagram for a real fluid includes a "vapor dome," inside which the fluid is a two-phase mixture of liquid and vapor. Our simulation shows a simplified version of this diagram. The four points of the cycle are:

  1. Evaporator Exit: Saturated vapor at low pressure.
  2. Compressor Exit: Superheated vapor at high pressure.
  3. Condenser Exit: Saturated liquid at high pressure.
  4. Expansion Valve Exit: A low-quality liquid-vapor mixture at low pressure.

Sources of Inefficiency

Real refrigerators are less efficient than the ideal Carnot cycle for several reasons:

  • Irreversibilities: The compression and expansion processes are not perfectly isentropic (constant entropy). There are frictional losses.
  • Pressure Drops: There are pressure drops in the pipes and coils due to fluid friction.
  • Heat Transfer: Heat transfer in the condenser and evaporator requires a temperature difference, meaning the peak and minimum temperatures of the refrigerant must be more extreme than the room and fridge temperatures, reducing the ideal COP.
  • Compressor Inefficiencies: The mechanical compressor is not 100% efficient at converting electrical work into fluid work.

Environmental Impact

Older refrigerants, like chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, e.g., R-12), were found to be extremely damaging to the Earth's ozone layer. International treaties like the Montreal Protocol led to their phase-out. They were replaced by hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs, e.g., R-134a), which don't harm the ozone layer but are potent greenhouse gases. The industry is now shifting again towards hydrocarbons (like R-600a, isobutane) or hydrofluoroolefins (HFOs), which have much lower global warming potential.
