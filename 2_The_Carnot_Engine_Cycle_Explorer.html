Plain-Language Overview

A heat engine, like the one in your car, is a device that converts heat into useful work. The Carnot cycle, devised by Sadi Carnot in 1824, is a theoretical "perfect" cycle for a heat engine. While no real engine can be perfectly efficient, the Carnot cycle sets the absolute upper limit on the efficiency of any engine operating between two temperatures. This simulation allows you to explore this ideal cycle. On the left, you'll see a piston-cylinder containing a gas that can be heated or cooled. On the right, you'll see the cycle traced on a Pressure-Volume (P-V) diagram. By stepping through the four stages—two expansions and two compressions—you can build an intuition for how heat is transformed into work and why the Carnot cycle is a cornerstone of thermodynamics.

Beginner's Guide: The Perfect Engine

Step 1: What is a Heat Engine?

A heat engine is a machine that operates in a cycle, taking in heat from a hot source, converting some of that heat into mechanical work (like pushing a piston), and then expelling the leftover waste heat to a cold sink. This process repeats over and over. The goal is to get as much work out as possible for the amount of heat you put in. This is called the engine's "efficiency."

Step 2: The Four Stages of the Carnot Cycle

The Carnot cycle consists of four distinct, reversible stages. You can step through them using the buttons in the controls. The simulation shows the physical process on the left and tracks its state on the P-V diagram on the right.

  1. Isothermal Expansion: The gas starts at a high temperature (TH). It's placed in contact with a "hot reservoir" and allowed to expand, pushing the piston out. Because it's absorbing heat from the reservoir, its temperature stays constant during this expansion. (Heat QH enters the system)
  2. Adiabatic Expansion: The hot reservoir is removed, and the cylinder is insulated. The gas continues to expand. Since it's insulated, it can't absorb any more heat, so it does work by using its own internal energy. This causes its temperature to drop until it reaches the temperature of the cold reservoir (TC).
  3. Isothermal Compression: The insulation is removed, and the cylinder is placed in contact with a "cold reservoir." The piston now pushes in, compressing the gas. Because it's in contact with the cold reservoir, the gas expels heat and its temperature stays constant at TC. (Waste heat QC leaves the system)
  4. Adiabatic Compression: The cold reservoir is removed, and the cylinder is insulated again. The piston continues to push in, doing work on the gas. This work increases the gas's internal energy, raising its temperature from TC back up to the starting temperature TH. The cycle is now complete and ready to repeat.

Step 3: Pressure-Volume (P-V) Diagrams

The panel on the right is a P-V diagram, a standard tool in thermodynamics. It plots the pressure of the gas versus its volume. As the engine goes through its cycle, the point on the graph traces a closed loop. The two top curves are the expansions, and the two bottom curves are the compressions. A key result from thermodynamics is that the net work done by the engine in one cycle is equal to the area enclosed by this loop. The simulation shades this area for you.

Step 4: Efficiency

The efficiency of a heat engine is the ratio of the work you get out to the heat you put in: Efficiency (η) = Work / QH. Carnot proved that the maximum possible efficiency for any engine operating between two temperatures, TH and TC, is given by a simple formula: η = 1 - (TC / TH). Core Theory: The Physics of the Cycle

The simulation calculates the state of an ideal monatomic gas (with heat capacity ratio γ = 5/3) as it moves through the four stages.

1. Isothermal Processes (Constant Temperature)

During stages 1 and 3, the temperature is held constant. The relationship between pressure and volume is given by the Ideal Gas Law: P = nRT / V. The work done on or by the gas is:

W = ∫ P dV = nRT ln(Vfinal / Vinitial)

Since the internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature, the change in internal energy is zero. By the First Law of Thermodynamics (ΔU = Q - W), the heat absorbed or expelled must equal the work done: Q = W.

2. Adiabatic Processes (No Heat Exchange)

During stages 2 and 4, the system is insulated, so Q = 0. The relationship between pressure and volume is now:

PVγ = constant

As the gas expands or is compressed, its temperature changes according to the relation TVγ-1 = constant. The work done is equal to the change in the gas's internal energy: W = -ΔU = -nCVΔT.

3. Carnot Efficiency

The efficiency, η, is the net work done (Wnet) divided by the heat absorbed from the hot reservoir (QH). After calculating the work and heat for all four stages, it can be shown that this simplifies to a remarkable result that depends only on the reservoir temperatures:

η = Wnet / QH = (QH - QC) / QH = 1 - TC / TH

(Note: Temperatures TC and TH must be in an absolute scale like Kelvin).
