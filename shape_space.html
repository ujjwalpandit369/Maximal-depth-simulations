The Space of All Possible Shapes

Morphing between 2D shapes by traveling through "Shape Space".

Plain-Language Overview

What is the "distance" between a square and a star? "Shape Space" is a fascinating mathematical idea where every possible shape is a single point in a vast, high-dimensional space. A path between two points in this space represents a smooth transformation, or "morph," from the start shape to the end shape. This simulation lets you define two 2D shapes. When you press "Morph," it calculates a straight line path between them in shape space and visualizes the result as a fluid animation. This concept is powerful, with applications in computer animation (like in movies), medical imaging (comparing anatomical structures), and even biology (studying the evolution of species' forms).

Beginner's Guide

1. Representing a Shape as a Point

To a computer, a shape is just a list of numbers. In our simulation, we define a shape by the (x, y) coordinates of its corners (vertices). For example, a square might be represented as four points: [(0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)]. This list of 8 numbers can be thought of as a single "point" in an 8-dimensional space. A triangle would be a point in a 6-dimensional space. This is the core idea: any shape can be "plotted" as a point in a sufficiently high-dimensional "shape space."

2. The Correspondence Problem

A major challenge is how to morph a shape with 4 vertices (like a square) into one with 5 vertices (like a pentagon). Which vertex on the square should travel to which vertex on the pentagon? This is the correspondence problem. Our simulation solves this in a simple but effective way: it forces both shapes to have the same number of vertices before morphing.

If you draw a triangle (3 points) and a square (4 points), the simulation will automatically add a new vertex to the triangle by splitting one of its edges. Now both shapes have 4 vertices, and it's easy to match them up: the 1st point of the triangle will travel to the 1st point of the square, the 2nd to the 2nd, and so on. This process of adding points is called resampling.

3. The Path Through Shape Space: Linear Interpolation

Once we have two shapes, A and B, with the same number of corresponding vertices, finding the path between them is easy. The path is just a straight line in shape space. To find the shape that is, say, 30% of the way from A to B, we take each vertex of A and move it 30% of the way in a straight line towards the corresponding vertex of B.

The formula for any vertex i in the new shape S at time t (where t goes from 0 to 1) is:
Si = (1 - t) * Ai + t * Bi

This is called Linear Interpolation (often shortened to "Lerp"). When you move the slider, you are changing the value of t from 0 to 1. When you press "Animate Morph," the simulation does this automatically over a short period of time, creating the smooth animation.

4. Analogy: Morphing Faces

Think of the classic "face morph" effect in movies. Animators place corresponding points on two different faces: one on the tip of the nose of face A, one on the tip of the nose of face B; one on the corner of the left eye of A, one on the corner of the left eye of B, etc. The computer then performs the exact same linear interpolation on all these corresponding points to create a smooth transformation. Our shape morphing uses the same fundamental principle.

Core Theory Notes

Shape as a Vector

A 2D shape with n vertices can be represented as a single vector in a 2n-dimensional space. The vector is formed by concatenating the coordinates of its vertices:

V = [x1, y1, x2, y2, ..., xn, yn]

This vector V is a single point in the shape space ℝ2n. This formalizes the idea of a shape being a "point."

Resampling via Perimeter

To solve the correspondence problem between a shape with N points and one with M points (assume N < M), we need to add M-N new points to the first shape. A good way to do this is to distribute them evenly based on perimeter length.

  1. Calculate the total perimeter P of the shape.
  2. The desired spacing between vertices is d = P / M.
  3. Traverse the perimeter of the original shape, placing a new vertex every time you travel a distance of d. This may require placing new points in the middle of existing edges.
This ensures that the new points are distributed naturally around the shape's boundary, leading to a more pleasing morph.

Beyond Linear Paths

While we use a simple straight-line path in shape space (linear interpolation), more advanced methods exist. A "geodesic" path would account for the curvature of the shape space, often producing a more natural-looking morph that minimizes distortion. However, linear interpolation is computationally simple and works very well for many cases.
