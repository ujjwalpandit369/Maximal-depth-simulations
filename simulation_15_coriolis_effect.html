The Coriolis Effect

An apparent force experienced in a rotating frame of reference.

Estimated Runtime: Interactive | Recommended Device: Desktop | Approx. HTML Size: 150 KB

Plain-Language Overview

If you launch a ball on a spinning carousel, its path seems to curve as if pushed by a mysterious sideways force. This apparent force is known as the Coriolis effect. It's not a real force, but an illusion created by being in a rotating frame of reference. This simulation lets you observe this phenomenon from two perspectives: a stationary "bird's-eye view," where the ball travels in a straight line, and from the rotating carousel itself, where the ball's path appears curved. This effect is crucial on Earth for explaining the rotation of hurricanes and large-scale ocean currents.

Beginner's Guide — A Tale of Two Viewpoints

Step 1: The Inertial Frame (The "Real" Path)

First, let's look at the system from a stationary viewpoint, as if we are floating above the spinning turntable. This is called an **inertial frame of reference**. When we launch the ball from the center, there are no horizontal forces acting on it. According to Newton's First Law, an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force. Therefore, in this view, the ball travels in a perfectly straight line from the center to the edge. The turntable simply rotates underneath it.

Step 2: The Rotating Frame (The "Apparent" Path)

Now, let's switch to the viewpoint of someone standing on the turntable, rotating along with it. This is a **non-inertial (rotating) frame of reference**. From this perspective, the world looks different. As the ball travels along its straight-line path, the turntable's surface moves. The point on the edge that was initially straight ahead has rotated away by the time the ball gets there. To the observer on the turntable, the ball appears to have followed a curved path.

Step 3: The "Fictitious" Force

To explain this curved path from within the rotating frame, an observer would have to invent a force to account for the sideways deflection. This "fictitious" force is what we call the Coriolis force. It's important to remember that it's not a real force like gravity or magnetism; it's an artifact that appears because the frame of reference itself is accelerating (rotating). This simulation allows you to switch between the two frames to see that the straight path and the curved path are just two different descriptions of the exact same event.

Analogy: Drawing on a Spinning Record

Imagine a vinyl record spinning on a turntable. Take a ruler and a pen, and carefully draw a straight line from the center of the record to its outer edge while it's spinning. Your pen moves in a perfect straight line relative to the room (the inertial frame). But what do you see when you stop the turntable and look at the record? The "straight" line you drew is now a spiral curve. The path of the ball in the rotating frame is exactly like the curved ink line on the record.

Frames of Reference

The key to understanding the Coriolis effect is the concept of different frames of reference.

  • Inertial Frame: A non-accelerating frame of reference. In this frame, Newton's Laws hold in their standard form (`F=ma`). An object with no net force on it moves in a straight line at a constant velocity. Our "bird's-eye view" is an inertial frame.
  • Non-Inertial Frame: An accelerating frame of reference. Because the frame itself is accelerating, objects appear to move in ways not explained by the real forces acting on them. To make Newton's laws work, we must introduce "fictitious forces." For a rotating frame, the two main fictitious forces are the centrifugal force and the Coriolis force.

The Coriolis Force

The Coriolis force is given by the vector cross product:

F_Coriolis = -2m (ω × v_rot)

Where:

  • m is the mass of the object.
  • ω is the angular velocity vector of the rotating frame (pointing out of the screen, along the axis of rotation).
  • v_rot is the velocity of the object *as measured in the rotating frame*.
The cross product `(ω × v_rot)` results in a vector that is perpendicular to both the axis of rotation and the velocity. This is the sideways push. The negative sign determines its direction (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern).
