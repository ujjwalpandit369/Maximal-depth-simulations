This simulation explores the fundamental concept of kinematics: the relationship between an object's position, its velocity, and time. You can draw a graph that dictates where an object should be at any given moment. When you press play, an animation shows the object following your instructions precisely. The key insight is that the steepness, or "slope," of your position-time graph directly translates to the object's speed and direction (its velocity). A steep upward slope means fast forward motion, a flat line means the object is stationary, and a downward slope means it's moving backward. This interactive tool helps build a strong intuition for how graphical representations are not just abstract drawings, but powerful descriptions of real-world motion.

Welcome to the Motion Graph Explorer!

If you've ever wondered how physicists describe motion, you've come to the right place. We're going to explore one of the most powerful tools they use: the Position vs. Time graph. Don't worry, it's much simpler than it sounds!

Step 1: What are we looking at?

You see three main panels:

  • Top Panel (Position vs. Time): This is your command center. Think of it as a storyboard for an object's journey. The horizontal direction (the x-axis) represents Time passing, from left to right. The vertical direction (the y-axis) represents the object's Position on a straight track. A higher point on the graph means the object is further to the right on the track; a lower point means it's further to the left.
  • Middle Panel (Velocity vs. Time): This graph is created automatically. It shows you the object's velocity at every moment in time. We'll get to what this means shortly.
  • Bottom Panel (The Animation): This is where the action happens! You'll see a small object (a blue ball) moving left and right on a track. Its movement will perfectly match the graph you draw in the top panel.

Step 2: Your First Drawing

Let's try something simple. In the top panel, click your mouse on the left side, near the middle. Now, hold the mouse button down and drag it in a straight, diagonal line up and to the right. Let go of the mouse button on the far right side of the panel. You've just drawn your first position-time graph!

What did you just tell the object to do? You told it: "Start at a certain position. As time goes on, steadily move to the right (a higher position)."

Step 3: Lights, Camera, Action!

Now, find the "Play" button in the controls panel on the right and click it. Watch the bottom animation panel. The blue ball starts moving! You'll also see a vertical red line sweep across your graph, showing the current moment in time. Notice how the ball moves exactly as you instructed: it starts at the position you clicked and moves to the right. The speed of the ball is constant because the line you drew has a constant steepness.

Step 4: The Secret of the Slope

This is the most important idea of this simulation. The slope (or steepness) of your line in the position-time graph tells you the object's velocity.

  • Steep upward slope: The position changes a lot in a small amount of time. This means the object is moving very fast to the right (positive velocity).
  • Shallow upward slope: The position changes slowly. This means the object is moving slowly to the right (small positive velocity).
  • Flat horizontal line (zero slope): The position does not change at all as time passes. This means the object is stopped. Its velocity is zero.
  • Downward slope: As time moves forward, the position value gets smaller (moves down the graph). This means the object is moving to the left (negative velocity). A steep downward slope means it's moving fast to the left.

Try it! Click the "Reset" button. Now draw a graph that goes up, then flat, then down. Press "Play". You should see the ball move right, then stop for a moment, then move left. Look at the middle panel (Velocity vs. Time) while this happens. You'll see the velocity graph is high, then drops to zero, then goes negative, perfectly matching the slope of your position graph!

Step 5: The Tangent Tool

To help see this, make sure the "Show Tangent Line" checkbox is ticked. When you play the simulation, you'll see a short green line that stays "stuck" to your curve, always pointing along its direction at the current time. This line shows the instantaneous slope. Watch how its angle matches the velocity graph and the object's speed.

Congratulations! You've just learned the core principle of kinematics. By simply looking at the shape of a position-time graph, you can tell the story of an object's entire journey: where it went, how fast it was going, and when it stood still.

Preset 1: Constant Velocity

What to expect: The object will move from left to right at a perfectly steady speed. The velocity graph will be a flat horizontal line, indicating the velocity never changes.

Preset 2: Acceleration

What to expect: The object will start slowly, then gradually speed up, covering more and more distance as time passes. The position graph is a curve that gets steeper. The velocity graph will be a straight, upward-sloping line.

Preset 3: Complex Motion

What to expect: A more complex journey. The object moves forward, slows down to a stop, reverses direction, and then stops again. Observe how each segment of the position graph corresponds to a specific action.

The simulation is built on the definition of average and instantaneous velocity.

Average Velocity

Average velocity (𝜵̅) is the total change in position (displacement, Δx) divided by the total change in time (Δt).

Equation:

𝜵̅ = Δx / Δt = (x_final - x_initial) / (t_final - t_initial)

Plain English: Your average speed on a road trip is the total distance you drove divided by the total time it took.

Numeric Example: If you draw a line from position x=2m at t=1s to position x=10m at t=5s, the average velocity is:

𝜵̅ = (10 - 2) m / (5 - 1) s = 8 m / 4 s = 2 m/s

Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous velocity (v) is the velocity at a single, specific moment in time. It's what your car's speedometer shows. In calculus, this is found by taking the derivative of the position function with respect to time. Graphically, it is the slope of the tangent line to the position-time graph at that instant.

Equation:

v(t) = lim (Δt → 0) [Δx / Δt] = dx/dt

Plain English: We find the instantaneous velocity by calculating the average velocity over an infinitesimally small time interval.

Numeric Example: In the simulation, we approximate this by taking two very close points on your curve. If at t=2.00s, x=4.00m and at t=2.01s, x=4.03m, the approximate instantaneous velocity is:

v ≈ (4.03 - 4.00) m / (2.01 - 2.00) s = 0.03 m / 0.01 s = 3 m/s

The velocity-time graph is a plot of this value for every point in time.
