Plain-Language Overview

In the quantum world, particles are either "individualist" fermions or "social" bosons. Bosons love to be in the same state together. This preference leads to a remarkable phenomenon when a gas of bosonic atoms is cooled to temperatures just fractions of a degree above absolute zero. At a certain critical temperature, the atoms undergo a phase transition, and a large fraction of them suddenly collapses into the single lowest-energy quantum state available. They cease to act as individual particles and begin to behave as a single, coherent quantum entity—a "super atom" called a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC). This simulation lets you control the temperature of a trapped gas of bosons and see this incredible transition for yourself, watching as a sharp, dense condensate peak emerges from a diffuse thermal cloud.

Beginner's Guide: The Social Network of Atoms

Step 1: Bosons, the Social Particles

All particles in the universe fall into one of two families. Fermions (like electrons) obey the Pauli Exclusion Principle and refuse to share the same quantum state. Bosons (like photons, the particles of light) are the opposite. They are happy to occupy the same state—in fact, they prefer it. This "social" behavior is responsible for phenomena like lasers, where countless photons act in perfect unison.

Step 2: Cooling Down and Quantum Overlap

In quantum mechanics, every particle has a wave-like nature. The size of this wave is called the "thermal de Broglie wavelength." At high temperatures, particles move fast, and this wavelength is tiny, so they act like classical billiard balls. As you cool a gas down, the particles slow down, and their quantum wavelengths grow larger. The simulation's main control is a temperature slider. As you lower the temperature, imagine the fuzzy quantum aura around each particle growing.

Step 3: The Phase Transition

A Bose-Einstein Condensate forms when the gas becomes so cold and dense that the quantum waves of the individual atoms start to overlap. Once they overlap, their "bosonic" nature takes over. Because they prefer to be in the same state, a large number of atoms will spontaneously drop into the single lowest-energy state available in their container (the "trap"). This is a true phase transition, like water freezing into ice, but it's a transition into a purely quantum state of matter.

Step 4: What You See in the Simulation

The simulation shows two views of the same process:

  • Left (Position Space): This shows the atoms in their trap. Above the critical temperature (Tc), they form a diffuse, classical gas cloud. As you cool the gas below Tc, a sharp, dense peak of atoms appears at the very center of the trap. This is the BEC. The atoms in this peak are all in the same ground state, acting as one.
  • Right (Momentum Space): This plots the distribution of the atoms' momenta. Above Tc, you see a broad distribution of momenta (some moving left, some right, some fast, some slow). When the BEC forms, a giant, sharp spike appears at zero momentum. This is because all the atoms in the condensate have dropped into the lowest energy state, which is a state of nearly zero momentum.
The "Condensate Fraction" in the readout tells you what percentage of the atoms have joined the BEC. This goes from 0% above Tc to nearly 100% as you approach absolute zero.

Core Theory: The BEC Phase Transition

1. Thermal De Broglie Wavelength

The quantum nature of a particle becomes significant when its thermal de Broglie wavelength, λT, becomes comparable to the average distance between particles.

λT = h / √(2πmkBT)

Where h is Planck's constant, m is the particle mass, kB is Boltzmann's constant, and T is the temperature. As T decreases, λT increases.

2. The Condition for Condensation

BEC occurs when the interparticle spacing is roughly equal to λT. This can be expressed more formally as when the "phase-space density" reaches a critical value.

nλT³ ≥ 2.612

Where n is the number density of the gas (N/V).

3. Critical Temperature (Tc)

By rearranging the condition above, we can solve for the critical temperature at which the transition occurs for a given density.

Tc ≈ (n / 2.612)2/3 * (h² / 2πmkB)

In the simulation, this value is calculated based on the fixed number of particles and trap size, and displayed for comparison with the current temperature.

4. Condensate Fraction

For temperatures below Tc, the fraction of atoms that have "condensed" into the ground state (N₀/N) is given by:

N₀/N = 1 - (T/Tc)3/2

This is the formula used to determine how many particles to draw in the sharp central peak versus the diffuse thermal cloud in the simulation.
