Overview

In 1949, logician Kurt Gödel discovered a bizarre but valid solution to Einstein's equations of general relativity. He imagined a universe filled with a uniformly rotating fluid. This global rotation drags the very fabric of spacetime so intensely that it twists the paths of light, allowing for "closed timelike curves" (CTCs). In simple terms, a CTC is a path a spaceship could take through spacetime to arrive back at its own past. This simulation provides a simplified 3D visualization of this concept, allowing you to launch a spaceship and see if its path can form a time loop.

Beginner's Guide to Time Travel

Welcome! Let's break down the mind-bending ideas of Gödel's universe.

1. Spacetime and Worldlines

Normally, we think of space (3 dimensions) and time (1 dimension) as separate. Einstein showed they are woven together into a single 4D fabric called "spacetime". Everything that exists traces a path through this fabric. This path is called a worldline. Your worldline is your history, stretching from your birth to your death. In this simulation, the vertical axis represents time, and the flat grid represents two dimensions of space. The colored tube is the worldline of your spaceship.

2. Light Cones: The Speed Limit of the Universe

Nothing can travel faster than light. This fundamental rule creates a "light cone" at every point in spacetime. Imagine dropping a pebble in a pond; the ripples expand outwards in a circle. In spacetime, an event (like a flash of light) expands as a sphere. If we plot this sphere's expansion over time, it forms a cone. The surface of this cone represents the path of light, and anything with mass must travel *inside* this cone. Your future is confined to your future light cone.

3. Spacetime Dragging and Tilted Cones

In Gödel's universe, the entire cosmos is rotating. This isn't like a planet spinning in empty space; the "stuff" of the universe itself is swirling. General relativity tells us that mass and energy warp spacetime. A huge, rotating mass will "drag" spacetime around with it. In Gödel's universe, this effect is so extreme that it tilts all the light cones. You can see this in the simulation: the cones are not pointing straight up but are uniformly tilted in the direction of rotation.

4. An Everyday Analogy: A River Whirlpool

Imagine you are in a boat on a wide, slowly moving river. You can easily travel upstream or downstream. This is like normal spacetime. Now, imagine you are near a massive whirlpool. The water itself is swirling violently. Even if you point your boat straight towards the shore, the current will drag you sideways. This is "spacetime dragging". If the whirlpool is strong enough, you could travel in a large circle and end up back where you started, but a little downstream.

In Gödel's universe, the "downstream" is the future. Because the light cones are tilted, the "future" direction is also tilted. By traveling in a wide enough circle, your worldline's "future" can be pointed back towards your own past, creating a Closed Timelike Curve (CTC).

5. How to Create a Time Loop

In this simulation, a CTC is formed when the spaceship's worldline (the tube) loops back and intersects itself at a lower point (an earlier time). To achieve this, the spaceship needs to travel on a path that exceeds a critical radius from the center.

  1. Use the Ship Initial Velocity slider to give the ship a high speed. A higher velocity creates a wider path.
  2. Use the Universe Rotation slider to increase the "dragging" effect. More rotation makes time travel easier.
  3. Press Launch Spaceship. Watch the worldline trace out. If the path is wide enough, you will see it loop back on itself and the "CTC DETECTED" message will appear. Try the "Time Travel Path" preset to see a guaranteed example.

Core Theory & Equations

While the full Gödel metric is complex, we can understand the key concepts with simplified equations.

1. The Light Cone Condition

In any spacetime, the path of a light ray is defined by `ds² = 0`, where `ds` is the spacetime interval. For Gödel's universe, this simplifies to an equation that shows the future light cone is tilted.

g_μν dx^μ dx^ν = 0

Plain English: This is the fundamental rule for how light travels. In Gödel's solution, the components of the metric `g_μν` contain terms for rotation, which causes the tilt.

2. Critical Radius for Time Travel

A closed timelike curve is possible only if a spaceship travels on a path with a radius `r` larger than a critical radius `r_c`.

r > r_c = c / ω

Plain English: You have to "go wide" to travel in time. The faster the universe rotates (larger `ω`), the smaller the critical radius, making time travel easier. If the universe rotates slowly, the radius required might be larger than the universe itself!

Numeric Example: In our simulation, we can set the speed of light `c = 1` for simplicity. If the universe rotation `ω` is set to 0.2, the critical radius is `r_c = 1 / 0.2 = 5`. The ship's trajectory must exceed a radius of 5 units from the center for a CTC to be possible.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Is this a real physical possibility?
Probably not. While it's a valid mathematical solution, our own universe does not appear to be rotating on a global scale. The Gödel universe also requires a specific, uniform distribution of matter-energy that we don't observe.
2. Why does the spaceship travel in a curve?
This is due to the "spacetime dragging" or "frame-dragging" effect. The rotating mass of the universe warps spacetime, forcing objects to follow curved paths (geodesics) even if no force is acting on them.
3. What is a "worldline"?
It's the path of an object through 4D spacetime. In our simulation, the tube represents the worldline of the spaceship, with the vertical axis being time.
4. What does "CTC" stand for?
Closed Timelike Curve. It's a worldline that loops back on itself, allowing an object to meet its past self. This is the technical term for a time travel loop.
5. Why does the simulation stop?
The simulation stops after a fixed number of steps (e.g., 2000) to prevent the scene from becoming too cluttered and to check for a CTC. You can launch a new ship at any time.
