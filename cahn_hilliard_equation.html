The Cahn-Hilliard Equation: Phase Separation

Guide: Unmixing the Mixture

An Intuitive Analogy: Oil and Vinegar

Imagine a bottle of oil and vinegar salad dressing. When you shake it vigorously, the oil and vinegar are temporarily mixed together. This is a high-energy, unstable state. If you let the bottle sit, you'll see the mixture spontaneously separate. The tiny droplets of oil will find each other and merge, and the same happens with the vinegar. Eventually, you have two distinct layers. The system does this because the separated state has a lower total free energy than the mixed state.

Spinodal Decomposition

The process shown in the simulation is a specific type of phase separation called spinodal decomposition. This happens when a mixture is very rapidly cooled (or "quenched") deep into an unstable region of its phase diagram. Instead of forming small droplets that grow (a process called nucleation), the mixture is unstable to even the tiniest fluctuations in concentration. Separation happens everywhere at once, leading to the characteristic, interconnected, spongy or web-like pattern you see. Over time, these regions will continue to grow larger, a process called coarsening.

This simulation solves the Cahn-Hilliard equation, which mathematically describes this process. It models a phenomenon called "uphill diffusion," where atoms or molecules move from regions of lower concentration to regions of higher concentration, which is the opposite of normal diffusion but is required to create the separated phases.

The Cahn-Hilliard Equation

The evolution of the concentration field `c(x,t)` is described by the Cahn-Hilliard equation, a fourth-order partial differential equation:

∂c/∂t = M * ∇²(μ)

Where `M` is mobility and `μ` is the chemical potential. The chemical potential itself depends on the concentration:

μ = ∂f/∂c - κ∇²c

Here, `f(c)` is the local free energy density, which has a "double-well" shape (e.g., `f(c) = A(c² - 1)²`) that favors separated states (c=-1 and c=1) over the mixed state (c=0). The term `κ∇²c` represents the gradient energy, which penalizes sharp interfaces between the phases, driving the coarsening behavior.

Solving this equation directly is difficult. This simulation uses a common and efficient technique called a spectral method. The equation is transformed into Fourier space, where the troublesome derivatives (Laplacians `∇²`) become simple multiplications. The system is evolved in Fourier space and then transformed back to real space for visualization.
