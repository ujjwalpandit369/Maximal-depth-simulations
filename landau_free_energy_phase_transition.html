Overview

How does a material spontaneously become magnetic, or a liquid become a superfluid? Landau's theory provides a powerful framework for understanding these "second-order" phase transitions. This simulation visualizes the core idea: the "free energy" of a system, which acts like a potential energy, changes its shape as temperature varies. Above a critical temperature (Tc), the system is most stable in a disordered state. Below Tc, the disordered state becomes unstable, and the system spontaneously settles into a new, ordered state. Watch as the energy landscape itself transforms, forcing the system to pick a new state of equilibrium.

Beginner's Guide to Phase Transitions

Let's explore how a system can suddenly change its properties at a specific temperature.

Step 1: The Order Parameter (M)

The horizontal axis of our graph is the "Order Parameter," M. This is a quantity that measures how "ordered" the system is.

  • M = 0: Represents a completely disordered state. Think of the random alignment of atomic magnets in a hot piece of iron—it's not magnetic.
  • M ≠ 0: Represents an ordered state. This would be the atomic magnets aligning to create a net magnetic field. `M > 0` might be "North" and `M < 0` might be "South".
The system's goal is to find the value of M that gives it the lowest possible Free Energy (F).

Step 2: The Free Energy Landscape (F)

The vertical axis is the Landau Free Energy, F. You can think of this as the potential energy of the system. A ball representing the system will always try to roll to the lowest point on this energy curve.

Step 3: The Critical Temperature (Tc)

Temperature is the key to everything here. Use the slider to change the temperature and watch the shape of the energy landscape transform.

  • Above Tc (High Temperature): The energy landscape is a simple bowl with its minimum at M = 0. The system is most stable in its disordered state. The ball rests in the middle.
  • At Tc (The Critical Point): The bottom of the bowl becomes very flat. The system is on the verge of change.
  • Below Tc (Low Temperature): The landscape dramatically changes! The center point at M=0 becomes an unstable peak, and two new, lower-energy valleys appear at non-zero M. The system can no longer stay at M=0. It must "choose" one of the two new ordered states to roll into. This is called spontaneous symmetry breaking and it's the heart of the phase transition.

How to Use the Simulation

  1. Use the Temperature Slider: This is your only control.
  2. Move the slider above Tc (marked on the slider) and see the single energy well.
  3. Slowly move the slider below Tc. Watch the curve transform and see the ball spontaneously roll from the M=0 state into one of the new M≠0 states.

Core Theory: The Landau Potential

Near a second-order phase transition, the free energy F can be approximated as a simple polynomial of the order parameter M:

F(M) = F₀ + a(T - Tc)M² + bM⁴

Where `a` and `b` are positive constants, and Tc is the critical temperature.

  • The `bM⁴` term is always positive and ensures the energy goes up for large M, keeping the system stable.
  • The `a(T - Tc)M²` term is the crucial part.
    • When `T > Tc`, this term is positive, creating a potential well at M=0.
    • When `T < Tc`, this term becomes negative, turning the M=0 point into a barrier and creating a "double-well" potential.

The equilibrium state is found by minimizing F with respect to M (i.e., setting `dF/dM = 0`). This gives `M=0` or `M² = -a(T-Tc)/(2b)`, which only has real solutions for `T < Tc`.
