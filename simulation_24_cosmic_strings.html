Plain-Language Overview

Imagine the Big Bang not just as an explosion, but as a rapid cooling process, like water freezing into ice. Just as imperfections like cracks can form in ice, some theories predict that "cracks" could have formed in the fabric of spacetime itself. These are known as cosmic strings: incredibly thin, unbelievably dense filaments of energy stretching across vast cosmic distances. While still hypothetical, their existence would have profound consequences. This simulation explores what a cosmic string might look like and demonstrates its most famous predicted effect: gravitational lensing, where the string's unique gravity bends light from distant objects to create distinctive double images.

Beginner's Guide — A Gentle Introduction

Step 1: A Universe of Changing States (Phase Transitions)

We are familiar with phase transitions in everyday life: water (liquid) boils to become steam (gas), or freezes to become ice (solid). The universe itself, as it cooled after the Big Bang, went through a series of similar phase transitions. The fundamental forces of nature we see today (like electromagnetism and the weak nuclear force) are thought to have once been unified in a hotter, more symmetric state. As the universe cooled, this symmetry "broke," and the forces separated.

Step 2: Defects in the Freezing Spacetime

When a material undergoes a phase transition, imperfections, or "topological defects," can form.

Analogy: Freezing a Lake. Imagine a lake freezing over. Ice crystals start forming in many different places at once, each with its own random orientation. As these patches of ice grow and meet, their crystal grids won't perfectly align. This forces the creation of defects, like long cracks or boundaries where the mismatch is resolved.

Cosmic strings are a type of topological defect predicted to have formed in a similar way. As the universe "froze" into its new state, different regions of spacetime made this transition independently. Where these regions met, the fields might have been unable to align, trapping a line of the old, high-energy "unfrozen" vacuum state. This trapped line of energy is a cosmic string.

Step 3: What is a Cosmic String?

A cosmic string is a hypothetical 1-dimensional object that is:

  • Extremely Thin: Thinner than a proton, but potentially stretching across millions of light-years.
  • Extremely Dense: A single kilometer of cosmic string could have more mass than the entire Earth.
  • Under Extreme Tension: This tension causes them to vibrate at near the speed of light and straighten out over time.

Step 4: The Weird Gravity of a String

A string's gravity is unlike a star's or a planet's. A star pulls you towards it. A cosmic string does not. Locally, spacetime around a string is perfectly flat—you would feel no gravitational pull at all. However, the global geometry is changed.

Analogy: The Cut-Out Wedge. Take a flat circle of paper. It represents normal, flat spacetime. Now, cut a thin wedge (like a slice of pizza) out of it and tape the two cut edges together. You have now formed a cone.

  • The surface of the cone is still "flat." An ant walking on it would think it's on a flat plane.
  • But the overall geometry is different. If the ant walks in a circle around the cone's tip, it will find it has traveled less than 360 degrees to get back to its starting point. There is a "deficit angle."
This is exactly what a cosmic string does to spacetime. It removes a "wedge" of angular space. Light from a distant quasar can travel along both sides of this conical spacetime to reach us, creating two identical images where we would normally only see one. This is the key gravitational lensing signature we look for.

Worked Example: Deficit Angle

The deficit angle δφ (how much angle is "missing" from spacetime) is the key to lensing. It's calculated in radians as:

δφ = 8π * (Gμ/c²)

If we set the String Tension Gμ/c² = 2.0 x 10⁻⁶:
δφ = 8π * (2.0 x 10⁻⁶) ≈ 5.02 x 10⁻⁵ radians.
This is a tiny angle! To convert to arcseconds (a common unit in astronomy), we multiply by 206,265.
δφ ≈ 5.02 x 10⁻⁵ * 206265 ≈ 10.4 arcseconds.
This means the two images of a lensed quasar would appear separated by about 10.4 arcseconds in the sky, a separation that is easily detectable by telescopes.

String Networks and Evolution

Cosmic strings are not expected to be isolated objects. They would form as a complex, tangled network of strings, including long strings stretching across the universe and small, closed loops. This network would evolve over time:

  • Straightening: The immense tension in the strings acts to straighten them out at speeds close to the speed of light.
  • Intersections: When two strings cross, they can "intercommute"—they cut and rejoin with the other string, exchanging partners.
  • Loop Formation: A string can intersect with itself, pinching off a closed loop.
  • Decay: These closed loops oscillate violently and are predicted to decay by radiating gravitational waves. This is a key mechanism for the string network to lose energy and not dominate the universe's mass density.

Observational Constraints

While a fascinating idea, there is currently no definitive evidence for cosmic strings. Searches have put tight limits on their possible tension:

  • Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB): Strings would leave tell-tale linear discontinuities in the temperature of the CMB. The lack of such signals in data from Planck and other telescopes puts strong constraints on their tension (Gμ/c² < ~1.5 x 10⁻⁷).
  • Pulsar Timing Arrays: The gravitational waves from decaying string loops would form a stochastic background. Searches for this background using precise timing of pulsars also constrain the string tension.
The values for tension used in this simulation are much higher than these limits, in order to make the lensing effect clearly visible.
