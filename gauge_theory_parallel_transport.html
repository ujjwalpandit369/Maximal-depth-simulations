Guide: Rolling Without Slipping

The Rolling Cylinder Analogy

Imagine the main view on the left is a flat table (the "base space"). At every point on this table, imagine a tiny, independent circle is attached (the "fibre," representing the internal phase of a particle). A gauge field is a rule that "connects" adjacent circles. It tells you how to relate the phase of a particle at one point to its phase at a neighboring point.

A good analogy is to imagine rolling a cylinder on the table without it slipping. The direction the cylinder points on the table is its position in the base space. An arrow drawn on the circular face of the cylinder represents its internal state (the fibre). If you roll the cylinder along a path on the table, the arrow on its face will rotate. If you roll it around a closed loop and return to the start, the arrow will be pointing in a new direction! The amount of rotation depends on the path taken. This final rotation is the holonomy.

What the Simulation Shows

  1. Draw a Loop: Click and drag on the left canvas to draw a closed path for a particle to travel.
  2. Parallel Transport: Click "Animate Transport." A particle will travel along your path. As it moves, its internal phase vector (the arrow on the right) is "parallel transported" according to the gauge field connection.
  3. Holonomy: When the particle returns to its starting point, observe the final angle of the phase vector. It has rotated! This rotation is the holonomy, and it is a direct measure of the total "flux" or field strength enclosed by your loop. The simulation calculates this expected value and compares it to the result of the transport.

The Aharonov-Bohm Effect in Geometric Language

This simulation is a geometric interpretation of the Aharonov-Bohm effect in electromagnetism. The U(1) gauge field is the electromagnetic vector potential, `A`, and the field strength is the magnetic field, `B = ∇ × A`.

The Connection

The rule for parallel transport dictates that as a charged particle moves by an infinitesimal distance `dl`, its quantum phase `θ` changes by `dθ = q * A · dl`, where `q` is the charge (we'll set `q=1`). This is the "connection" that links the phase (the fibre) at different points in space (the base manifold).

Holonomy and Flux

When a particle is transported around a closed loop `C`, the total phase change (the holonomy) is the integral of these small changes:

Δθ = ∮_C A · dl

By Stokes' theorem, this line integral is equal to the flux of the curl of `A` through the surface `S` enclosed by the loop:

∮_C A · dl = ∫_S (∇ × A) · dS = ∫_S B · dS

For the uniform field `B` in this simulation, this simplifies to `Δθ = B * Area(S)`. The final phase rotation is directly proportional to the magnetic flux enclosed by the path, even if the particle never travels through a region where the magnetic field is non-zero (a key feature of the Aharonov-Bohm effect).
