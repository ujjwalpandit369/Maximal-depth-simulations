Relativistic Velocity Addition

Why you can't just add speeds in Special Relativity.

Plain-Language Overview

If you're on a train moving at 50 km/h and throw a ball forward at 10 km/h, someone on the ground sees the ball moving at 60 km/h. This simple addition breaks down at speeds close to the speed of light. If a spaceship moving at 0.6c (60% the speed of light) fires a probe forward at 0.6c, the probe does *not* travel at 1.2c! This is impossible. Einstein's theory provides a new formula for adding velocities that ensures `c` is the ultimate speed limit. This simulation shows the difference between the (wrong) classical addition and the (correct) relativistic addition.

1.200 c
Relativistic (u):

Beginner's Guide

1. The Setup

The animation shows a mothership moving at speed v. It fires a probe, which moves at speed u' *relative to the ship*. We want to find the probe's speed u as seen by a stationary observer.

2. Classical vs. Relativistic

Set both `v` and `u'` to 0.6c. The classical result is 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.2c, which is impossible. The relativistic result is u ≈ 0.882c, which is less than `c`. No matter what values you choose, the relativistic result will never exceed the speed of light.

3. Firing Backwards

Try a negative value for the probe's velocity, like -0.5c. This means firing it backwards. You'll see that the classical and relativistic results are still different, showing the formula is not just about a speed limit, but is the correct way to transform velocities.

Core Theory

The relativistic velocity addition formula is derived from the Lorentz transformations for space and time: u = (v + u') / (1 + vu'/c²). This formula elegantly preserves the speed of light as the universal speed limit. For example, if a ship moving at `v=c` were to fire a light beam `u'=c`, the result would be (c+c)/(1+c²/c²) = 2c/2 = c.
