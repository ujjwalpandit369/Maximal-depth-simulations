The Tangent Bundle: Space of Velocities

A simulation visualizing the tangent bundle for a particle on a sphere.

Overview

In modern physics and mathematics, the space of all possible states of a system—its positions and velocities—is described by a geometric object called a tangent bundle. This simulation provides an intuitive visualization of this concept for a simple system: a particle constrained to move on the surface of a sphere. At every point on the sphere, the particle's velocity must lie in a flat plane that is tangent to the sphere at that point. You can move the particle around and see this "tangent space" move with it. You can also define a velocity vector and launch the particle to see it travel along a "straight line" on the curved surface, known as a geodesic.

Beginner's Guide to Tangent Spaces

Step 1: The Sphere (Configuration Space)

Imagine a tiny particle that is forced to live on the surface of a sphere. The set of all possible positions for this particle is the sphere itself. In geometry, this is called the "configuration space."

Step 2: The Tangent Plane (The Space of Velocities)

Now, imagine the particle is at some point on the sphere. In what directions can it move? It can't move into or out of the sphere; its velocity must be along the surface. The set of all possible velocity vectors at that single point forms a flat 2D plane that just touches the sphere where the particle is. This plane is called the tangent space.

The simulation visualizes this as a small, transparent blue plane attached to the particle. As you drag the particle around, watch how the plane moves with it, always staying tangent to the surface.

Step 3: The Tangent Bundle

The "tangent bundle" is simply the name for the entire collection of all the tangent spaces for all the points on the sphere, all bundled together. It's the total space of (position, velocity) pairs.

Step 4: Geodesics - Straight Lines on a Curve

If you give the particle a push and then let it coast freely, what path will it follow? It will follow the "straightest possible path" on the curved surface. On a sphere, this path is always a great circle (like the equator). These paths of "straightest" motion on a curved manifold are called geodesics.

How to Use the Simulation

  1. Rotate the View: Click and drag on an empty part of the canvas to rotate the 3D view.
  2. Move the Particle: Click and drag the white particle to move it to any point on the sphere. Observe how the blue tangent plane follows it.
  3. Define a Velocity: With the particle placed, click and drag on the blue tangent plane itself. An orange arrow will appear. This is your velocity vector.
  4. Launch: Release the mouse to "launch" the particle with that velocity. Watch it trace a geodesic path around the sphere.
  5. Reset: Click "Reset" to stop the animation and place a new particle.

Controls

Drag the sphere to rotate. Drag the particle to move it. Drag on the blue plane to set velocity and launch.
