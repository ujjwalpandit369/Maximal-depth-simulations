Overview

Why do some chemical reactions proceed while others don't? The answer often lies in the Gibbs Free Energy (G). This simulation visualizes how a system finds its most stable state, or "equilibrium." Gibbs Free Energy is a thermodynamic potential that balances two competing tendencies: the drive to reach the lowest energy state (Enthalpy, H) and the drive to achieve the highest disorder (Entropy, S). Temperature (T) is the critical factor that determines how important the entropy term is. By adjusting the temperature, you can change the shape of the Gibbs energy curve and see how the system's equilibrium point shifts in response.

Beginner's Guide to Chemical Equilibrium

Let's explore the driving forces behind change in the universe.

Step 1: The Two Fundamental Drives

Imagine a system that can be a mix of "Reactants" (state 0) and "Products" (state 1). The system is driven by two competing desires:

  • To Minimize Enthalpy (H): Enthalpy is roughly the total energy of the system. Like a ball rolling downhill, systems prefer to be in a lower energy state. The red curve (H) on the graph shows this energy landscape.
  • To Maximize Entropy (S): Entropy is a measure of disorder or randomness. Systems prefer to be in a more disordered state. The "entropy contribution" to the free energy is given by the term `-TS`. The blue curve (-TS) shows this contribution. Notice its U-shape: a mix of reactants and products (around the middle) is usually more disordered than pure reactants or pure products.

Step 2: Gibbs Free Energy (G) - The Decider

Gibbs Free Energy, `G = H - TS`, is the master variable that combines these two drives. The system doesn't just minimize H or maximize S; it minimizes G. The purple curve (G) is simply the sum of the red curve and the blue curve.

The most important rule in thermodynamics (at constant temperature and pressure) is: A system will spontaneously evolve to the state with the minimum possible Gibbs Free Energy. In our simulation, the "ball" representing the system's state will always roll to the lowest point on the purple G curve. This lowest point is the position of equilibrium.

Step 3: The Power of Temperature

Temperature (T) is the key. Look at the equation `G = H - TS`. The `T` multiplies the entropy term. This means:

  • At low temperatures, the `-TS` term is small. The G curve looks very similar to the H curve. The system's equilibrium is mostly determined by the drive to lower its energy (enthalpy).
  • At high temperatures, the `-TS` term is large and becomes dominant. The G curve starts to look more like the `-TS` curve. The drive to increase disorder (entropy) takes over, and the equilibrium point will shift towards the state with the highest entropy.

How to Use the Simulation

  1. Use the Temperature Slider: This is your only control. Drag the slider to change the temperature of the system.
  2. Watch the Curves: Observe how the blue `-TS` curve gets deeper as you increase T. Notice how this changes the shape of the purple `G` curve.
  3. See the Equilibrium Shift: As the G curve changes, watch the ball animate to the new lowest point. This shows you how temperature can change the equilibrium of a reaction, for example, favoring products at one temperature and reactants at another.
