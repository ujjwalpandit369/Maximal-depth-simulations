The Affine Space: A Vector Space that Forgot its Origin

An interactive simulation to distinguish an affine space from a vector space.

Estimated runtime: Interactive Recommended device: Desktop/Mobile

Plain-Language Overview

In mathematics, a vector space is a familiar concept: a world of arrows (vectors) anchored to a special spot called the origin. An affine space is what you get if you take a vector space and "forget" where the origin is. You're left with a collection of points and vectors, but no special reference point. This simulation lets you play in an affine space. You can create points and vectors and try to combine them. You'll discover that some operations, like adding a vector to a point, make perfect sense. But others, like trying to add two points together, are fundamentally meaningless without an origin, and the simulation will stop you from performing them.

Click to create points or vectors. Drag them to interact.

Beginner's Guide

1. What are Points and Vectors?

In this simulation, we make a crucial distinction:

  • Points (Green Dots): A point represents a location. It just *is*. Think of it as a specific spot on a map, like "the Eiffel Tower." It doesn't have a size or direction. Click "Create Point" to make one.
  • Vectors (Blue Arrows): A vector represents a displacement or a translation. Think of it as instructions like "go 3 miles northeast." It has both a length (magnitude) and a direction, but no fixed starting position. Click "Create Vector" to make one.

A vector space is full of vectors, all starting from a single origin. An affine space has points scattered everywhere, and a separate "palette" of vectors that can be applied to them.

2. The Allowed Operations

By dragging the objects on the canvas, you can discover the fundamental rules of affine geometry. The simulation will highlight valid operations in green in the status bar.

  1. Point - Point = Vector: Drag one point (P₂) over another (P₁). A new vector will appear, representing the displacement from P₁ to P₂. This makes intuitive sense: the difference between two locations is a displacement.
  2. Point + Vector = Point: Drag the tail of a vector (v) over a point (P₁). A new point (P₂) will appear. This is like starting at a location (P₁) and following displacement instructions (v) to arrive at a new location (P₂).
  3. Vector + Vector = Vector: Drag the tail of one vector (v₂) to the head of another (v₁). A new vector will be created representing their sum (v₁ + v₂).

3. The Forbidden Operation: Point + Point

What happens if you try to add two points, P₁ + P₂? The simulation will show an error. Why? Because the operation is meaningless without a reference origin. Imagine P₁ is the Eiffel Tower and P₂ is the Statue of Liberty. What is "Eiffel Tower + Statue of Liberty"? The question doesn't make sense.

To perform this addition, you would first need to define an origin (say, London). Then you could define P₁ and P₂ as vectors from that origin. But if you changed the origin to Tokyo, the resulting vectors and their sum would be completely different. Since the result depends on an arbitrary choice of origin, the operation isn't a fundamental property of the space itself, so it is disallowed in affine geometry.

Core Theory

An affine space `A` consists of a set of points and is associated with a vector space `V`. The geometry is defined by a few simple axioms that correspond to the operations in this simulation.

  1. For any two points P, Q in A, there exists a unique vector `v` in V, denoted `v = Q - P`, such that `P + v = Q`. (This is our Point - Point operation).
  2. For any point P in A and any vector `v` in V, there is a unique point Q in A such that `Q = P + v`. (This is our Point + Vector operation).
  3. The vectors in `V` follow the standard rules of vector addition. (Our Vector + Vector operation).

Crucially, there is no axiom for adding two points P₁ + P₂. The structure is defined only by the relationship between points and vectors.

Analogies & Intuition

1. Locations vs. Displacements (Physical Analogy)

This is the most direct analogy. Points are locations ("My house," "The grocery store.") and vectors are displacements ("One mile east," "Up 3 flights of stairs."). You can find the displacement between two locations ("The grocery store" - "My house" = "One mile east"). You can start at a location and apply a displacement ("My house" + "Up 3 flights of stairs" = "My bedroom"). But you cannot add two locations ("My house" + "The grocery store" = ???).

2. Dates vs. Durations (Computational Analogy)

Think about how dates and times are handled in software. Points are specific moments in time (timestamps): `Oct 26, 1985, 01:22`. Vectors are durations of time: `30 minutes`. You can subtract two timestamps to get a duration. You can add a duration to a timestamp. But you cannot add two timestamps together.

3. An Infinite, Unmarked Map (Visualization Analogy)

Imagine an infinitely large sheet of blank paper. This is an affine space. You can mark points on it. Now, pick one special point, mark it with a big "O", and declare it the origin. You have just created a vector space. Now, every other point on the sheet can be described by a unique vector starting from O. The concept of "adding" two points now has a meaning: you add their corresponding vectors from the origin. The key is that this meaning only exists because you made an arbitrary choice of an origin.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. How do I perform an operation?
Create at least two objects. Click and drag one object so it overlaps with another. The status bar at the bottom will tell you what operation is possible. Release the mouse to complete it.
2. Why are vectors blue and points green?
To visually distinguish between the two fundamental types of objects in the space. Points are locations, and vectors are displacements.
3. What is the difference between this and a normal graph?
A normal graph (a Cartesian plane) is an example of a vector space. It has a pre-defined origin at (0,0). This simulation intentionally has no visible grid or origin to emphasize that these are not necessary for the geometry to work.
4. The simulation seems simple. What's the point?
Its purpose is to make a very specific and fundamental mathematical distinction tangible. This distinction is crucial in fields like computer graphics (affine transformations), physics (spacetime is an affine space), and geometry.
