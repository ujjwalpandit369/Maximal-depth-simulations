The Space of All Possible Functions

Approximating user-drawn functions with different mathematical basis sets.

Est. runtime: Real-time | Recommended device: Desktop | Approx. size: 50 KB

Overview

This simulation explores a powerful idea from mathematics and physics: that complex functions can be built by adding up simpler "basis" functions, much like any color can be made by mixing red, green, and blue light. Here, you can draw any function you like and then watch as the computer tries to approximate it using a chosen set of mathematical building blocks, like sine waves (Fourier Series) or special polynomials (Legendre Polynomials). By adjusting how many building blocks you use, you can see the approximation get closer and closer to your original drawing, visually demonstrating the concept of a function space.

Beginner's Guide to Building Functions

Welcome! This guide will introduce you to the powerful idea of "function spaces" using a simple, visual approach. Don't worry if these terms are new; we'll use helpful analogies along the way.

Step 1: Thinking of Functions as Points in a "Space"

You're probably used to thinking of a point in 3D space as a set of three coordinates: (x, y, z). These three numbers uniquely define a location. A function, like the ones you can draw in this simulation, can be thought of in a similar way. Instead of just three coordinates, a function has a coordinate for every single point along the x-axis. The value of the function at that x-point is its coordinate in that "direction".

Since there are infinitely many points on the x-axis, a function is like a point in an infinite-dimensional space. This is the mind-bending, core idea of a Hilbert Space. The green line you draw is a single "point" in this vast space!

Step 2: Basis Functions - The Building Blocks

In 3D space, we have special directions: the x-axis, the y-axis, and the z-axis. We can describe any point in space by saying "go 5 units along x, 3 units along y, and 10 units along z." These axes are our "basis vectors."

In function space, we have special functions called basis functions that act like our axes. We can build any other function by adding up the right amounts of these basis functions. This simulation lets you use two different sets of basis functions:

  • Fourier Series: Uses sines and cosines (wavy lines) as its building blocks. This is excellent for representing anything that is periodic or has wave-like features.
  • Legendre Polynomials: Uses a special set of polynomials (smooth, curving lines like x², x³, etc.) as building blocks. This is very good at representing smooth, non-repeating shapes.

The "amount" of each basis function we need is called its coefficient. The yellow line in the simulation is the sum of all the basis functions multiplied by their coefficients.

Step 3: How to Use the Simulation

  1. Draw a Function: Click and drag your mouse across the black canvas area. A green line will appear, representing your target function.
  2. Choose a Basis Set: Click the "Fourier Series" or "Legendre Polynomials" button to select your building blocks.
  3. Add More Terms: Use the "Number of Terms" slider. As you increase the number of terms, you are adding more basis functions to your approximation. Watch how the yellow line (the approximation) gets closer and closer to your green line.
  4. Check the Error: The "Error" readout below the canvas shows the average distance between your function and the approximation. As you add more terms, this number should get smaller, meaning the fit is getting better!

Core Theory: Projecting into a Function Space

The magic of this simulation comes from a mathematical process called "projection." We are projecting your hand-drawn function onto each of our basis functions to find out how much of each one we need.

1. The Inner Product (Measuring Alignment)

First, we need a way to measure how "aligned" two functions, f(x) and g(x), are. In vector math, this is the dot product. In function space, it's the inner product, which is defined as an integral:

⟨f, g⟩ = ∫ f(x)g(x) dx

Plain-English Translation: To get the inner product, you multiply the two functions together at every point and then find the total area under the curve of their product. A large positive value means they are very aligned; a value near zero means they are "orthogonal" (like the x and y axes are).

2. Calculating the Coefficients (Finding the "Amount")

The "amount" of each basis function (φₙ) we need is called the coefficient (cₙ). We find it by projecting your function (f) onto the basis function and normalizing it.

cₙ = ⟨f, φₙ⟩ / ⟨φₙ, φₙ⟩

Plain-English Translation: The best "amount" of a basis function is found by taking the inner product of your function with the basis function (how aligned they are) and dividing by the inner product of the basis function with itself (its "length squared").

Numeric Example: Imagine your function `f` has an inner product of 1.5 with the basis function `φ₁`, and `φ₁` has an inner product of 2.0 with itself. The coefficient would be `c₁ = 1.5 / 2.0 = 0.75`.

3. Building the Approximation

Once we have all the coefficients, we just add everything together.

f_approx(x) = Σ cₙφₙ(x)

Plain-English Translation: The final approximation is the sum of all your chosen basis functions, with each one scaled by its calculated coefficient. This is what the yellow line represents.

Advanced Notes & Extensions

Gibbs Phenomenon: When using a Fourier series to approximate a function with a jump discontinuity (like a square wave), the approximation always overshoots the jump by about 9% of the jump's height. This ringing artifact is called the Gibbs Phenomenon, and it doesn't go away no matter how many terms you add.

Choice of Basis: The choice of basis is critical. Fourier series are a "complete" basis for periodic functions. Legendre polynomials are a complete basis for functions on the interval [-1, 1] and are particularly good for functions that are well-approximated by polynomials. Other famous orthogonal polynomials include Chebyshev (minimizes maximum error) and Hermite (used in quantum mechanics for the harmonic oscillator).

Convergence: The error we are measuring is related to L² convergence, which means the integral of the squared difference between the function and its approximation goes to zero. This doesn't guarantee that the value at every single point converges, which is why the Gibbs phenomenon can exist.

How to Extend This Simulation:

Analogies & Intuition

1. Vector Components (Mathematical Analogy)

A function is like a vector in an infinite-dimensional space. The basis functions (sines, cosines, polynomials) are the orthogonal axes of this space. Calculating a coefficient is identical to finding the component of a vector along an axis by taking a dot product.

Limitations: It's hard to visualize infinite dimensions, but the mathematical parallel is exact.

2. Mixing Colors (Physical Analogy)

Think of the basis functions as pure primary colors (Red, Green, Blue). Any color on your screen can be represented as a mix of these three, like (R=255, G=128, B=0). Your drawn function is the target color, and the coefficients are the brightness values for each primary color needed to match it.

Limitations: This analogy only has three basis functions. Our simulation uses up to 50, and a true function space has infinitely many.

3. Audio Equalizer (Computational Analogy)

Any complex sound, like a piece of music, is a complex waveform. A Fourier series is exactly what an audio spectrum analyzer does: it breaks down the complex sound into the intensity of its fundamental frequencies (simple sine waves). The coefficients are the "volume sliders" for each frequency band on an equalizer.

Limitations: This analogy is specific to the Fourier basis, but it's a very direct and practical application of the same mathematics.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Why does the yellow line have ripples near the sharp edges of my drawing?
This is the Gibbs Phenomenon! It happens when you use a Fourier series (made of smooth waves) to approximate a function with a sudden jump. The approximation overshoots the jump, and the ripple doesn't go away even with many terms.
2. Which basis set is "better"?
Neither is better in general; it depends on the job! Use Fourier Series for things that are periodic (repeating) or wave-like. Use Legendre Polynomials for smooth, non-repeating shapes.
3. What is a "basis set"?
It's a set of "building block" functions that you can use to build up more complex functions. To be a valid basis, the functions should be "orthogonal" (not overlapping, in a mathematical sense) and "complete" (able to build any function in the space).
4. Why does the approximation get so slow when I use many terms?
For each term, the simulation has to do a lot of calculations (an entire numerical integral). The total work scales with the number of terms, so going from 40 to 50 terms is much more work than going from 1 to 10.
5. Why does the error never go to exactly zero?
Two reasons: 1) We are only using a finite number of basis functions, while a perfect fit might require infinitely many. 2) Your hand-drawn function is stored as a series of discrete points, and the math involves numerical approximations, not perfect symbolic integrals.
6. My drawing is messy. How do I start over?
Just click the "Reset" button in the control panel, or press the 'r' key on your keyboard.
