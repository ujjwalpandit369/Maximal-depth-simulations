Synchronization of Oscillators: The Kuramoto Model

A simulation of how large populations can spontaneously synchronize.

The Oscillator Circle

Adjust the coupling strength `K`. Past a critical point, the oscillators will start to synchronize.

Guide: The Emergence of Order

Fireflies, Neurons, and Clapping

Have you ever seen a field of fireflies start to flash in unison? Or been part of an audience whose clapping spontaneously falls into a single rhythm? This phenomenon, where large groups of independent "oscillators" synchronize their behavior, is widespread in nature. The Kuramoto model is a beautifully simple mathematical model created to understand how this happens.

The Model

The simulation shows a group of oscillators, each represented by a dot on a circle.

  • Each Oscillator has its own "opinion": Every oscillator has a natural frequency `ω` at which it would prefer to rotate around the circle. These frequencies are chosen randomly from a distribution.
  • They are "socially influenced": The oscillators are coupled together. Each one is influenced by the phase of every other oscillator. The strength of this influence is the Coupling Strength, K.

The Phase Transition to Synchrony

Use the slider for `K` to explore the system's behavior.

  1. Low K: When the coupling is weak, each oscillator mostly listens to its own natural frequency. They are spread out all over the circle, and the system is disordered. The orange order parameter vector in the center is very short, indicating no overall synchrony.
  2. Increasing K: As you increase `K`, the oscillators begin to "pull" on each other more strongly.
  3. Critical Point (K ≈ 1.2-1.6): At a specific critical value of `K`, a phase transition occurs! A small cluster of oscillators suddenly locks in phase. This creates a stronger collective signal, which rapidly pulls in more and more oscillators. You will see the order parameter vector grow dramatically in length.
  4. High K: For strong coupling, almost all oscillators are locked together, rotating at the same average frequency, despite their different natural frequencies.
