Plain-Language Overview

When you heat up a gas, you're adding energy to its molecules. But how do the molecules *store* that energy? The Equipartition Theorem provides a simple and powerful answer: in thermal equilibrium, the energy is shared equally among all the possible ways a molecule can move, rotate, or vibrate. This simulation demonstrates this principle by showing a gas mixture of simple, single-atom (monatomic) particles and two-atom "dumbbell" (diatomic) particles. You'll see that at the same temperature, the more complex diatomic molecules, which can both move and tumble, store more total energy than their simpler monatomic cousins. The simulation includes real-time charts that break down the energy, showing how nature "equally partitions" the energy among all available modes of motion.

Beginner's Guide: Sharing the Energy Pie

Step 1: Degrees of Freedom

Imagine you have to describe the motion of an object. The number of independent pieces of information you need is its number of "degrees of freedom."

  • A single point-like atom (monatomic) can move along the X, Y, and Z axes. That's **3 translational degrees of freedom**. (In our 2D simulation, we only see 2).
  • A dumbbell-shaped molecule (diatomic) can also move along the X, Y, and Z axes (3 translational degrees). But in addition, it can tumble or rotate. It can rotate around two independent axes (think of a spinning baton). That's **2 rotational degrees of freedom**. (In 2D, it can only rotate around one axis).
These different ways of moving—translating and rotating—are the "modes" into which a molecule can store energy.

Step 2: The Equipartition Theorem

The theorem is a cornerstone of classical statistical mechanics. It states that for a system in thermal equilibrium, the total energy is shared equally among all its degrees of freedom. More precisely, each degree of freedom that is "quadratic" (like kinetic energy, which depends on velocity squared) gets an average energy of:

Average Energy per Degree of Freedom = ½kBT

Where kB is the Boltzmann constant and T is the absolute temperature.

Step 3: Comparing the Molecules

This simple rule leads to a clear prediction, which you can test in the simulation:

  • The monatomic gas has only 2 translational degrees of freedom (in 2D). So, its average total energy should be: 2 × (½kBT) = kBT.
  • The diatomic gas has 2 translational and 1 rotational degree of freedom (in 2D). So, its average total energy should be: 3 × (½kBT) = (3/2)kBT.
At the same temperature, the diatomic molecules should have, on average, 50% more energy than the monatomic ones, because they have more ways to store it! Check the "Average Energy Readouts" panel below the simulation to see if this holds true.

Step 4: Watching Energy Transfer

In the simulation, the particles are constantly colliding. When a monatomic particle hits a diatomic one off-center, it can cause it to spin, transferring some translational energy into rotational energy. When two diatomic molecules collide, they can change both their speed and their rate of tumble. This constant shuffling is what allows the system to reach "thermal equilibrium," the state where the energy is, on average, distributed according to the equipartition theorem.

Core Theory: Energy Components

The simulation tracks the translational and rotational kinetic energy for each particle.

1. Translational Kinetic Energy

For any particle (monatomic or diatomic) of mass m and velocity v = (vx, vy), the translational kinetic energy is:

KEtrans = ½mvx² + ½mvy²

This represents two degrees of freedom. The equipartition theorem predicts the average energy for this part will be 2 × (½kBT) = kBT for both types of molecules.

2. Rotational Kinetic Energy

For a diatomic molecule with moment of inertia I and angular velocity ω, the rotational kinetic energy is:

KErot = ½Iω²

In 2D, there is only one axis of rotation, so this represents one additional degree of freedom. The theorem predicts its average energy will be ½kBT.

3. Total Average Energy

By summing the average energies of the degrees of freedom, we get the predicted total average energy for each molecule type:

Eavg, mono = Etrans = kBT

Eavg, di = Etrans + Erot = kBT + ½kBT = (3/2)kBT

The readouts in the simulation display these average energies, divided by kBT, so you should see values approaching 1.0 and 1.5 respectively.

The Ultraviolet Catastrophe

The classical equipartition theorem was known to fail for certain problems, most famously black-body radiation. It predicted that electromagnetic waves of high frequency (ultraviolet) should contain an infinite amount of energy, which was obviously wrong. This "ultraviolet catastrophe" was a major puzzle that was only solved by the advent of quantum mechanics, which showed that high-frequency modes are "frozen out" because they require a large minimum quantum of energy to be excited.
