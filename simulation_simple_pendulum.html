Beginner's Guide to the Pendulum

1. The Physics of a Swing

A simple pendulum consists of a mass (the "bob") hanging from a string attached to a fixed pivot point. When you pull the bob to one side and release it, gravity pulls it back down towards its lowest point (equilibrium). It overshoots this point due to its momentum and swings up the other side, and the process repeats. The force that always tries to bring the bob back to the center is called the restoring force. For a pendulum, this force is a component of gravity.

2. The Small-Angle Approximation

The motion of a pendulum is only truly "simple" for small starting angles (typically less than 15°). In this case, the restoring force is almost perfectly proportional to the displacement, just like a mass on a spring. This is called the small-angle approximation. When this is true, the pendulum exhibits Simple Harmonic Motion, and its period (the time for one full swing) does not depend on the starting angle or the mass of the bob. It only depends on the length of the string and the strength of gravity.

Try it yourself! Set the initial angle to 10°, measure the period, then set it to 60° and measure again. You'll see the period is longer for the larger angle.

3. Energy Transformation

As the pendulum swings, energy is continuously converted between two forms:

  • Gravitational Potential Energy (PE): Energy stored due to the bob's height. It's maximum at the highest points of the swing. (PE = mgh)
  • Kinetic Energy (KE): Energy of motion. It's maximum at the bottom of the swing, where the bob is moving fastest. (KE = ½mv²)
In the simulation, watch the ■ Potential and ■ Kinetic energy bars. With no damping, their sum remains constant.
