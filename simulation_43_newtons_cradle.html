Newton's Cradle

A classic demonstration of conservation of momentum and energy.

Plain-Language Overview

Newton's Cradle is a classic desk toy that elegantly demonstrates some deep principles of physics. When you lift and release a ball on one end, it strikes the row, and a single ball emerges from the other side with the same speed. If you lift two balls, two balls fly out. Why does this happen? This interactive simulation lets you play with a virtual Newton's Cradle to see how the laws of conservation of momentum and conservation of energy work together to produce this clean, satisfying transfer of motion.

Beginner's Guide — The Domino Effect of Collisions

Step 1: The Setup

The device consists of a row of identical steel balls, each suspended by two strings from a sturdy frame. The bifilar suspension (two strings) is important to keep the balls swinging in a single plane.

Step 2: The Action and Reaction

In the simulation, you can click and drag one or more balls from the end. This lifts them, giving them potential energy. When you release the mouse, this potential energy is converted into kinetic energy as they swing down. They strike the stationary row of balls with a certain velocity and momentum.

Step 3: Two Conservation Laws

The behavior of the cradle is governed by two fundamental laws of physics that must be satisfied during the collisions:

  1. Conservation of Momentum: The total momentum (mass × velocity) of all the balls before the collision must equal the total momentum after the collision.
  2. Conservation of Kinetic Energy: The total kinetic energy (½ × mass × velocity²) is also (nearly) conserved, because the steel balls are very hard and bouncy. This is called an elastic collision.

Step 4: Why One Ball In, One Ball Out?

Let's say you lift one ball. It hits the row with velocity `v`. Why doesn't the last ball fly out with velocity `v`, but instead two balls fly out with velocity `v/2`? Let's check the laws:

  • Momentum: The initial momentum is `m*v`. If two balls fly out, their total mass is `2m` and their speed is `v/2`, so their total momentum is `2m * (v/2) = m*v`. Momentum is conserved! So this is possible, right?
  • Kinetic Energy: The initial KE is `½m*v²`. If two balls fly out, their total KE is `½*(2m)*(v/2)² = ½*(2m)*(v²/4) = ½m*v² * (1/2)`. Kinetic energy is **not** conserved! It was cut in half.
This outcome is physically possible (it happens in an inelastic collision), but it's not what happens here. The *only* way to satisfy both conservation laws simultaneously is for the same number of balls that enter the collision to exit the collision with the same initial speed. The motion is transferred almost instantly through the chain of intermediate balls.

Controls

Click and drag the end balls to lift them, then release.

Idealized Collision Model

The simulation treats the series of collisions as happening instantaneously. When a group of `k` balls moving with angular velocity `ω` strikes the stationary balls, the model does the following:

  1. The `k` incoming balls have their `ω` set to zero.
  2. The last `k` balls on the opposite end of the chain have their `ω` set to the incoming `ω`, multiplied by the elasticity coefficient.
This is a simplified but effective model that correctly reproduces the observed behavior. It implicitly assumes that the only way to conserve both momentum and kinetic energy (for elasticity=1) is for the outgoing motion to mirror the incoming motion.

Pendulum Motion

The swinging motion of the balls is that of a simple pendulum. The force of gravity provides a restoring torque that causes the balls to oscillate. The angular acceleration `α` is given by:

α = -(g / L) * sin(θ)

Where `g` is the acceleration due to gravity, `L` is the length of the string, and `θ` is the angular displacement. This equation is solved numerically for each swinging ball to create the animation.
