The Electroweak Interaction

A visualization of the unification of fundamental forces.

Overview

In our everyday, low-energy world, we experience two distinct fundamental forces: the electromagnetic force, which governs light and magnetism, and the weak nuclear force, which governs radioactive decay. However, the Standard Model of particle physics reveals a deeper truth: at extremely high energies, such as those present moments after the Big Bang, these two forces are not distinct at all. They merge, or "unify," into a single electroweak force. The difference we see today is an illusion created by a process called spontaneous symmetry breaking, mediated by the Higgs field. This simulation visualizes this unification as you increase the energy of an interaction.


Energy: 1 GeV — Forces are Distinct

From Two Forces to One

The key players in this story are the force-carrying particles, or bosons:

  • Electromagnetism is carried by the massless photon (γ).
  • The Weak Force is carried by three very massive particles: the W⁺, W⁻, and Z⁰ bosons.

Low Energy (Our World)

At low energies, the Higgs field gives mass to the W and Z bosons, making them very heavy (around 80-90 GeV/c²). This large mass makes the weak force extremely short-ranged and appear very different from the long-ranged electromagnetic force with its massless photon. In the simulation, you see four distinct particles with different properties.

High Energy (The Early Universe)

As you increase the energy slider past the "electroweak scale" of about 100 GeV, the underlying symmetry of nature is restored. In this high-energy environment, the Higgs mechanism no longer gives mass to the W and Z bosons. They become massless, just like the photon.

In this state, the four particles are indistinguishable and are all part of a single, unified electroweak interaction. They are often referred to as W¹, W², W³, and B⁰, which then mix to form the photon, W, and Z particles we see at low energies. The simulation visualizes this by having the massive particle icons "transform" and merge into a single family of four identical, massless, wavy-line icons, showing that the distinction between them has vanished.
