Brachytherapy Planning Simulation

A simplified tool for placing radioactive seeds to treat a tumor.

One-Paragraph Overview

Brachytherapy is a powerful form of internal radiation therapy where tiny, radioactive "seeds" are implanted directly inside or next to a tumor. This clever technique allows doctors to deliver a very high, concentrated dose of radiation to the cancerous cells while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissue. This simulation provides a simplified 3D planning environment for treating a prostate tumor. You can place radioactive seeds using a virtual grid, and in real-time, you'll see the resulting "dose cloud" and a Dose-Volume Histogram (DVH)—a critical clinical tool. Your goal is to arrange the seeds to completely cover the target volume with a sufficient dose, just as a radiation oncologist would.

Beginner's Guide — Planning an Internal Radiation Treatment

Step 1: The Goal

The main objective in radiation therapy is to deliver a lethal dose of radiation to a tumor while sparing the surrounding healthy organs as much as possible. In this simulation, our target is the prostate gland, shown as a semi-transparent red shape.

Step 2: The Tools - Radioactive Seeds

Instead of firing a radiation beam from outside the body, brachytherapy involves implanting tiny radioactive sources, or "seeds," directly into the prostate. These seeds emit radiation that travels only a short distance. This is powerful because the highest dose is naturally concentrated right where you need it.

Step 3: Placing the Seeds

In the 3D view, you'll see a blue grid. This represents the template a doctor uses to guide needles for implanting the seeds. You can:

  • Rotate the 3D view by clicking and dragging.
  • Move the grid up and down through the prostate using the "Axial Slice" slider.
  • Place a seed by clicking on a grid point within the prostate outline.
As you place seeds, you'll see a blue "dose cloud" appear and grow. This cloud represents the region that is receiving a certain level of radiation dose. You can change which dose level you see with the "Isodose Surface" slider.

Step 4: Evaluating the Plan - The DVH

How do we know if our plan is any good? Looking at the 3D cloud is helpful, but it's not precise. The gold standard is the Dose-Volume Histogram (DVH), shown in the bottom plot.

  • The X-axis is the dose in Grays (Gy).
  • The Y-axis is the percentage of the prostate's volume.
The curve answers the question: "What percentage of the prostate (Y) received at least a certain dose (X)?" A perfect plan would be a straight line across at 100% volume until it hits the prescribed dose (e.g., 145 Gy), then drop straight down to 0. Our goal is to create a plan where the curve is as close to that "L" shape as possible.

Step 5: Key Metrics

Doctors use specific points on the DVH curve to quickly judge a plan. The most important are:

  • V100: The percentage of the prostate Volume receiving at least 100% of the prescribed dose. We want this to be as close to 100% as possible (e.g., > 95%).
  • D90: The minimum Dose received by 90% of the prostate volume. We want this to be at or above the prescribed dose.
Watch these metrics in the control panel update as you add seeds. Try to create a plan with a good V100 and D90!

Core Theory — The Physics of Dose Deposition

1. The Inverse Square Law

The dose delivered by a single, small radioactive source follows the inverse square law. The dose rate is proportional to the inverse of the square of the distance `r` from the source.

Dose Rate ∝ 1 / r²

This rapid fall-off is what makes brachytherapy so effective. A point 2 cm away from a seed receives only 1/4 of the dose as a point 1 cm away. This allows for a very high dose inside the tumor and a much lower dose to nearby healthy organs.

2. Total Dose Calculation

For this simulation, we simplify the physics. The total dose at any point in space is the sum of the doses contributed by all the seeds that have been placed. For each seed `i`, its contribution to a point `P` is:

Dose(P) = Σ [ (Activity * C) / distance(P, seed_i)² ]

Where `Activity` is the strength of the seed and `C` is a constant that includes factors like the isotope type and total decay time. In a real treatment planning system, this calculation is much more complex, accounting for tissue absorption and scattering (the AAPM TG-43 formalism).

3. Dose Cloud Visualization (Ray Marching)

To render the 3D dose cloud, this simulation uses a technique called ray marching inside the GPU's fragment shader. For each pixel on the screen, a ray is cast through the 3D scene. The shader then "marches" along the ray in small steps. At each step, it calculates the total dose from all seeds. If the dose is above the selected isodose threshold, it adds a small amount of color and opacity to the pixel. By accumulating these values along the entire ray, we get a volumetric rendering of the dose cloud, all calculated in real-time.

4. Generating the Dose-Volume Histogram (DVH)

The DVH is generated by a statistical sampling method.

  1. A large number of random points (e.g., 5000) are generated inside the 3D shape of the prostate.
  2. For each point, the total dose from all placed seeds is calculated using the formula above.
  3. This gives us a list of 5000 dose values.
  4. We then create a histogram from this list: we count how many points received a dose between 0-5 Gy, 5-10 Gy, and so on.
  5. From this histogram, we can calculate the cumulative DVH curve shown in the plot, which tells us what percentage of points received a dose greater than or equal to a given value.
