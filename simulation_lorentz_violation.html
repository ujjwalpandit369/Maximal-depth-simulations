Lorentz Symmetry Violation

A conceptual race between photons of different energies.

Overview

One of the pillars of modern physics is Lorentz Invariance—the idea that the laws of physics, particularly the speed of light, are the same for all observers. However, some theories of quantum gravity speculate that this symmetry might be broken at incredibly high energies. If this were true, the vacuum of space itself could act like a refractive medium, causing photons to travel at slightly different speeds depending on their energy. This simulation visualizes this effect by staging a race between a low-energy photon and a high-energy gamma-ray photon from a distant cosmic explosion. Will they arrive at the same time, or will one pull ahead?

Beginner's Guide to a Cosmic Race

1. The Unchanging Speed of Light

Einstein's theory of relativity is built on a surprising fact: the speed of light in a vacuum is constant. It doesn't matter if you're standing still or flying in a rocket at half the speed of light; you will always measure a light beam as moving at the same speed, c. This principle, known as Lorentz Invariance, has been tested and confirmed to incredible precision. It's a fundamental rule of the universe as we know it.

2. A "Foamy" Spacetime

At the smallest possible scales—the Planck scale, far smaller than an atom—some physicists believe spacetime is not smooth. They imagine it as a "quantum foam," a turbulent sea of virtual particles and tiny, fleeting gravitational fluctuations. If spacetime is granular like this, it might interact with particles that travel through it.

Think of the difference between running on a paved track versus running through a dense forest. On the track (normal spacetime), your speed is your own. In the forest (quantum foam), you are constantly interacting with trees. A very small, nimble runner (a low-energy photon) might zip through easily, while a larger runner (a high-energy photon) might get slowed down more by bumping into things.

3. The Race from a Gamma-Ray Burst

Gamma-Ray Bursts (GRBs) are the most powerful explosions in the universe. They occur billions of light-years away and release photons across a huge range of energies. This makes them perfect natural laboratories for testing Lorentz Invariance. If the theory is violated, then high-energy gamma rays and low-energy radio waves from the same burst should travel at slightly different speeds. Over billions of years of travel, this tiny speed difference would add up, causing them to arrive at Earth at different times.

4. What the Simulation Shows

This simulation visualizes a simplified version of this cosmic race.

  • The blue dot is a low-energy photon. It always travels at the "normal" speed of light, `c`.
  • The purple dot is a high-energy photon. Its speed is affected by the spacetime "foam".
  • The Lorentz Violation Factor slider controls how strong this effect is. A value of 0 means no violation, and both photons arrive at the same time. A higher value means the high-energy photon is slowed down more, arriving later.

Press Run Race to see them go. The simulation will record their arrival times and show you the difference, demonstrating how a tiny change in the laws of physics can have a measurable effect over cosmic distances.
