Overview

Why does a compass needle always point north? It's because it's trying to reach its lowest energy state. This simulation explores the potential energy of a magnetic dipole (like a compass needle) in a uniform magnetic field. By rotating the needle, you can see how its potential energy changes based on its alignment with the field. The accompanying graph plots this energy, showing that the lowest energy state is when the needle is aligned with the field, and the highest is when it's anti-aligned. You'll also see a visualization of the torque—the twisting force—that the field exerts on the needle, constantly trying to pull it back into alignment.

Beginner's Guide to Magnetic Alignment

Let's break down the physics behind a simple compass.

Step 1: The Dipole and the Field

  • The Compass Needle: This is a "magnetic dipole," a small magnet with a North pole (red) and a South pole (blue). Its magnetic strength is called its "dipole moment (μ)".
  • The Magnetic Field (B): The parallel green lines represent a uniform magnetic field, like the Earth's magnetic field in a small area. The arrows show its direction.

Step 2: Potential Energy (U)

The potential energy of the compass depends entirely on its angle (θ) relative to the magnetic field. The graph below the compass shows this relationship.

  • Lowest Energy (Aligned): When the needle's North pole points in the same direction as the field (θ=0°), it is perfectly aligned. This is the most stable, lowest-energy state. It's at the bottom of the "potential well" on the graph.
  • Highest Energy (Anti-aligned): When the needle points in the opposite direction (θ=180°), it is in a very unstable, high-energy state. It's at the peak of the hill on the graph.
  • Zero Energy (Perpendicular): By convention, the energy is defined as zero when the needle is perpendicular to the field (θ=90°).

As you rotate the needle, watch the white dot move along the energy curve.

Step 3: Torque (τ) - The Twisting Force

The magnetic field exerts a twisting force, or torque, on the needle, trying to pull it back to its lowest energy state (alignment). The orange arrow on the needle visualizes this torque.

  • The torque is strongest when the needle is at 90°, where it has the most "leverage" to be twisted. This corresponds to the steepest part of the energy graph.
  • The torque is zero when the needle is perfectly aligned (0°) or anti-aligned (180°). This corresponds to the bottom and top of the energy curve, where the slope is flat.

How to Use the Simulation

  1. Rotate the Needle: Click and drag the tip of the compass needle to change its angle.
  2. Watch the Graph: See how the potential energy changes on the graph below.
  3. Observe the Torque: Notice how the orange torque arrow grows and shrinks as you rotate the needle.
  4. Release to Settle: Let go of the needle, and it will oscillate back and forth before settling into its lowest energy state, just like a real compass.
  5. Use the Sliders: Increase the Magnetic Field Strength (B) or the Dipole Moment (μ) to see how this makes the potential well deeper and the restoring torque stronger.

Core Theory: Energy and Torque

The physics is described by two simple equations relating the potential energy U and the torque τ to the dipole moment vector μ, the magnetic field vector B, and the angle θ between them.

1. Potential Energy

U = -μ ⋅ B = -μB cos(θ)

The energy is the negative dot product of the two vectors. It is minimized (-μB) when they are parallel (cos(0)=1) and maximized (+μB) when they are anti-parallel (cos(180)=-1).

2. Torque

τ = μ × B

The torque is the cross product of the two vectors. Its magnitude is given by `τ = μB sin(θ)`. This shows the torque is maximum when the vectors are perpendicular (sin(90)=1) and zero when they are parallel or anti-parallel (sin(0)=0, sin(180)=0).
