Precession of Mercury's Perihelion

A classic test of General Relativity.

Plain-Language Overview

According to Isaac Newton's law of gravity, a planet orbiting a star should trace a perfect, closed ellipse, returning to the same spot every time. For centuries, this model worked perfectly for almost every planet. The one exception was Mercury. Astronomers observed that Mercury's orbit wasn't perfectly closed; its closest point to the Sun (the "perihelion") was slowly shifting with each orbit. This tiny anomaly couldn't be explained by Newtonian physics. It was a major mystery until Einstein's General Relativity provided the answer. GR predicts this exact precession, not as a flaw, but as a natural consequence of motion in the curved spacetime near a massive object. This simulation lets you compare the two models side-by-side.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Newtonian Orbit

Select "Newtonian Gravity" and press "Start/Reset." You will see a planet trace a perfect, unchanging ellipse around the central star. The path is drawn over and over on top of itself. This is the orbit predicted by Newton's universal law of gravitation (the famous inverse-square law). For most situations in our solar system, this model is incredibly accurate.

2. The General Relativistic Orbit

Now, select "General Relativity" and press "Start/Reset." At first, the orbit looks the same. But as the simulation runs, you will notice that the entire ellipse is slowly rotating, or precessing. The point of closest approach (the perihelion) is no longer fixed in space. Over many orbits, this creates a beautiful rosette or flower-like pattern. This precession is a tiny correction to Newton's model, caused by the way mass curves spacetime in General Relativity. The effect is most pronounced for objects that are very close to a very massive star and have a highly elliptical (non-circular) orbit, which is why it was first observed for Mercury.

3. Using the Controls

  • Star's Mass: Increasing the mass of the star makes the gravitational field stronger and will increase the speed of the planet and the rate of precession in the GR model.
  • Orbital Eccentricity: This slider controls how "squashed" the ellipse is. An eccentricity of 0 is a perfect circle, while a value close to 1 is a very long, thin ellipse. You will find that the precession effect is much more noticeable for highly eccentric orbits, as the planet dips deeper into the star's gravity well at its closest approach.

A Triumph for General Relativity

In the 19th century, the French astronomer Urbain Le Verrier, who had famously predicted the existence of Neptune from irregularities in Uranus's orbit, tackled the problem of Mercury. He calculated that Mercury's perihelion precessed by about 574 arcseconds per century. The gravitational tugs of all the other planets could account for about 531 arcseconds of this. This left a stubborn discrepancy of 43 arcseconds per century that could not be explained.

Scientists proposed many solutions, from a cloud of dust near the sun to a hypothetical planet named "Vulcan" orbiting even closer. None of these were found. The problem remained unsolved until 1915, when Albert Einstein applied his newly formulated theory of General Relativity to the problem. His equations predicted an additional precession of exactly 43 arcseconds per century, perfectly matching the observations without any new planets or fudge factors. It was one of the first and most powerful confirmations of his theory and a major blow to Newtonian physics.

The GR Correction

The motion of a planet in Newtonian gravity is governed by the inverse-square law force: F = -GMm/r². In General Relativity, there is no "force." The planet follows a geodesic in curved spacetime. For orbits, this can be *approximated* by adding a small correction term to the Newtonian force. This effective force looks something like:

F(r) ≈ -GMm/r² - 3GMh²/(c²r⁴)

Where h is the planet's specific angular momentum and c is the speed of light. That tiny extra term, which is proportional to 1/r⁴, is what breaks the perfect symmetry of the Newtonian ellipse and causes the orbit to precess. This simulation uses a simplified version of this corrected force law when in "General Relativity" mode.
