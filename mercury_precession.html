Precession of Mercury's Perihelion

A classic test of General Relativity.

Plain-Language Overview

According to Newton's law of gravity, a planet should trace a perfect, closed ellipse. For centuries, this model worked perfectly, except for Mercury. Astronomers observed that Mercury's orbit wasn't closed; its closest point to the Sun (the "perihelion") was slowly shifting. This tiny anomaly couldn't be explained until Einstein's General Relativity predicted this exact precession as a natural consequence of motion in curved spacetime. This simulation lets you compare the two models side-by-side.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Newtonian Orbit

Select "Newtonian Gravity" and press "Start/Reset." You will see a planet trace a perfect, unchanging ellipse, drawn over and over on top of itself. This is the orbit predicted by Newton's inverse-square law.

2. The General Relativistic Orbit

Select "General Relativity" and press "Start/Reset." You will notice the entire ellipse slowly rotating, or precessing. The point of closest approach is no longer fixed. This creates a rosette pattern. This effect is most pronounced for objects close to a massive star with a highly elliptical orbit, like Mercury.

Historical Context

In the 19th century, astronomers calculated that Mercury's perihelion precessed by 574 arcseconds per century. The gravitational tugs of other planets accounted for 531 of these, leaving a stubborn discrepancy of 43 arcseconds per century. In 1915, Einstein's General Relativity predicted an additional precession of exactly 43 arcseconds per century, a major triumph for his theory.

Core Theory

The motion of a planet in Newtonian gravity is governed by the force: F = -GMm/r². In General Relativity, this is approximated by adding a small correction term: F(r) ≈ -GMm/r² - 3GMh²/(c²r⁴), where `h` is the specific angular momentum. That tiny extra term, proportional to 1/r⁴, breaks the perfect symmetry of the Newtonian ellipse and causes the orbit to precess.
