Overview

Chemical reactions are all about energy changes. This simulation provides a visual map for the energy journey from reactants to products, called a Potential Energy Diagram. You can control the two most important factors of a reaction: the overall energy change (Enthalpy, ΔH) and the energy barrier that must be overcome for the reaction to happen (Activation Energy, Ea). Discover the difference between reactions that release heat (exothermic) and those that absorb it (endothermic). You can also add a catalyst to see how it speeds up a reaction by providing a "shortcut" over the energy hill, without changing the starting or ending energy levels.

Beginner's Guide to Reaction Energy

Let's explore the energetic landscape of a chemical reaction. It's like a story with a beginning, an ending, and a big hill in the middle.

Step 1: The Main Characters - Reactants and Products

Every reaction starts with Reactants (the ingredients) and ends with Products (the result). On our diagram, the flat line on the left is the energy level of the reactants, and the flat line on the right is the energy level of the products.

Step 2: The Overall Energy Change (Enthalpy, ΔH)

The difference in energy between the products and reactants is called the Enthalpy Change, or ΔH. Use the "Enthalpy Change (ΔH)" slider to control this.

  • If you move the slider to a negative value, the products are at a lower energy level than the reactants. The reaction releases energy (usually as heat). This is called an EXOTHERMIC reaction. The ΔH label will turn blue.
  • If you move the slider to a positive value, the products are at a higher energy level. The reaction must absorb energy from its surroundings to proceed. This is called an ENDOTHERMIC reaction. The ΔH label will turn red.

Step 3: The Energy Hill (Activation Energy, Ea)

For a reaction to happen, the reactants don't just turn into products. They first have to overcome an energy barrier. Think of it as needing a "push" to get started. This push is the Activation Energy (Ea). It's the height of the hill from the reactant's energy level to the peak.

A high activation energy means a slow reaction, because few molecules have enough energy to make it over the big hill. A low activation energy means a fast reaction. Use the "Activation Energy (Ea)" slider to change the height of this hill.

Step 4: The Role of a Catalyst

A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction. How does it do this? It provides a different, easier pathway for the reaction to take—one with a lower activation energy.

Click the "Add Catalyst" button. A new, dashed green line will appear. Notice two crucial things:

  1. The hill for the catalyzed path is lower. The reaction is now faster.
  2. The starting (reactant) and ending (product) energy levels are exactly the same. A catalyst makes the journey easier, but it doesn't change the starting point or the destination.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. What is the "Transition State"?
The very peak of the energy hill is called the transition state. It's a highly unstable, fleeting arrangement of atoms that is halfway between being reactants and being products.
2. Why does the Activation Energy have to be positive?
A chemical bond needs to be broken or weakened for a reaction to start, and this always requires an input of energy. You can't have a "negative" energy hill. If Ea was zero, the reaction would happen instantly.
3. Is the catalyst used up in the reaction?
No. A key property of a catalyst is that it participates in the reaction to lower the energy hill, but it is regenerated at the end. It's not a reactant or a product.
4. What does kJ/mol mean?
It stands for "kilojoules per mole." It's a standard unit of energy in chemistry. A mole is just a very large, specific number of molecules (about 6.022 x 10²³).
5. Can I make the Activation Energy lower than the Enthalpy Change?
Only if the enthalpy change is negative (exothermic). The energy hill must always be taller than the product's energy level. The simulation prevents impossible combinations.
