The Butterfly Effect in Action

A single pendulum swings back and forth in a simple, predictable way. But what happens if you attach a second pendulum to the end of the first? You get a double pendulum, a system so exquisitely sensitive that it becomes a beautiful demonstration of Chaos Theory.

Step 1: Set the Stage

The simulation starts paused. The main panel shows the double pendulum. You can change its physical properties, like the masses of the bobs and the lengths of the arms, using the sliders.

Step 2: Your First Launch

Click and drag the two red bobs to any starting position you like. When you release the mouse, the simulation starts. The pendulum begins its wild, unpredictable dance. The blue line traces the chaotic path of the second bob. Notice how it never seems to exactly repeat itself.

Step 3: Launch a Clone

This is the most important part of the simulation. Click "Reset", set the pendulum to a new starting position, and release it. Let it swing for a second or two. Now, click the "Launch Clone" button.

A second, transparent "ghost" pendulum appears. It starts in the exact same position as the first one, but with one of its angles changed by a minuscule, invisible amount (0.001 radians).

At first, the two pendulums will swing in perfect sync. They look like a single pendulum. But wait. After a few seconds, you'll see the clone start to separate from the original. Soon after, their paths will be completely different and uncorrelated.

The Essence of Chaos

This is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, famously known as the "butterfly effect." It means that even an infinitesimally small change in the starting state of a chaotic system will lead to enormous, unpredictable differences in its future state. This is why we can't predict the weather perfectly more than a week or so in advance—the atmosphere is a chaotic system, just like the double pendulum.

The energy plot at the bottom shows the constant, complex exchange between kinetic energy (energy of motion) and potential energy (stored energy due to height) in the system. With damping, you'll see the total energy slowly decrease over time.
