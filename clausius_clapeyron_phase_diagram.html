Welcome to the Phase Explorer!

Water can be ice (solid), liquid water, or steam (gas). Which form it takes depends on its Pressure and Temperature. A Phase Diagram is a map that shows the state of a substance at any given P-T combination.

Step 1: Reading the Map

The main panel shows the phase diagram for Water. You'll see three colored regions:

  • Solid (Ice): Found at low temperatures and high pressures.
  • Liquid (Water): The familiar state, in the middle.
  • Gas (Steam): Found at high temperatures and low pressures.
The lines separating these regions are called coexistence curves. On these lines, two phases exist in equilibrium. For example, on the line between liquid and gas, water is boiling.

Step 2: Explore the Curves

Move your mouse over the diagram. When you move over one of the coexistence curves, the information panel on the right will light up.

  • It tells you the Temperature and Pressure at your cursor's location.
  • It shows a green tangent line, which represents the slope of the curve at that exact point.
The slope (written as dP/dT) is very important. It tells us how much the pressure needs to change for a given change in temperature to stay on the line (e.g., to keep water boiling).

Step 3: The Clausius-Clapeyron Equation

A famous thermodynamic equation, the Clausius-Clapeyron relation, predicts this slope. It says:

dP/dT = L / (T · ΔV)
Where L is the Latent Heat (the energy needed for the phase change), T is the temperature, and ΔV is the change in volume during the transition.

Look at the info panel. It shows you the "Measured Slope" from the graph and the "Calculated" value from the equation. Notice how they are always almost identical! This simulation proves the equation works.

Step 4: The Anomaly of Water

Most substances get denser when they freeze. Water is special: ice is less dense than liquid water (which is why ice cubes float). This has a fascinating consequence.

Move your mouse over the boundary between the Solid (ice) and Liquid (water) phases. Look at the slope. It's negative! It goes down and to the right. Why?

In the equation, when ice melts into water, the change in volume (ΔV) is negative (it shrinks). A negative ΔV makes the whole right side of the equation negative, so the slope dP/dT must also be negative. This is a unique feature of water.

Step 5: Compare with Carbon Dioxide

Now, click the "Carbon Dioxide" button. Its phase diagram looks different. Most notably, move your mouse over its solid-liquid boundary. The slope is positive, as it is for most substances. This is because solid CO₂ (dry ice) is denser than liquid CO₂.

The Clausius-Clapeyron Relation

The Clausius-Clapeyron relation is a fundamental equation in thermodynamics that describes a discontinuous phase transition between two phases of matter of a single constituent. On a pressure-temperature (P-T) diagram, the line separating the two phases is known as the coexistence curve.

Equation:

dP / dT = L / (T ΔV)

Where:

  • dP/dT is the slope of the coexistence curve.
  • L is the specific latent heat of the transition (e.g., heat of vaporization or fusion). This is the energy required to change the phase of a unit mass of the substance.
  • T is the temperature at which the transition occurs, in Kelvin.
  • ΔV is the change in specific volume (volume per unit mass) during the phase transition.

Derivation Insight

The relation is derived from the fact that the chemical potential (or specific Gibbs free energy, g) of the two phases must be equal all along the coexistence curve: `gₐ(P,T) = gₑ(P,T)`. By considering a small change along this curve, `dgₐ = dgₑ`, and using the Gibbs-Duhem relation `dg = -s dT + v dP` (where s is specific entropy and v is specific volume), we arrive at the Clausius-Clapeyron equation. The relation `L = T Δs` is used to substitute entropy (s) with latent heat (L).

Application: Water's Negative Slope

For the melting of ice into liquid water:

  • `L` (latent heat of fusion) is positive (energy is required to melt ice).
  • `T` is positive (absolute temperature).
  • `ΔV = V_liquid - V_solid`. Since ice is less dense than water, its specific volume is greater. Thus, `V_liquid < V_solid`, which makes `ΔV` negative.
Therefore, `dP/dT = (+) / ((+) · (-))`, which results in a negative slope. This means that if you increase the pressure on ice, its melting point actually decreases. This phenomenon is responsible for the movement of glaciers and is why ice skating works (though modern understanding suggests frictional heating is a larger factor).
