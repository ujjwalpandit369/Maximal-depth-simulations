Overview

Why does a piece of metal glow red, then orange, then white as it gets hotter? The answer lies in blackbody radiation, the light emitted by any object due to its temperature. This simulation plots the spectrum of this radiation, showing its intensity at different wavelengths. The primary control is the object's temperature. As you increase the temperature, you'll see two key changes, first described by Planck's Law: the object emits more energy at all wavelengths, and the peak of the spectrum shifts to shorter, bluer wavelengths. This shift is described by Wien's Displacement Law, which you can verify yourself using the simulation's readouts.

Beginner's Guide to Thermal Radiation

Let's look at the light emitted by hot objects.

Step 1: What is a Blackbody?

In physics, a "blackbody" is a perfect absorber and emitter of radiation. It doesn't reflect any light. The light it gives off is purely a product of its own temperature. Stars, a glowing poker, or the filament in a lightbulb are all good approximations of a blackbody.

Step 2: The Radiation Spectrum

The graph shows the "spectrum" of the emitted light. The horizontal axis is the wavelength (λ) of the light. The colored band shows the part of the spectrum that is visible to the human eye. The vertical axis is the intensity, or how bright the light is at that specific wavelength.

The curve shows that a hot object doesn't just emit one color, but a whole range of them. The "peak" of the curve is the wavelength where the object is emitting the most light.

Step 3: The Effect of Temperature (The Main Event)

Use the temperature slider to see how the spectrum changes. This is the most important part of the simulation.

  • Getting Brighter: As you increase the temperature, the entire curve gets much taller. This means hotter objects radiate significantly more energy. This is known as the Stefan-Boltzmann law (total energy is proportional to T⁴).
  • Changing Color (Wien's Law): As you increase the temperature, the peak of the curve shifts to the left, towards shorter wavelengths. This is Wien's Displacement Law. A relatively cool object (like a 3000 K star) will have its peak in the red or infrared, appearing reddish. A very hot object (like a 10,000 K star) will have its peak in the blue or ultraviolet, appearing blue-white. The color swatch next to the temperature slider shows the approximate color your eye would see.

Step 4: Verifying Wien's Law

Wien's Law states that the peak wavelength (λmax) multiplied by the temperature (T) is always equal to a constant value (`λ_max * T = b`). Check the readouts below the graph. As you change the temperature, you will see λmax change, but the value for "Wien's Constant Check" should remain the same, proving the law!
