Geophysics: The Milankovitch Cycles

The astronomical pacemaker of Earth's ice ages.

Plain-Language Overview

Earth's climate has naturally cycled between long ice ages and shorter warm periods for millions of years. The "pacemaker" for these cycles is not on Earth, but in space. The Milankovitch cycles are three slow, predictable changes in Earth's orbit and orientation relative to the Sun. These cycles—eccentricity, axial tilt, and precession—subtly alter the amount and distribution of sunlight reaching Earth, especially at the high latitudes where ice sheets grow. This simulation visualizes these three cycles and shows how their combined effect on solar radiation correlates with the timing of past ice ages.

Beginner's Guide — Earth's Orbital Dance

Use the time slider to see how three long-term cycles change Earth's relationship with the Sun.

1. Eccentricity (The Shape of the Orbit)

This cycle describes how the shape of Earth's orbit changes from being nearly circular to more elliptical (oval-shaped). This happens on a long cycle of about 100,000 years. A more elliptical orbit means that the Earth's distance from the Sun varies more throughout the year, which slightly changes the total amount of solar energy received. In the simulation, you can see the orbit "stretch" and relax.

2. Obliquity (The Tilt of the Axis)

This cycle is the change in the tilt of Earth's axis of rotation, which varies between about 22.1° and 24.5°. The cycle takes about 41,000 years. Our seasons are caused by this tilt. A greater tilt means more extreme seasons—hotter summers and colder winters. A smaller tilt means milder seasons for everyone. This is a crucial factor, as cool summers in the Northern Hemisphere are thought to be the key to starting an ice age, as they allow winter snow to survive and accumulate into ice sheets.

3. Precession (The Wobble of the Axis)

This is the slow "wobble" of Earth's axis, much like a spinning top that is slowing down. This cycle takes about 26,000 years. Precession determines which season occurs when the Earth is closest to the Sun (perihelion). Currently, the Northern Hemisphere experiences winter when it's closest to the Sun, leading to milder winters. In about 13,000 years, this will be reversed, and the Northern Hemisphere will have summer when it's closest to the Sun, leading to more extreme seasons.

The Combined Effect: Insolation

None of these cycles is powerful enough on its own to cause an ice age. But their effects combine and interfere with each other over time. The graph at the bottom of the simulation shows the calculated result of all three cycles on the amount of solar radiation (insolation) received during the summer at a high northern latitude (65°N). You can see that the dips in this insolation curve line up remarkably well with the periods of past ice ages (shown as the blue shaded regions), confirming that these astronomical cycles are the primary driver of Earth's long-term climate.
