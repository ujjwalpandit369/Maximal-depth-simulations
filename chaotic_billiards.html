Predictable vs. Chaotic Motion

Imagine hitting a billiard ball. On a normal rectangular table, you can predict with high accuracy where the ball will go. The motion is regular. But if we change the shape of the table slightly, the motion can become completely unpredictable, or chaotic.

Step 1: Regular Motion

Start with the "Rectangle" or "Circle" table. These are regular, non-chaotic shapes. Click on the table to place a ball, drag your mouse to aim, and release to launch it. The ball follows a predictable path.

Now, click "Launch Twin". This launches a second, blue ball from the same starting point, but with its angle changed by a tiny, almost imperceptible amount. Notice how the two paths stay close together for a very long time. A small error in your aim doesn't cause a huge difference in the outcome.

Step 2: Chaotic Motion

Now switch to the "Stadium" table. A stadium is just a rectangle with two semicircles attached to the ends. This seemingly small change has dramatic consequences. Launch a single ball and watch its path. It seems much more complex and space-filling.

This is where it gets interesting. Click "Clear Traces", launch a new red ball, and then immediately click "Launch Twin".

The two balls start out together, but after just a few bounces off the curved walls, their paths will diverge wildly! Within seconds, their positions will be completely different. This is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the hallmark of chaos. Any tiny error in the initial angle is rapidly magnified by the curved boundaries.

Step 3: The Sinai Billiard

The "Sinai Billiard" is another famous chaotic system. It's just a square table with a circular obstacle in the middle. Try the "Launch Twin" experiment here as well. You'll see the same exponential divergence. The circular obstacle acts as a "scatterer" that makes the system chaotic. These simple tabletop models are fundamental to physics for studying the transition from order to chaos.
