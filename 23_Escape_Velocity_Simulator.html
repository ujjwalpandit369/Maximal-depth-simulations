Plain-Language Overview

If you throw a ball into the air, it comes back down. If you throw it harder, it goes higher but still comes back down. But what if you could throw it *hard enough*? There is a certain speed, called the **escape velocity**, at which an object has just enough energy to overcome a planet's gravitational pull and never fall back. This simulation lets you launch rockets from a planet of your own design. By adjusting the planet's mass and the rocket's launch speed, you can discover this critical speed for yourself and see the dramatic difference between a rocket that falls back to its doom and one that successfully escapes to the stars.

Beginner's Guide: Throwing a Rock Really, Really Hard

Step 1: The Pull of Gravity

Gravity is a force that pulls objects toward each other. The strength of this pull depends on the mass of the objects and the distance between them. For a planet, its gravity is strongest at the surface and gets weaker as you move farther away. When you launch a rocket, it's a constant battle between the rocket's upward motion and the planet's downward pull.

Step 2: The Launch

In this simulation, we give the rocket a single, powerful "kick" at the very beginning (its initial launch speed). We are not simulating a real rocket that has its engines on for a long time; we are just exploring the "what if" scenario of having a certain speed at the surface. Use the sliders to set up your planet and choose a launch speed, then press **"Launch"**.

Step 3: Three Possible Fates

Depending on the launch speed you choose, one of three things will happen:

  1. Below Escape Velocity: The rocket travels upwards, but gravity continuously slows it down. Eventually, its speed drops to zero, and the planet's gravity pulls it back down to the surface. It fails to escape.
  2. Exactly Escape Velocity: This is the "Goldilocks" speed. The rocket has *just* enough energy to overcome gravity. It will travel away forever, continuously slowing down but never stopping or falling back. Its speed will approach zero as its distance approaches infinity.
  3. Above Escape Velocity: The rocket has more than enough energy to escape. It will travel away from the planet forever and will still have some leftover speed (kinetic energy) even when it is infinitely far away.
The readout panel will show you the calculated escape velocity for your custom planet. Try launching just below and just above this speed to see the difference!

Step 4: What Determines Escape Velocity?

Play with the **Planet Mass (M)** and **Planet Radius (R)** sliders. You will discover that:

  • A **more massive** planet has stronger gravity, so it requires a much higher speed to escape.
  • A **denser** planet (more mass packed into a smaller radius) also has stronger gravity at its surface, increasing the escape velocity.
Interestingly, the mass of the rocket itself doesn't matter at all! A small probe and a giant spaceship need the exact same speed to escape the same planet.

Core Theory: Conservation of Energy

The concept of escape velocity is derived from the principle of conservation of energy.

1. The total energy E of the rocket is the sum of its kinetic energy (KE) and its gravitational potential energy (PEg):

E = KE + PEg = ½mv² - GMm/r

Where m is the rocket's mass, v is its speed, G is the gravitational constant, M is the planet's mass, and r is the distance from the planet's center.

2. To "escape" means to be able to reach an infinite distance (r → ∞) from the planet. For the *minimum* escape speed, we want the rocket to have just barely run out of energy at that point, meaning its speed v would be 0. If we plug r=∞ and v=0 into the energy equation, we find the total energy of an escaped object is Efinal = 0.

3. By conservation of energy, the total energy at launch must equal this final energy of 0. At launch, the rocket is at the planet's surface (r=R) and has its launch speed, vesc.

Elaunch = ½mvesc² - GMm/R = 0

4. We can now solve this equation for vesc:

½mvesc² = GMm/R

vesc² = 2GM/R

vesc = √(2GM/R)

Notice that the rocket's own mass, m, cancels out completely.
