Slicing Up Spacetime (and other things)

In mathematics, a manifold is a type of space that, if you zoom in far enough, looks like familiar Euclidean space (like a flat plane or 3D space). The surface of a sphere is a 2D manifold; it's curved globally, but any small patch looks flat.

A foliation of a manifold is a way of "slicing" it up into a stack of smaller-dimensional manifolds, called "leaves." Think of it like a deck of cards: the whole deck is a 3D object, but it's composed of a stack of 2D leaves (the cards). Or think of the grain in a piece of wood.

Example 1: The Simple Foliation

Select the "Simple Foliation of a Cube" view. Here, our 3D manifold is the cube. We are foliating it with a family of 2D leaves. Use the "Sweep through leaves" slider. You are moving a single leaf (a 2D plane) through the cube. This is the simplest possible example: slicing a 3D space with parallel planes.

Example 2: The Reeb Foliation Analogy

Now for something much stranger. The Reeb foliation is a famous way to foliate a 3-sphere (the 3D surface of a 4D ball). We can't see this directly, so this simulation shows a common analogy inside a solid torus (a donut).

Select the "Reeb Foliation Analogy" view. The transparent donut is our manifold. The leaves are the surfaces inside.

  • Use the slider to move from the outer edge towards the center. The leaves are nested surfaces that look like distorted donuts themselves.
  • Right in the middle (at slider value 50), there is a single, special leaf: a flat torus.
  • As you move the slider away from the center, the leaves "unroll" and spiral outwards, eventually becoming the boundary of the solid torus itself.
This demonstrates a much more complex way a space can be sliced up. The leaves are all 2D surfaces, but they curve and twist around each other, and there is one special leaf (the central torus) that is topologically different from the others nearby. This has deep implications in fields like dynamical systems and topology.
