Plain-Language Overview

Nuclear fusion, the process that powers the Sun, promises a source of clean, virtually limitless energy. The challenge is recreating the Sun's core here on Earth. A tokamak is a leading design for a fusion reactor that aims to do just that. It uses a powerful, donut-shaped magnetic field to trap and squeeze a plasma of hydrogen isotopes, heating it to over 100 million degrees Celsius—many times hotter than the Sun—to trigger fusion reactions. This conceptual simulation shows a cross-section of a tokamak, visualizing how the magnetic "bottle" confines the hot plasma, keeping it away from the reactor walls.

Beginner's Guide — A Magnetic Bottle

Step 1: The Fuel - Plasma

The fuel for a fusion reactor is typically a mixture of two isotopes of hydrogen: Deuterium and Tritium. To get them to fuse, you must heat them to such extreme temperatures that the electrons are stripped away from the nuclei. This creates a **plasma**, an electrically charged gas of ions and electrons.

Step 2: The Problem - Extreme Heat

No physical material can withstand the 100+ million degree temperatures required for fusion. If the hot plasma touches the wall of the reactor, the wall would instantly vaporize, and the plasma would cool down, quenching the reaction. The plasma must be confined without touching anything.

Step 3: The Solution - Magnetic Fields

Since plasma is made of charged particles, its motion can be controlled by magnetic fields. The fundamental principle is that charged particles are forced to spiral around magnetic field lines. A **tokamak** (a Russian acronym for "toroidal chamber with magnetic coils") is a device designed to exploit this. It's a donut-shaped vacuum chamber surrounded by powerful electromagnets.

Step 4: The Helical "Racetrack"

A simple donut-shaped (toroidal) magnetic field isn't quite enough to confine the plasma stably. A tokamak uses a clever combination of two magnetic fields to create a more stable, twisted, corkscrew-like field:

  1. Toroidal Field: Large D-shaped coils wrapped around the donut create a strong field that runs the long way around the torus.
  2. Poloidal Field: A massive electrical current is induced to flow *within the plasma itself*, creating a second magnetic field that circles around the plasma the short way.
The combination of these two fields results in a **helical magnetic field**. The plasma particles are trapped, forced to spiral along these helical field lines like racecars on a perfectly banked, twisting racetrack, keeping them safely away from the walls. In the simulation, you can see the particles following these helical paths.

Fusion Conditions

Fusion Rate

The Lorentz Force

The fundamental reason magnetic fields can confine a plasma is the Lorentz force. The force `F` on a particle with charge `q` moving with velocity `v` in a magnetic field `B` is:

F = q(v × B)

The cross product `×` means that the force is always perpendicular to both the particle's direction of motion and the magnetic field line. This perpendicular force does no work on the particle (it doesn't speed it up or slow it down), but it constantly changes its direction, forcing it into a spiral or helical path around the field line.

The Lawson Criterion and the Triple Product

For a fusion reactor to be successful, it must produce more power than it consumes. The British physicist John D. Lawson established the condition for this, which can be expressed as the "triple product" of plasma density `n`, energy confinement time `τ` (how long the plasma can be held hot), and plasma temperature `T`.

n * τ * T > L

Where `L` is a target value that depends on the specific fusion reaction (for Deuterium-Tritium fusion, it's about 3 x 10²¹ m⁻³·s·keV). The "Fusion Rate" meter in the simulation is a simplified representation of this triple product. As you increase the temperature, density, and magnetic field strength (which improves confinement time), the fusion rate increases, moving closer to the goal of ignition.
