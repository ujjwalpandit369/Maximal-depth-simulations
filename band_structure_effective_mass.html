Overview

In the quantum world of a crystal, an electron behaves very differently than it does in free space. This simulation explores the "band structure" diagram, which is a map of an electron's allowed energy (E) versus its crystal momentum (k). By interacting with this graph, you can uncover two profound properties: the electron's group velocity, which is determined by the slope of the curve, and its effective mass, which is determined by the curve's curvature. Discover how an electron can have zero velocity, or even behave as if it has a negative mass, all based on the shape of its energy band.

Beginner's Guide to Band Structure

This graph might look abstract, but it tells a deep story about how electrons move through materials.

Step 1: The E-k Diagram

The graph shows the allowed energy (E, on the y-axis) for an electron versus its crystal momentum (k, on the x-axis). Crystal momentum is a concept related to the electron's wavelike nature inside the periodic lattice of atoms in a crystal.

The key idea is that the electron is not free; its energy is dictated by the shape of this curve, which is a result of its interaction with the crystal lattice.

Step 2: Group Velocity (The Slope)

The velocity of an electron through the crystal is not constant. It's given by the slope of the E-k diagram at its current state. The simulation draws this slope as a green tangent line.

  • Steep Slope: The electron moves quickly.
  • Flat Slope: At the very bottom or very top of the band, the slope is zero. Here, the electron has zero velocity; it's a standing wave.

As you move the selection point, watch the green line's angle change and see the "Group Velocity" readout update.

Step 3: Effective Mass (The Curvature)

How does an electron accelerate? In a crystal, its inertia is modified by the lattice. We call this its "effective mass" (m*). This property is determined by the curvature of the E-k diagram. The simulation visualizes this with an orange circle.

  • Sharp Curve (Small Circle): At the bottom of the band, the curve is sharp, like a tight parabola. This corresponds to a small, positive effective mass. The electron is "light" and easy to accelerate.
  • Gentle Curve (Large Circle): In the middle of the band, the curve flattens out. The curvature is low, corresponding to a large, positive effective mass. The electron is "heavy" and sluggish.
  • Negative Curvature (Circle opens down): At the top of the band, the curve is bent downwards. This corresponds to a negative effective mass! This bizarre result means that if you push the electron, it accelerates in the opposite direction. This is the origin of the concept of "holes" (positive charge carriers) in semiconductors.

Core Theory: Velocity and Mass from the Band

For an electron in a one-dimensional crystal, its properties are derived from the dispersion relation E(k).

1. Group Velocity

The velocity of the electron as a wave packet is the group velocity, given by the first derivative of the energy with respect to k (in units where ħ=1):

vg = dE/dk

This is the slope of the E-k diagram.

2. Effective Mass

The effective mass m* describes how the electron accelerates in response to an external force. It is defined by the second derivative:

1/m* = d²E/dk² or m* = (d²E/dk²)⁻¹

This means the effective mass is the inverse of the curvature of the E-k diagram. A large curvature implies a small effective mass, and vice-versa.
