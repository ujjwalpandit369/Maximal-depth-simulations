Welcome to a Tale of Two Worlds!

Imagine a skateboarder in a valley. They can ride back and forth, but they can't get to the next valley unless they have enough energy to get over the hill in between. This is the classical world we know. But in the weird world of quantum mechanics, particles can sometimes "tunnel" right through hills! This simulation lets you see both worlds.

Step 1: The Classical World

Make sure you're in "Classical View". The U-shaped curve is the "potential well"—our valley. The blue ball is our "particle" (our skateboarder). The blue horizontal line represents the particle's Total Energy. You can change this with the slider.

Set the energy slider to a low value, like -2, so the blue energy line is below the top of the central "hill". Notice the particle is trapped in one of the two wells. It moves back and forth, but it never has enough energy to get over the barrier and into the other well. This is Classical Trapping.

Now, increase the energy to a high value, like +10, so the blue line is above the central hill. The particle now has enough energy to travel freely across both wells.

Step 2: The Quantum World

Now for the weird part. Click the "Quantum View" button. The classical particle disappears and is replaced by a red wave. This is the particle's wavefunction (ψ), which tells us the probability of finding the particle at any given position.

Set the energy slider back to a low value, like -2. In the classical world, the particle was trapped. But look at the wavefunction! It's a single wave spread across both wells. Even though the particle's energy is less than the barrier height, the wave "leaks" through the center. This means there is a probability of finding the particle on the other side. This is Quantum Tunneling!

Step 3: Split Energles

In the quantum world, energy is "quantized"—it can only take on specific values. For the double well, the two lowest allowed energies are shown as thin red lines (E₀ and E₁). Notice they are very close together. This is called energy splitting. If the two wells were infinitely far apart, they would have the same ground state energy. But because they are close enough for the particle to tunnel, this single level splits into two: a symmetric state (ψ₀, the ground state) and an anti-symmetric state (ψ₁, the first excited state). You can switch between them using the buttons in the "Quantum Controls" section.

This simulation shows a core mystery of quantum mechanics: particles are also waves, and this wavelike nature allows them to do things that seem impossible in our everyday classical world.

The Potential V(x)

The double-well potential is modeled by a quartic polynomial, a common and simple representation.

Equation: `V(x) = a·x⁴ - b·x²`

Where `a` and `b` are positive constants that determine the shape of the wells. The `x⁴` term provides the confinement at large distances, and the `-x²` term creates the dip in the center, which is then pushed up by the `x⁴` term to form the barrier.

Classical Mechanics

A classical particle's total energy `E` is the sum of its kinetic energy (KE) and potential energy `V(x)`.
Equation: `E = KE + V(x) = ½mv² + V(x)`
Classically allowed regions are where `KE ≥ 0`, which means `E ≥ V(x)`. The particle's velocity becomes zero and it turns around at points where `E = V(x)`.

Quantum Mechanics

A quantum particle is described by a wavefunction `ψ(x)` which is a solution to the Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation:

(-ħ²/2m) · (d²ψ/dx²) + V(x)ψ = Eψ

Where `ħ` is the reduced Planck's constant.

  • Solutions `ψ(x)` only exist for specific, discrete energy values `E`, called eigenvalues.
  • The probability of finding the particle at position `x` is given by `|ψ(x)|²`.
  • Tunneling: In regions where `E < V(x)` (classically forbidden), the Schrödinger equation still has real, exponentially decaying solutions. This means `|ψ(x)|²` is non-zero inside the barrier, leading to a finite probability of the particle appearing on the other side.
  • Energy Splitting: The two lowest-energy solutions (`ψ₀` and `ψ₁`) can be approximated by symmetric and anti-symmetric combinations of the ground-state wavefunctions of the individual wells. The energy difference `ΔE = E₁ - E₀` is related to the tunneling probability. A higher or wider barrier leads to a smaller `ΔE`.
