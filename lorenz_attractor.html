The Birth of the Butterfly Effect

In 1963, meteorologist Edward Lorenz was working with a simplified computer model of atmospheric convection. He discovered that a tiny change in his initial conditions—equivalent to rounding a variable from 0.506127 to 0.506—led to a completely different long-term forecast. This was the birth of the "butterfly effect" and the discovery of one of the first and most famous strange attractors.

Step 1: The Phase Space

The 3D space you see is not physical space, but an abstract phase space. Each point in this space represents the complete state of Lorenz's simplified atmosphere model at one instant. The three axes represent the values of the three variables (`x`, `y`, and `z`) in his equations. As the system evolves, the point representing its state moves through this phase space, tracing a path.

Step 2: The Attractor

Click "Reset". A single point starts moving. Watch as it traces a path. The trajectory loops around one side, then might suddenly jump to the other lobe, loop around for a while, and then jump back. It never settles down into a simple orbit, and it never exactly repeats its path. However, the path is not random; it is confined to a specific, bounded region with a beautiful butterfly shape. This shape is the Lorenz Attractor.

Step 3: The Butterfly Effect

Now, let the simulation run for a few seconds. Click the "Launch Second Point" button. A second, blue tracer point will appear, starting infinitesimally close to the first red point.

At first, they travel together, their paths seemingly identical. But watch closely. After a few loops, they will begin to separate. One might jump to the other lobe while the first stays put. Soon, their positions will be completely uncorrelated.

This is a profound demonstration of sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Even the tiniest, unmeasurable difference in the starting state of a chaotic system will lead to exponentially diverging outcomes, making long-term prediction impossible. Yet, despite their divergence, both points will always remain somewhere on the same strange attractor.

Step 4: Play with Physics

Use the sliders to change the parameters of the Lorenz equations. The standard values are `σ=10`, `ρ=28`, `β=8/3`. Chaos is known to emerge for `ρ > 24.74`. Try setting `ρ` to a lower value, like 15. You'll see that the system no longer behaves chaotically; the trajectory quickly settles into a simple, predictable fixed point. The strange attractor disappears!
