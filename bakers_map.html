Guide: The Recipe for Chaos

Kneading Dough

The Baker's Map gets its name from the process of kneading dough. It's a simple, two-step process that provides a powerful illustration of how chaotic mixing works.

  1. Stretch: First, the square of "dough" (the image) is stretched to twice its original width and compressed to half its original height. This action separates nearby points.
  2. Cut and Fold: Next, the stretched dough is cut down the middle. The right half is lifted and placed on top of the left half. This folding action brings distant points back close together.
This combination of stretching and folding, when repeated, is a fundamental mechanism for generating chaos. The stretching ensures sensitive dependence on initial conditions (nearby points get separated exponentially fast), while the folding ensures the system remains confined to a finite space (the unit square). After several iterations, the initial image is completely mixed, just like a baker thoroughly mixes the ingredients in their dough.

The Baker's Map Equations

The transformation for a point `(x, y)` on the unit square `[0,1] x [0,1]` is defined as:

T(x, y) =
  (2x, y/2)                if 0 ≤ x < 1/2
  (2x - 1, (y+1)/2)        if 1/2 ≤ x < 1

This is a dissipative map, meaning that areas are not preserved (they are compressed by a factor of 2 at each step). This is unlike Arnold's Cat Map, which is area-preserving. The Baker's Map is one of the simplest and most intuitive examples of a chaotic dynamical system.
