The Quantum Road Trip

How does a particle get from point A to point B? The classical answer is simple: it takes the single, most efficient path (usually a straight line). The quantum answer, proposed by Richard Feynman, is mind-bogglingly different: the particle takes every possible path simultaneously.

Step 1: All Possible Paths

The left panel shows two points in spacetime, A (start) and B (end). When you click "Generate and Sum Paths," the simulation draws numerous possible paths the particle could take.

  • The bright yellow line is the classical path—the path of least "action."
  • The faint blue lines are a random sample of the infinite number of other "quantum paths." They can be wild and convoluted.

Step 2: The Contribution of Each Path

In quantum mechanics, we don't deal with probabilities, but with "probability amplitudes," which can be complex numbers. Each path contributes an amplitude, which we can visualize as a small arrow, or "phasor," in the complex plane (right panel).

The direction of this phasor is determined by the "action" (S) of the path, a concept from classical physics related to the energy used over time. The rule is that the phasor for a path points at an angle of `S/ħ` (where ħ is Planck's constant).

Step 3: The Sum and Interference

The total amplitude to get from A to B is the sum of all these little phasor arrows, laid end-to-end. Click the button and watch the right panel.

You'll notice a pattern:

  1. For paths that are "wild" and far from the classical path, the action `S` changes dramatically. This means their phasors point in random directions. When you add them up, they mostly cancel each other out, leading to a little random walk around the origin. This is destructive interference.
  2. For paths that are very close to the classical path, the action `S` is nearly the same for all of them. This means their phasors all point in roughly the same direction. When you add these up, they combine powerfully, creating a long, straight arrow. This is constructive interference.
The final magenta arrow shows the total sum. Its length squared gives the probability of the particle arriving at B. This simulation shows how the classical world emerges from the quantum. While a quantum particle explores all paths, the only ones that matter are the bundle of paths right around the classical one.
