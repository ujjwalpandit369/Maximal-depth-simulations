Welcome to the Cellular Balancing Act!

Our bodies' cells are filled with charged proteins that can't escape. But small ions like sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) can pass in and out. How does the cell maintain balance? It uses a clever trick of physics called the Donnan Equilibrium.

Step 1: The Setup

The animation shows two compartments separated by a membrane. Think of "Compartment 1" as inside a cell and "Compartment 2" as outside.

  • ■ Red squares are large, negatively charged Proteins (P⁻). They are trapped inside Compartment 1.
  • ● Green circles are positive Sodium ions (Na⁺).
  • ● Yellow circles are negative Chloride ions (Cl⁻).
Both Na⁺ and Cl⁻ can pass freely through the membrane.

Step 2: Initial State

Use the sliders to set up the starting conditions. Let's start with Salt [NaCl] at 100 and Protein [P⁻] at 50. Click "Reset". Initially, the salt concentration is the same on both sides. But notice Compartment 1 has a lot of extra negative charge because of the proteins.

Step 3: The Drive for Balance

The system wants to achieve two types of balance simultaneously:

  1. Charge Balance: Each compartment tries to be electrically neutral (total positive charges = total negative charges).
  2. Concentration Balance: The ions *want* to be equally concentrated on both sides.
These two goals are in conflict! To neutralize the negative proteins, you might think positive Na⁺ ions should rush in. But that would create a huge concentration imbalance.

Step 4: Reaching Equilibrium

Click the red "Run to Equilibrium" button. Watch the ions move!

What happens? To balance the protein's negative charge, some negative Cl⁻ ions are pushed *out* of Compartment 1. To maintain charge neutrality, some positive Na⁺ ions are pulled *into* Compartment 1.

Step 5: The Final State

When the animation stops, look at the result:

  • Unequal Ions: The concentrations of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are no longer equal across the membrane! There is more Na⁺ inside and more Cl⁻ outside.
  • Donnan Potential: Because of this slight imbalance of charged ions, a small electrical voltage, the Donnan Potential, is created across the membrane. You can see its value in the results box.
This is the clever compromise the system makes. It can't achieve perfect concentration balance, so it creates a small electrical potential instead to keep everything stable. This very principle is fundamental to how our nerves and muscles work!

The Donnan Equilibrium Condition

For a system with permeable cations (C⁺) and anions (A⁻) and non-permeable anions (P⁻) in compartment 1 ("in"), the equilibrium state must satisfy the condition that the product of the concentrations of the permeable ions inside equals the product of their concentrations outside.

Equation: `[C⁺]ᵢₙ · [A⁻]ᵢₙ = [C⁺]ₒᵤₜ · [A⁻]ₒᵤₜ`

This arises from the fact that at equilibrium, the electrochemical potential for each permeable ion must be the same across the membrane.

Maintaining Electroneutrality

In parallel, each compartment must remain electrically neutral.
Inside: `[C⁺]ᵢₙ = [A⁻]ᵢₙ + [P⁻]ᵢₙ`
Outside: `[C⁺]ₒᵤₜ = [A⁻]ₒᵤₜ`

Calculating Equilibrium

We can solve these equations. Let `x = [A⁻]ᵢₙ` at equilibrium. From electroneutrality, `[C⁺]ᵢₙ = x + [P⁻]ᵢₙ`. If the initial salt concentration on both sides was `C_s`, then by conservation of ions, `[A⁻]ₒᵤₜ = 2C_s - x`. Since `[C⁺]ₒᵤₜ = [A⁻]ₒᵤₜ`, we can substitute into the Donnan condition:
`(x + [P⁻]ᵢₙ) · x = (2C_s - x) · (2C_s - x)`
This can be solved as a quadratic equation for `x` to find the final concentration of the anion inside, from which all other concentrations can be derived.

The Donnan Potential (E_D)

The resulting electrical potential across the membrane can be calculated with the Nernst equation for any of the permeable ions.

Equation (for a cation like Na⁺):

E_D = (RT/zF) · ln([C⁺]ₒᵤₜ / [C⁺]ᵢₙ)

Where `R` is the gas constant, `T` is temperature, `z` is the ion's charge (+1 for Na⁺), and `F` is the Faraday constant. At room temperature, RT/F is approx. 26.7 mV for z=1.
