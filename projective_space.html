Projective Space in Computer Graphics

Visualizing how 3D scenes are projected onto a 2D screen.

Plain-Language Overview

How does a 3D video game world get flattened onto your 2D computer screen while still looking realistic? The secret lies in projective geometry, a branch of mathematics that became central to computer graphics. This simulation shows the two key views: on the left is the "real" 3D world, seen from the outside, with a cube and a camera. On the right is what the camera actually sees. By dragging the cube or the camera in the 3D world, you can instantly see how the perspective changes in the camera's view. This process mimics how a real camera works: objects farther away appear smaller. In graphics, this is achieved with a special mathematical tool called a projection matrix.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Two Worlds: World Space and Screen Space

This simulation shows two different "spaces":

  • World Space (Left Panel): This is a god's-eye view of the 3D universe where our objects live. We can see the cube's true 3D position and the camera's position and orientation. The pyramid shape extending from the camera is its view frustum—its field of view. Anything inside the frustum will be visible to the camera.
  • Screen Space (Right Panel): This is the final 2D image that the camera produces. It's the result of taking everything inside the view frustum and "projecting" it onto a flat plane. Notice how the side of the cube that is farther from the camera in the left panel appears smaller in the right panel. This is perspective!

2. The Magic of Matrices

The entire transformation from a 3D model's coordinates to the final 2D screen coordinates is done with a series of multiplications by special 4x4 matrices. It's like a recipe with three main steps:

  1. Model Matrix: Takes the cube's vertices from its local "drawing board" and moves, rotates, and scales it into the 3D world. This is what happens when you drag the cube.
  2. View Matrix: This matrix does the opposite of moving the camera. Instead of moving the camera, it moves the entire world so that the camera is at the origin (0,0,0) looking down one axis. It's like the whole universe poses for the camera.
  3. Projection Matrix: This is the most important matrix for perspective. It takes the 3D scene (now positioned in front of the camera) and squashes it into a 2D image, making distant objects smaller. It transforms the pyramid-shaped view frustum into a perfect cube called "clip space".

3. Homogeneous Coordinates: The 4th Dimension

You might wonder why we use 4x4 matrices for 3D graphics. This is done by adding a 4th component, often called 'w', to our 3D coordinates (x, y, z), making them (x, y, z, w). This system is called homogeneous coordinates. It has a huge advantage: it allows a single matrix multiplication to perform not only rotation and scaling, but also translation (moving) and perspective projection. Without it, we would need to handle translation as a separate, more complicated step.

The `w` component is especially crucial for perspective. After being transformed by the projection matrix, a vertex's `w` value will no longer be 1. The final step to get the 2D screen position is the perspective divide: the x, y, and z coordinates are all divided by w. This single division is what makes objects farther away (which get a larger `w` value) shrink, creating the illusion of depth.

Core Theory: The Transformation Pipeline

The final position of a vertex on screen, Vclip, is calculated from its original model position, Vmodel, by multiplying it by three matrices.

Vclip = Mprojection × Mview × Mmodel × Vmodel

The Projection Matrix (Simplified)

A typical perspective projection matrix looks something like this. Here, f is the field of view, and n and f are the near and far clipping planes that define the frustum.

When a 3D vertex (x, y, z, 1) is multiplied by this, the new 4th component becomes w' = -z. After the perspective divide, the final screen coordinates will be inversely proportional to their original depth (-z), which is the mathematical basis for perspective.
