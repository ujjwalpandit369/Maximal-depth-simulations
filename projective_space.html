Projective Space in Computer Graphics

Visualizing how 3D scenes are projected onto a 2D screen.

Plain-Language Overview

How does a 3D video game world get flattened onto your 2D screen? The secret is projective geometry. This simulation shows the two key views: on the left is the "real" 3D world, with a cube and a camera. On the right is what the camera actually sees. By dragging the cube or the camera in the 3D world, you can instantly see how the perspective changes. This process mimics how a real camera works and is achieved with a mathematical tool called a projection matrix.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Two Worlds

This simulation shows two spaces: World Space (Left) is a god's-eye view of the 3D universe. The pyramid shape is the camera's view frustum—its field of view. Screen Space (Right) is the final 2D image the camera produces by projecting everything in the frustum onto a flat plane. Notice how objects farther away on the left appear smaller on the right. That's perspective!

2. The Magic of Matrices

The transformation from 3D to 2D is done with a series of 4x4 matrix multiplications. It's a recipe with three steps: Model Matrix (places the object in the world), View Matrix (moves the world so the camera is at the origin), and Projection Matrix (squashes the 3D scene into a 2D image with perspective).

3. Homogeneous Coordinates: The 4th Dimension

We use 4D coordinates (x, y, z, w) to allow a single matrix to handle translation, rotation, scaling, AND perspective. The `w` component is key. After the projection matrix, `w` is no longer 1. The final step is the perspective divide: the x, y, and z coordinates are all divided by `w`. This division is what makes distant objects (which get a larger `w` value) shrink.

Core Theory: The Transformation Pipeline

The final position of a vertex on screen, Vclip, is calculated from its original model position, Vmodel, by multiplying it by the Model, View, and Projection matrices.

Vclip = Mproj × Mview × Mmodel × Vmodel

When a 3D vertex (x, y, z, 1) is multiplied by a typical perspective projection matrix, its new 4th component becomes w' = -z (in a right-handed system). The perspective divide (dividing all components by `w'`) then makes the final screen coordinates inversely proportional to their original depth, creating the illusion of perspective.
