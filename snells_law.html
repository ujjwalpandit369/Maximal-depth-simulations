Beginner's Guide — Why a Straw in Water Looks Bent

Step 1: Light Changes Speed

Light travels at different speeds in different materials. The "refractive index" (`n`) is a measure of how much a material slows down light compared to a vacuum. A higher `n` means light travels slower. In the simulation, you can change the refractive index for the top medium (Medium 1) and the bottom medium (Medium 2).

Step 2: Reflection and Refraction

When a ray of light hits the boundary between two media, two things happen:

  • Reflection: Some of the light bounces off the surface. The Law of Reflection is simple: the angle of reflection is always equal to the angle of incidence.
  • Refraction: The rest of the light passes into the new medium and bends. The amount it bends is governed by Snell's Law. This bending is why a straw in a glass of water appears broken at the surface.

In the simulation, you can control the incoming "incident ray" by dragging the laser. The simulation then shows you both the reflected and refracted rays.

Step 3: Snell's Law in Action

Snell's Law describes a beautiful relationship between the angles and the refractive indices. The key idea is:

  • When light enters a denser medium (higher `n`), it bends towards the normal (the dashed line perpendicular to the surface).
  • When light enters a less dense medium (lower `n`), it bends away from the normal.
Try it! Start with n₁=1.00 (Air) and n₂=1.50 (Glass). See how the ray bends towards the normal. Now, swap them so n₁=1.50 and n₂=1.00. Notice how the ray bends away.

Step 4: Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

A fascinating phenomenon occurs ONLY when light travels from a denser medium to a less dense one (n₁ > n₂). Set n₁ to 1.50 (Glass) and n₂ to 1.00 (Air).

Now, slowly increase the angle of incidence by dragging the laser. Watch the refracted ray. It bends further and further away from the normal, getting closer to the boundary. At a specific angle, called the critical angle (θ_c), the refracted ray will skim right along the boundary at 90°.

If you increase the angle of incidence beyond this critical angle, the light can no longer escape! The refracted ray vanishes, and 100% of the light is reflected back into the first medium. This is Total Internal Reflection. The simulation will clearly indicate when this happens. TIR is not just a curiosity; it's the principle behind fiber optic cables that power the internet.

Core Theory — The Laws of Reflection and Refraction

1. The Law of Reflection

This law states that the angle of the incident ray (θ₁) equals the angle of the reflected ray (θᵣ), both measured with respect to the normal.

θᵣ = θ₁

2. Snell's Law of Refraction

This law relates the angles of incidence and refraction to the refractive indices of the two media.

n₁ * sin(θ₁) = n₂ * sin(θ₂)

  • n₁ and θ₁ are the refractive index and angle in the first medium.
  • n₂ and θ₂ are the refractive index and angle in the second medium.

We can rearrange this to find the angle of refraction: θ₂ = arcsin( (n₁/n₂) * sin(θ₁) )

Example: Light travels from air (n₁=1.00) into water (n₂=1.33) at an angle of θ₁ = 30°.
1.00 * sin(30°) = 1.33 * sin(θ₂)
0.5 = 1.33 * sin(θ₂)
sin(θ₂) = 0.5 / 1.33 ≈ 0.376
θ₂ = arcsin(0.376) ≈ 22.1°

3. The Critical Angle for TIR

Total Internal Reflection occurs when the angle of refraction `θ₂` reaches 90°. This can only happen if `n₁ > n₂`. We can find the critical angle of incidence `θ_c` by setting `θ₂ = 90°` in Snell's Law.

n₁ * sin(θ_c) = n₂ * sin(90°)

Since `sin(90°) = 1`, the formula simplifies to:

sin(θ_c) = n₂ / n₁ or θ_c = arcsin(n₂ / n₁)

Example: For light going from glass (n₁=1.50) to air (n₂=1.00):
θ_c = arcsin(1.00 / 1.50) = arcsin(0.667) ≈ 41.8°
Any angle of incidence greater than 41.8° will result in Total Internal Reflection.
