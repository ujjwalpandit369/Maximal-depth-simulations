Beginner's Guide — How Lenses and Mirrors Form Images

Step 1: The Setup

The main view is our optical workbench. The horizontal line is the optical axis. The green arrow is our object. Our goal is to see what kind of image is formed by the optical elements we add.

Step 2: Adding Lenses and Mirrors

Use the buttons to add different components to the workbench. You can drag them left and right along the axis. There are four types:

  • Converging Lens: A standard convex lens, thicker in the middle. It bends parallel light rays so they converge at a focal point. Think of a magnifying glass.
  • Diverging Lens: A concave lens, thinner in the middle. It spreads parallel light rays out as if they were coming from a focal point.
  • Concave Mirror: A curved mirror that focuses parallel light rays. Think of a satellite dish or a makeup mirror.
  • Convex Mirror: A curved mirror that spreads light rays out. Think of a security mirror in a store.

Step 3: The Three Principal Rays

To figure out where an image will form, we don't need to trace every ray of light. We can use a clever shortcut by drawing just three special "principal rays" from the tip of the object. Where these three rays (or their imaginary extensions) cross, that's where the tip of the image will be!

  1. The Parallel Ray (Blue): This ray leaves the object parallel to the axis. After hitting the lens/mirror, it is bent to pass through the focal point on the other side.
  2. The Focal Ray (Red): This ray first passes through the focal point on the same side as the object. When it hits the lens/mirror, it is bent so it comes out parallel to the axis.
  3. The Central Ray (Gray): This ray passes through the very center of the lens (or hits the vertex of the mirror). It continues in a straight line, undeviated.

Step 4: Real vs. Virtual Images

After you place an object and a lens, you'll see an image arrow appear.

  • If the principal rays actually cross after passing through the lens, they form a real image (shown in red). A real image is always inverted and can be projected onto a screen. This is how a movie projector works.
  • If the rays are spreading out and don't cross, our brain traces them backwards to a point where they appear to cross. This is a virtual image (shown in yellow and dashed). A virtual image is always upright and cannot be projected onto a screen. This is what you see when you look at yourself in a regular flat mirror.
Try moving the object closer to and further from a converging lens. You'll see it can create both real and virtual images depending on the object's position!

Core Theory — The Mathematics of Optics

The Thin Lens / Mirror Equation

The relationship between the object distance (dₒ), image distance (dᵢ), and focal length (f) is described by this beautiful and simple equation:

1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ = 1/f

To use this equation correctly, we must follow sign conventions:

  • dₒ is always positive.
  • f is positive for converging elements (converging lens, concave mirror) and negative for diverging elements (diverging lens, convex mirror).
  • dᵢ is positive if the image is real (on the opposite side of the lens from the object) and negative if the image is virtual (on the same side of the lens).

Example: An object is placed 30 cm from a converging lens with a 10 cm focal length. Where is the image?
1/30 + 1/dᵢ = 1/10 => 1/dᵢ = 1/10 - 1/30 = 2/30 => dᵢ = 15 cm. Since dᵢ is positive, the image is real.

Magnification (M)

The magnification tells us how large the image is compared to the object, and whether it is upright or inverted.

M = hᵢ / hₒ = -dᵢ / dₒ

  • If |M| > 1, the image is magnified. If |M| < 1, it's reduced.
  • If M is negative, the image is inverted. If M is positive, the image is upright.

Example (continued): M = -15 / 30 = -0.5. The image is inverted (due to the minus sign) and half the size of the object.
