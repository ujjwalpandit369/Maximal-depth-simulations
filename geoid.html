Visualizing the Earth's Geoid

Exploring the "lumpy" shape of Earth's true gravitational surface.

Estimated Runtime: Real-time | Recommended Device: Desktop (with WebGL) | Approx. HTML Size: KB

One-Paragraph Overview

While we often think of the Earth as a perfect sphere, it's not. It bulges at the equator, making it an "ellipsoid." But even that isn't the whole story. The planet's gravity is not uniform; some places pull slightly stronger or weaker than others due to variations in mass deep within the Earth. If we could map out a surface where the gravitational pull is exactly the same everywhere—the level that oceans would settle to without tides or currents—we would get a lumpy, potato-like shape called the geoid. This simulation visualizes that geoid, massively exaggerating its bumps and dips to make them visible and to reveal the true, complex gravitational face of our planet.

Beginner's Guide — A Gentle Introduction

Step 1: Beyond the Perfect Sphere

Most globes show Earth as a perfect ball. A more accurate model is an "oblate ellipsoid"—it's slightly squashed, bulging at the equator due to its rotation. The simulation can show this reference ellipsoid as a smooth, semi-transparent shape.

Step 2: Lumpy Gravity

Now for the interesting part. The Earth's mass isn't distributed evenly. There are denser regions (like parts of the mantle) and less dense regions. Denser areas pull with slightly more gravity. This isn't something you'd ever feel, but sensitive instruments can measure it.

Step 3: Defining the Geoid

Imagine a global ocean that could cover the entire planet, completely free from winds, tides, and currents. This water would settle into a shape dictated purely by gravity. It would rise slightly over areas with stronger gravity (where it's "pulled" up more) and sink over areas with weaker gravity. That lumpy, uneven surface is the geoid. It represents a surface of constant gravitational potential.

Step 4: The Bumps and Dips (Geoid Undulation)

The "lumps" on the geoid are the differences between the geoid and the perfect reference ellipsoid. We call this the "geoid height" or "geoid undulation."

  • A positive geoid height (a bump, shown in red) means the geoid is above the reference ellipsoid. This happens over areas of higher-than-average mass concentration.
  • A negative geoid height (a dip, shown in blue) means the geoid is below the reference ellipsoid, over areas of lower-than-average mass.
It's crucial to understand that these undulations are tiny in reality. The biggest bump is about +85 meters (near Iceland) and the biggest dip is about -106 meters (south of India). On a planet with a radius of 6.3 million meters, this is incredibly smooth—smoother than a billiard ball! That's why we need the "Exaggeration" slider.

Step 5: The "Potato Earth"

Everyday Analogy: A Lumpy Bed. Think of the reference ellipsoid as a perfectly flat, new mattress. The geoid is the shape the mattress takes after several people of different weights have slept on it for years. It has permanent dips and bumps corresponding to where the most and least pressure was applied. The "geoid height" is simply the depth of a dip or the height of a bump relative to the original, flat mattress surface.
When you crank up the "Exaggeration" slider in the simulation, you are magnifying these tiny bumps and dips thousands of times until the Earth starts to look like a lumpy potato. This isn't what the Earth *looks* like, but it's a true representation of its underlying gravitational structure.

Core Theory — How It Works

Visualizing the geoid requires three main components: creating a sphere, modeling the geoid heights, and using shaders to displace the sphere's surface.

1. Modeling the Geoid with Spherical Harmonics

Any function on the surface of a sphere can be described as a sum of special mathematical functions called Spherical Harmonics, `Y(θ, φ)`. Real geoid models like EGM2008 are defined by thousands of coefficients for these functions. For this simulation, we use a highly simplified model with just a few terms to capture the most prominent features.

The geoid height `N` at latitude `φ` and longitude `λ` is approximated by:

N(λ, φ) = Σ [ C_lm * cos(mλ) + S_lm * sin(mλ) ] * P_lm(sin φ)

Plain English: The height at any point is a sum of several fundamental wave-like patterns (`Y_lm`, which are composed of sines, cosines, and polynomials `P_lm`). Each pattern is scaled by a coefficient (`C_lm`, `S_lm`). Our model includes terms that create the equatorial bulge, the pear-shape asymmetry, and the major highs and lows like the one in the Indian Ocean.

Numeric Example: A dominant term is the `C_2,0` coefficient, which represents the Earth's equatorial bulge. Its associated function `P_2,0` gives a negative value at the poles and a positive one at the equator, effectively "squashing" the sphere into an ellipsoid.

2. Creating the Sphere Mesh (Icosphere)

Instead of a standard "UV sphere" which has pinched poles, we generate an icosphere. We start with an icosahedron (a 20-sided polygon made of triangles). Then we repeatedly subdivide each triangle into four smaller ones, and push the new vertices out to the surface of the sphere. This process creates a mesh with vertices that are much more evenly distributed across the surface.

3. Vertex Displacement in the GPU (Vertex Shader)

The real magic happens in the Graphics Processing Unit (GPU). For every single vertex in our sphere mesh, the GPU runs a tiny program called a vertex shader.

// Simplified GLSL vertex shader code
vec3 original_position = ...;
float height = calculateGeoidHeight(original_position);
vec3 normal = normalize(original_position);
vec3 new_position = original_position + normal * height * exaggeration;

Plain English: For each point on the original perfect sphere:

  1. Calculate the geoid height `N` for that point using our spherical harmonic model.
  2. Find the direction pointing straight out from the sphere's center at that point (the "normal vector").
  3. Move the point outwards (or inwards) along that direction by an amount equal to `height * exaggeration`.
This is done for tens of thousands of vertices simultaneously, creating the lumpy shape in real-time.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Is the Earth really this lumpy?
Absolutely not. This visualization is extremely exaggerated. The real Earth is, relatively speaking, smoother than a billiard ball. The exaggeration is a scientific visualization technique to make imperceptible features understandable.
2. If there's a "bump" in the geoid over the North Atlantic, does that mean there's a mountain on the ocean floor?
Not necessarily. The geoid bump is caused by higher-than-average mass density, which could be in the crust or deep in the mantle. While it often correlates with features like the mid-Atlantic ridge, it's a measure of gravity, not topography.
3. Why is the biggest dip in the Indian Ocean?
This is the "Indian Ocean Geoid Low," a famous feature. It's believed to be caused by a huge, low-density plume of hot rock rising from the deep mantle, creating a region of slightly weaker gravity that the geoid "sinks" into.
4. The 3D model is not showing up, just a black box.
This simulation requires WebGL, a technology for rendering 3D graphics in the browser. Please ensure you are using a modern browser (like Chrome, Firefox, or Safari) on a computer or mobile device that supports WebGL. You may need to enable "Hardware Acceleration" in your browser's settings.
5. The rotation is choppy on my machine.
Rendering a detailed 3D object can be demanding. This simulation automatically reduces the sphere's detail (number of vertices) on smaller screens. If it's still slow, try closing other tabs or using a more powerful device.
