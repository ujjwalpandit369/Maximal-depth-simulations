The Grassmannian Manifold

A visualization of the Grassmannian G(2, 3), the space of all 2D planes through the origin.

Overview

In mathematics, we can create new spaces whose "points" are themselves other spaces. The Grassmannian manifold, G(k, n), is the space of all k-dimensional subspaces within an n-dimensional space. This simulation explores the simplest non-trivial example: `G(2, 3)`, the space of all 2D planes that pass through the origin in 3D space. By using sliders to change the orientation of a plane in 3D space, you will see how each unique plane corresponds to a single point on a new manifold. This new manifold, the Grassmannian, turns out to be a familiar shape with a special twist.

Beginner's Guide to the Space of Planes

Step 1: A Plane Through the Origin

The left panel shows our familiar 3D space. A 2D plane is drawn passing through the origin. You can change the "tilt" of this plane using the sliders.

How can we uniquely describe the orientation of this plane? The easiest way is to describe the one direction that is *not* in the plane: the direction of the line that is perpendicular (or "normal") to it. This is shown as the orange normal vector.

Step 2: From Planes to Lines to Points on a Sphere

So, the space of all planes through the origin is the same as the space of all lines through the origin (their normal vectors). How can we describe the space of all lines through the origin?

Imagine a giant sphere centered at the origin. Any line that passes through the origin must intersect the sphere at exactly two opposite points (called antipodal points). For example, the North Pole and the South Pole both lie on the same line (the Earth's axis of rotation).

This gives us the key insight: each line corresponds to a pair of opposite points on a sphere.

Step 3: The Grassmannian G(2, 3)

The right panel shows this new space. It's a sphere. As you change the orientation of the plane on the left, watch the point on the sphere on the right. The position of this point corresponds to where the plane's normal vector pierces the sphere.

Notice that there are two dots: one bright, one faded. The faded dot is at the antipodal point. Since both of these points lie on the same line, they both represent the **exact same plane**. Therefore, the true Grassmannian manifold `G(2, 3)` is the space of a sphere where we have "glued" or identified every point with its opposite. This space is called the **real projective plane, ℝP²**.

Controls

Use the sliders to change the orientation of the plane on the left. Observe how this corresponds to a single point (and its opposite) on the sphere on the right.

You can also drag the canvases to rotate the views.
