Length Contraction Explorer

Visualizing the paradox of the fast-moving spaceship and the tunnel.

Plain-Language Overview

Special relativity doesn't just affect time; it also affects space. An object moving at a very high speed appears shorter in its direction of motion to a stationary observer. This is called length contraction. This simulation explores this idea with a classic paradox: a long spaceship is flying towards a shorter tunnel. From the perspective of someone standing by the tunnel, if the ship moves fast enough, it will shrink and, for a brief moment, fit entirely inside the tunnel. But from the pilot's perspective, the spaceship is stationary and the *tunnel* is rushing towards them. To the pilot, the tunnel appears even shorter, making it seem impossible for the ship to ever be fully contained. Who is right? This simulation lets you see both viewpoints and understand the resolution to the paradox.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Setup: A Paradox

We have a spaceship whose length, when measured at rest (its "proper length"), is 100 meters. We also have a tunnel whose proper length is 80 meters. Clearly, the ship is longer than the tunnel. The paradox arises when the ship starts moving at relativistic speeds.

2. The View from the Tunnel

Click the "View from Tunnel" button. In this reference frame, you are stationary, and the tunnel is 80 meters long. The spaceship is flying towards you. As you increase its velocity with the slider, you'll see its length shrink according to the formula L = L₀ / γ. At a velocity of 0.6c (60% the speed of light), the Lorentz factor γ is 1.25, so the ship's apparent length becomes 100m / 1.25 = 80m. At this speed, it's exactly the same length as the tunnel! If you increase the speed further, the ship becomes even shorter.

Press "Animate." At v=0.6c, you will see a moment where the back of the ship enters the tunnel at the exact same instant the front of the ship exits. From this viewpoint, the ship "fits."

3. The View from the Spaceship

Now for the paradox. Click the "View from Spaceship" button. From the pilot's perspective, the ship is at rest and has its proper length of 100m. It is the *tunnel* that is rushing towards them. Therefore, the tunnel's length gets contracted. At v=0.6c, the tunnel's apparent length becomes 80m / 1.25 = 64m. From this viewpoint, the tunnel is now ridiculously short! How could the 100m ship possibly fit inside a 64m tunnel?

Press "Animate." You will see that the ship does *not* fit. The front of the ship exits the tunnel long before the back of the ship enters it.

4. The Resolution: The Relativity of Simultaneity

So, who is right? Both observers are right, from their own perspectives. The paradox is resolved because they disagree on the timing of events. The concept of "fitting" requires two events—the back of the ship being at the tunnel entrance, and the front of the ship being at the tunnel exit—to happen at the same time.

But as Einstein showed, simultaneity is relative!

  • In the Tunnel's Frame, these two events *are* simultaneous (at v=0.6c). The ship fits.
  • In the Spaceship's Frame, these two events are *not* simultaneous. The front of the ship exits the tunnel first, and only later does the back of the ship enter. The ship is never fully inside the tunnel.
The event log in the simulation will record the times of these two key events in each frame, clearly showing that observers in relative motion will disagree on whether events happen at the same time. There is no paradox, just a universe that is stranger than our everyday intuition suggests.

The Lorentz Transformations

The physics behind this is described by the Lorentz transformations, which relate the coordinates of an event in one frame (x, t) to another frame (x', t') moving at a velocity v.

x' = γ(x - vt)
t' = γ(t - vx/c2)

The key is the time transformation. Notice how the new time t' depends not only on the old time t but also on the old position x. Two events that are simultaneous in the first frame (have the same t) but happen at different locations (different x) will have different times in the new frame. This is the mathematical root of the relativity of simultaneity.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Does the ship *really* shrink?
No, not in its own reference frame. The pilot would never feel squished. Length contraction is a relational effect: an object's length is measured to be different by observers who are moving relative to it. There is no physical compression happening to the object.
2. What would the pilot see if they looked at the tunnel?
The pilot would see the tunnel rushing towards them, and it would appear visually shorter (contracted) than its rest length. This is what the "View from Ship" demonstrates.
3. So does the ship fit or not?
The question itself is flawed because it assumes the concept of "fitting" (being entirely inside at one moment in time) is absolute. It is not. The answer depends on who you ask. In the tunnel's frame, it fits. In the ship's frame, it does not. Both are correct descriptions of reality.
4. Is this related to time dilation?
Yes, absolutely. Length contraction and time dilation are two sides of the same coin. They are both consequences of the way spacetime is structured, as described by the Lorentz transformations. You cannot have one without the other.
