Beginner's Guide — Why a Moving Siren Changes Pitch

Step 1: Sound as Waves

Sound travels through the air in waves, much like ripples spreading out from a pebble dropped in a pond. The pitch of a sound is determined by its frequency—the number of wave crests that reach your ear per second. Higher frequency means a higher pitch.

Step 2: The Stationary Source

First, set the "Source Velocity" slider to 0. The source is now stationary. Notice that it emits perfectly concentric, evenly-spaced circles representing the sound waves. No matter where you place the observer, they will perceive the same number of waves per second, so they hear the true "Source Frequency."

Step 3: The Moving Source

Now, increase the source's velocity. As the source moves, it continues to emit waves, but from a new position each time. This has a dramatic effect:

  • In Front of the Source: The source is "catching up" to the waves it just sent out. This causes the wave fronts to bunch up together. The distance between waves (wavelength) gets shorter, so more waves hit the observer's ear per second. The result: the observer hears a higher frequency (higher pitch).
  • Behind the Source: The source is moving away from the waves it sent out in that direction. This stretches the wave fronts apart. The wavelength gets longer, so fewer waves hit the observer's ear per second. The result: the observer hears a lower frequency (lower pitch).

This change in perceived frequency due to relative motion is the Doppler Effect. Drag the observer around to see how the frequency they "hear" changes based on their position relative to the moving source.

Step 4: The Sonic Boom

What happens if the source moves faster than the waves it creates? Use the slider to set the source velocity higher than the speed of sound (343 m/s). The source now outruns its own sound waves. These waves, unable to get out of the way, pile up into a single, massive pressure wave in the shape of a V or a cone. This is a shock wave, or a "Mach cone."

When this highly-compressed cone of air passes over the observer, they experience a sudden, dramatic change in pressure, which they hear as a loud sonic boom. Watch the simulation and you'll see the "SONIC BOOM!" indicator flash at the exact moment the cone hits the observer.

Core Theory — The Doppler Effect Equation

The perceived frequency can be calculated precisely.

Moving Source, Stationary Observer

The frequency `fₒ` heard by the observer is related to the source's true frequency `fₛ` by the following formula:

fₒ = fₛ * ( v / (v - vₛ) )

  • v is the speed of sound (a constant, 343 m/s in this simulation).
  • vₛ is the velocity of the source. This value is positive if the source is moving towards the observer and negative if it is moving away.

Example: A source with fₛ = 440 Hz moves towards an observer at 50 m/s.
fₒ = 440 * (343 / (343 - 50)) = 440 * (343 / 293) ≈ 515 Hz (a higher pitch).
After it passes and moves away, vₛ becomes -50 m/s:
fₒ = 440 * (343 / (343 - (-50))) = 440 * (343 / 393) ≈ 384 Hz (a lower pitch).

The Mach Cone

A sonic boom occurs when the source velocity `vₛ` is greater than the speed of sound `v`. The ratio of these speeds is the Mach number, `M = vₛ / v`.

The angle `θ` of the V-shaped shock wave (the Mach cone) is given by:

sin(θ) = v / vₛ = 1 / M

This shows that a shock wave can only form when `M > 1`. As the source gets faster (M increases), the angle `θ` of the cone gets smaller and sharper.
