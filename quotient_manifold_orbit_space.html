Overview

In mathematics, we often want to consider things "the same" if they are related by a symmetry. For example, we might consider two positions on a circle to be equivalent if one can be rotated to the other. The process of "gluing" these equivalent points together creates a new space, called a quotient space or orbit space. This simulation provides a visual introduction to this powerful idea. By applying a group of rotations to a 2D plane, you can see how the set of all "orbits" (the sets of equivalent points) itself forms a new shape, such as a cone. This concept is fundamental in geometry, topology, and physics, particularly in gauge theories.

Beginner's Guide to Gluing Spaces

Step 1: The Original Space and the Group Action

The left panel shows our original space, a simple 2D plane. We are going to apply a "group action" to it, which is just a set of symmetry transformations. You can choose between two different groups:

  • ℤ₄ (Discrete Rotations): This group contains four rotations: 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°.
  • SO(2) (Continuous Rotations): This group contains all possible rotations around the origin.

Step 2: The Orbit

When you click on a point `p` in the left panel, the simulation highlights its orbit. An orbit is the set of all points you can get to by starting at `p` and applying all the transformations from the group.

  • Under the ℤ₄ action, the orbit of a point is a set of four points.
  • Under the SO(2) action, the orbit of a point is the entire circle that passes through it.

Step 3: The Quotient Space - The Space of Orbits

The core idea is to create a new space where each orbit from the original space becomes a single point. We are "collapsing" or "gluing" all the points in an orbit together. The right panel shows this new quotient space.

  • For ℤ₄: The quotient space is a cone. A point's distance from the origin determines its height on the cone, and its angle within the first 90° quadrant determines its position around the cone's base. The origin of the plane becomes the special "singular" tip of the cone.
  • For SO(2): The quotient space is a half-line. Since every point on a circle is now considered the same, the only thing that distinguishes one orbit from another is its radius `r`. The space of all possible radii is a line starting at 0 and going to infinity.

As you move your selected point in the left panel, watch how the corresponding single point moves in the right panel. This shows the mapping from the original space to the quotient space.
