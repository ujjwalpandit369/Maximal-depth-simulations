Plain-Language Overview

Just like electrons in an atom occupy discrete energy shells, the protons and neutrons inside an atomic nucleus also organize themselves into energy levels. The Nuclear Shell Model describes this structure. It successfully predicts that nuclei with specific "magic numbers" of protons or neutrons are exceptionally stable, much like the noble gases with their filled electron shells. This interactive simulation lets you build your own nucleus by adding protons and neutrons, filling up the energy shells, and discovering which combinations lead to these islands of stability.

Beginner's Guide — Shells in the Nucleus

Step 1: Analogy to Electron Shells

You may be familiar with the idea that electrons in an atom don't just orbit anywhere; they are restricted to specific energy shells (1s, 2s, 2p, etc.). When an atom has a completely filled outer shell, it is very stable and chemically inert—this is why the noble gases like Helium, Neon, and Argon are so unreactive. The Nuclear Shell Model applies this same powerful idea to the particles *inside* the nucleus.

Step 2: Separate Ladders for Protons and Neutrons

Inside the nucleus, both protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons) exist in a set of quantized energy levels, like rungs on a ladder. A key difference from the atomic model is that protons and neutrons are distinct particles, so they have their own separate, independent energy ladders. In the simulation, you will see one ladder for protons and one for neutrons.

Step 3: Filling the Shells

As you add protons or neutrons using the sliders, they will fill their respective energy ladders from the bottom up. Each energy level, or "shell," can only hold a specific number of particles due to a quantum rule called the Pauli Exclusion Principle. The simulation shows the capacity of each shell.

Step 4: The Magic Numbers

When the number of protons or neutrons reaches a value that corresponds to a completely filled shell, the nucleus gains a great deal of extra stability. These numbers are known as the **magic numbers**: 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126.

  • A nucleus with a magic number of protons OR neutrons is called "magic."
  • A nucleus with a magic number of BOTH protons and neutrons (like Helium-4, Oxygen-16, or Lead-208) is called "doubly magic" and is exceptionally stable and tightly bound.
As you adjust the number of protons and neutrons in the simulation, watch the stability indicator. It will light up when you hit one of these magic numbers.

Nuclear Potential and Shell Ordering

Unlike atoms, where electrons orbit a central positive charge, there is no central point in a nucleus. Each nucleon moves in an "effective potential" created by the average interaction with all the other nucleons. This potential is often modeled as a combination of a square well and a harmonic oscillator, with a crucial "spin-orbit" coupling term added.

This spin-orbit interaction is what correctly predicts the observed magic numbers. It splits the energy levels that would otherwise be degenerate (have the same energy). The ordering of the shells and their capacity (2j+1) is as follows:

  1. 1s₁/₂ (Capacity: 2) -> Cumulative: 2 (Magic)
  2. 1p₃/₂ (Capacity: 4)
  3. 1p₁/₂ (Capacity: 2) -> Cumulative: 8 (Magic)
  4. 1d₅/₂ (Capacity: 6)
  5. 2s₁/₂ (Capacity: 2)
  6. 1d₃/₂ (Capacity: 4) -> Cumulative: 20 (Magic)
  7. 1f₇/₂ (Capacity: 8) -> Cumulative: 28 (Magic)
  8. 2p₃/₂ (Capacity: 4)
  9. 1f₅/₂ (Capacity: 6)
  10. 2p₁/₂ (Capacity: 2)
  11. 1g₉/₂ (Capacity: 10) -> Cumulative: 50 (Magic)
  12. ... and so on for 82 and 126.

The simulation fills the particles into these shells in this precise order.
