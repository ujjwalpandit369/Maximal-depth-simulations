Plain-Language Overview

The motion of two bodies orbiting each other, like the Earth and the Sun, is perfectly predictable and has been understood for centuries. But what happens if you add just one more body to the mix? The result is the infamous "three-body problem," a system so complex and sensitive that it has no general solution and often behaves chaotically. This interactive sandbox puts you in control of your own three-body system. You can place three stars or planets in space, give them initial velocities, and watch their intricate and often beautiful gravitational dance unfold. Discover for yourself how tiny changes in the starting conditions can lead to wildly different outcomes, and try to find rare, stable orbits in the midst of chaos.

Beginner's Guide: The Unpredictable Dance

Step 1: The Two-Body Problem (Simple)

When only two objects are involved, like a single planet orbiting a star, their motion is governed by simple, elegant laws (Kepler's Laws). The planet follows a stable, repeating elliptical path. This problem is "solved" in the sense that we can write down a precise mathematical formula to predict the planet's position at any time in the future.

Step 2: The Three-Body Problem (Complex)

Now, let's add a third body. Each of the three bodies now feels the gravitational pull from the other two. The net force on each body is constantly changing in a complex way as the other two bodies move. This seemingly small change—from two bodies to three—makes the system fundamentally different. There is no general formula to describe the motion. The only way to see what happens is to simulate it step-by-step, which is what this simulation does.

Step 3: Creating Your Own System

This simulation is a sandbox. You set the initial conditions:

  1. Use the sliders to set the mass for each of the three bodies (represented by the red, blue, and green circles).
  2. Click and drag the bodies on the canvas to set their starting positions.
  3. To give a body an initial velocity, click on it and drag away. A line will appear representing the velocity vector. The length and direction of the line determine the initial speed and direction of the body. Release the mouse to set the velocity.
  4. Once you're ready, click **"Start"**.

Step 4: Chaos and Stability

As you experiment, you will discover the chaotic nature of the system.

  • Chaos: Try setting three equal masses in a rough line and giving them small initial velocities. Watch the result. Now press "Reset" and try again with slightly different starting positions. The initial paths might look similar, but they will quickly diverge into completely different, unpredictable orbits. This extreme sensitivity to initial conditions is the hallmark of chaos.
  • Stability: While most configurations are chaotic, some stable or periodic orbits are possible! Try creating a "solar system" by making one mass much larger than the other two. If you give the two smaller "planets" stable circular orbits, the system can remain stable for a long time. There are also more exotic stable solutions, like the famous figure-8 orbit.

Core Theory: Numerical Integration of Newton's Law

Since there is no general formula for the three-body problem, we must solve it numerically. This means calculating the forces at one moment in time, using that to predict where the bodies will be a tiny fraction of a second later, and then repeating this process thousands of times.

1. The gravitational force vector exerted on mass m₁ by mass m₂ is given by Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation:

F₁₂ = G * (m₁m₂) / |r₂ - r₁|³ * (r₂ - r₁)

Where G is the gravitational constant and r is the position vector.

2. The net force on each body is the vector sum of the forces from the other two bodies:

Fnet,1 = F₁₂ + F₁₃

3. From Newton's Second Law, we find the acceleration of each body:

a₁ = Fnet,1 / m₁

4. The simulation uses a numerical integration method (like the Leapfrog method) to update the velocities and positions over a small time step, Δt:

v(t + Δt/2) = v(t - Δt/2) + a(t)Δt

r(t + Δt) = r(t) + v(t + Δt/2)Δt
