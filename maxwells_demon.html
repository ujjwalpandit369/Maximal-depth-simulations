Maxwell's Demon

A thought experiment that challenges the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

Overview

Can you separate hot from cold without doing any work? In 1867, James Clerk Maxwell imagined a tiny, intelligent "demon" that could do just that. By guarding a door between two chambers of gas and selectively allowing fast particles to pass one way and slow particles the other, the demon could create a temperature difference from nothing, seemingly violating the Second Law of Thermodynamics. This simulation lets you be the demon, sorting particles to see how entropy can be locally decreased. The accompanying text explains the modern resolution to this famous paradox, which lies in the profound connection between energy and information.

The Paradox Explained

  1. The Second Law of Thermodynamics: This fundamental law states that the total entropy (a measure of disorder or randomness) of an isolated system can never decrease over time. Heat naturally flows from hot to cold, and systems tend to move towards a uniform, disordered state of thermal equilibrium.
  2. The Demon's Task: The demon watches individual gas particles. Particles in a gas have a wide range of speeds; temperature is just the *average* kinetic energy. The demon waits for a fast (hot) particle to approach the door from the left and opens it. It then waits for a slow (cold) particle to approach from the right and opens it.
  3. The Violation: By repeating this process, the demon sorts the gas into a hot chamber and a cold chamber, decreasing the overall entropy of the system. It has created order from disorder. Because the demon itself does no work (opening a tiny, frictionless door costs no energy), it appears to have violated the Second Law. A heat engine could then be run between the two chambers to do useful work, creating a perpetual motion machine of the second kind.

The Resolution: Information is Physical

For over a century, physicists debated the resolution to this paradox. The modern answer, solidified by Rolf Landauer and Charles Bennett, is that the paradox ignores a crucial component: the demon's memory.

To do its job, the demon must first measure a particle's speed and store that information (e.g., "fast particle approaching"). To be ready for the next particle, it must then erase that bit of information from its memory. Landauer's Principle, a cornerstone of the physics of information, states that the act of erasing one bit of information has an unavoidable minimum thermodynamic cost. It must dissipate a small amount of heat into the environment.

This dissipated heat increases the entropy of the environment. It has been proven that the entropy increase from erasing the demon's memory is always greater than or equal to the entropy decrease gained by sorting the particle. The Second Law is saved! The demon can create local order in the gas, but only at the cost of creating even more disorder in its own memory system.

Analogy: A Librarian Sorting Books

Imagine a librarian in a messy, disordered library. Their job is to sort books onto "Fiction" and "Non-Fiction" shelves, decreasing the library's entropy. To do this, the librarian must read the cover of each book ("measure"), hold that information in their brain ("memory"), and walk the book to the correct shelf. This process requires energy—the librarian consumes food, generates body heat, and breathes, increasing the total entropy of the wider world (the library + librarian system) more than the books' entropy was decreased.
