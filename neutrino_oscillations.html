Neutrino Oscillations

A simulation of a neutrino's changing identity.

Overview

Neutrinos are ghostly fundamental particles that come in three "flavors": electron (νₑ), muon (νμ), and tau (ντ). Neutrino oscillation is a bizarre quantum mechanical phenomenon where a neutrino created with one flavor can, as it travels through space, be measured to have a different flavor. This discovery proved that neutrinos have mass and resolved the long-standing "solar neutrino problem." This simulation visualizes this process, showing how the probability of detecting a certain flavor oscillates as the neutrino travels from a source to a detector.



Flavor vs. Mass

The key to understanding neutrino oscillations is that the states we produce and detect (the "flavor states") are not the same as the states that actually travel through space (the "mass states"). Each flavor state is a quantum superposition, or mixture, of the three mass states (ν₁, ν₂, ν₃). Because the mass states have slightly different masses, their quantum waves travel at slightly different "speeds" (or more accurately, their phases evolve at different rates). This causes them to get out of sync with each other as they travel.

As the phase relationship between the mass states changes, the specific mixture that defines a flavor also changes. The simulation visualizes this for a simplified two-flavor system (muon and electron neutrinos). The traveling particle is shown as a pie chart representing its flavor composition. The graph below plots the probability of measuring each flavor as a function of distance. Notice how the probabilities oscillate sinusoidally.

The Oscillation Formula (2-Flavor)

P(νμ → νₑ) = sin²(2θ) * sin²(1.27 * Δm² * L / E)
  • P(νμ → νₑ) is the probability that a muon neutrino becomes an electron neutrino.
  • sin²(2θ) is the "mixing amplitude," determined by the mixing angle θ. A larger value means more dramatic oscillations.
  • Δm² is the difference between the squares of the masses of the two mass states. A larger mass difference leads to faster oscillations.
  • L is the travel distance in km.
  • E is the neutrino energy in GeV.

Try adjusting the Distance and Energy sliders. You'll see that the oscillation pattern depends on the ratio L/E. This is why different experiments looking at different distances and energy ranges are needed to measure all the oscillation parameters.
