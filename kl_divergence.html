Information Space & KL Divergence

Visualizing the "distance" between two probability distributions.

Plain-Language Overview

In statistics and machine learning, we often need to compare how different one probability distribution is from another. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence is a fundamental tool for this. It measures the "information loss" when we use an approximate distribution (Q) to model a true distribution (P). Think of it as a kind of "distance," but with a twist: the distance from P to Q is not the same as the distance from Q to P! This simulation lets you control two probability distributions, P (in red) and Q (in orange), and see how the KL Divergence changes as you make them more or less similar.

Beginner's Guide

1. What is KL Divergence?

KL Divergence measures the "surprise" or inefficiency of using a simplified model when you know the true state of the world. Let's say P is the true probability distribution of weather patterns. You, as a forecaster, create a simplified model, Q. KL Divergence DKL(P || Q) quantifies how much "information" you lose by using your model Q instead of the true distribution P.

  • If your model Q is identical to the true distribution P, the KL Divergence is zero. You lose no information.
  • The more Q differs from P, the larger the KL Divergence becomes.

Try it in the simulation: Set the sliders for P and Q to be identical. The KL divergence will be 0. Now, slowly change the mean (µ) or standard deviation (σ) of Q. You'll see the DKL(P || Q) value increase.

2. The Asymmetry: Why DKL(P || Q) ≠ DKL(Q || P)

This is the trickiest but most important property of KL Divergence. It is not a true distance like meters or miles, because the distance from A to B is not always the same as the distance from B to A.

Consider the term DKL(P || Q). Here, P is the "true" distribution. We are measuring the penalty for using Q when the truth is P. The penalty is high where p(x) is high and q(x) is low. This is like being very surprised: an event happened that you thought was impossible according to your model Q.

Now consider DKL(Q || P). Here, we pretend Q is the truth and P is our model. The penalty is high where q(x) is high and p(x) is low.

Try this in the simulation:

  1. Set P to have a small sigma (a narrow, spiky peak), like σP = 0.5.
  2. Set Q to have a large sigma (a wide, flat peak), like σQ = 3.0.
  3. Look at DKL(P || Q). It will be very large. This is because Q fails to model the high-probability event at the peak of P. It's very "surprised" by the certainty of P.
  4. Now look at DKL(Q || P). It will be much smaller. This is because P does a decent job of modeling events from Q. If you pick a point from the wide Q distribution, it's likely to be somewhere P already assigns some probability. P is rarely "surprised" by Q.

The Formula for KL Divergence

For two continuous probability distributions p(x) and q(x), the KL divergence is defined as the integral:

DKL(P || Q) = ∫ p(x) log( p(x) / q(x) ) dx

This integral can be difficult to compute for arbitrary distributions. Fortunately, for two 1D Gaussian (Normal) distributions, P ~ N(μ1, σ12) and Q ~ N(μ2, σ22), there is a convenient closed-form solution, which is what this simulation uses:

DKL(P || Q) = log(σ2/σ1) + (σ12 + (μ1-μ2)2) / (2σ22) - ½

This formula directly shows the non-symmetry. If you swap the roles of P and Q (i.e., swap all the 1's and 2's), you will get a different mathematical expression.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. If it's not a distance, what is it?
It's a "divergence." It measures how one distribution diverges from another. It's also known as "relative entropy," as it measures the entropy of P relative to Q.
2. Why does the value sometimes become "Infinity"?
This happens if there is a place where p(x) > 0 but q(x) = 0. This corresponds to an event that is possible under the true distribution P but which your model Q said was absolutely impossible. The "surprise" is infinite, and the KL divergence reflects that. In our simulation, this doesn't happen because Gaussian distributions are never exactly zero.
3. What is this used for in practice?
KL Divergence is fundamental to machine learning and statistics. It's used in Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) to ensure the learned latent space resembles a simple distribution (like a standard normal distribution). It's also used to compare the performance of different statistical models.
