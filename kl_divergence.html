Plain-Language Overview

In statistics and machine learning, we often need to compare how different one probability distribution is from another. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence is a key tool for this. It measures the "information loss" when we use an approximate distribution (Q) to model a true distribution (P). Think of it as a kind of "distance," but with a twist: the distance from P to Q is not the same as the distance from Q to P! This simulation lets you control two distributions, P (True) and Q (Approximate), and see how the KL Divergence changes as you make them more or less similar.

Beginner's Guide

1. What is KL Divergence?

KL Divergence measures the "surprise" or inefficiency of using a simplified model when you know the true state of the world. Let's say P is the true probability distribution of heights of people. You create a model, Q, to approximate it. KL Divergence DKL(P || Q) quantifies how much "information" you lose by using your model Q instead of the truth P.

  • If your model Q is identical to P, the KL Divergence is zero. You lose no information. Try this by clicking the "P = Q" preset.
  • The more Q differs from P, the larger the KL Divergence becomes.

2. The Asymmetry: Why DKL(P || Q) ≠ DKL(Q || P)

This is the trickiest but most important property. It is not a true distance. The "distance" from P to Q is not the same as from Q to P.

DKL(P || Q) measures the penalty for using Q when the truth is P. The penalty is high where p(x) is high and q(x) is low. This is like being very surprised: an event happened that P said was likely, but your model Q said was very unlikely.

Try this: Click the "P Narrow, Q Wide" preset. DKL(P || Q) is large. Your wide model Q is "surprised" because it fails to capture the certainty of the narrow, spiky P. Now, click the "P Wide, Q Narrow" preset. Notice that DKL(P || Q) is much smaller this time. Your narrow model Q is less "surprised" by the wide P, because anywhere Q predicts a high probability, P also has a reasonable probability.

Core Theory

For two continuous probability distributions p(x) and q(x), the KL divergence is defined as: DKL(P || Q) = ∫ p(x) log( p(x) / q(x) ) dx. For two 1D Gaussian distributions, P ~ N(μ₁, σ₁²) and Q ~ N(μ₂, σ₂²), this has a closed-form solution:

DKL(P || Q) = log(σ₂/σ₁) + (σ₁² + (μ₁-μ₂)²) / (2σ₂²) - ½

This formula reveals the asymmetry. The term log(σ₂/σ₁) becomes log(σ₁/σ₂) when swapping P and Q, which is not the same. Likewise, the main fraction term has σ₂ in the denominator, which becomes σ₁ when swapped.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

If it's not a distance, what is it?
It's a "divergence." It measures how one distribution diverges from another. It's also known as "relative entropy," as it measures the entropy of P relative to Q.
What is this used for in practice?
KL Divergence is fundamental to machine learning. It's used in Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) to ensure the learned latent space resembles a simple distribution and in reinforcement learning to constrain policy updates.
