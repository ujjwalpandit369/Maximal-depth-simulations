Overview

This simulation lets you become a master of electric fields. You can place positive and negative charges on a 2D canvas and instantly see the invisible "potential energy landscape" they create. This landscape is visualized as a color-coded map where positive charges form "hills" and negative charges create "valleys." As you drag a charge around, you'll see a real-time calculation of the "work" you are doing against the electric field, which directly corresponds to the change in the system's total potential energy. This provides a tangible, intuitive feel for abstract concepts like voltage, potential, and electrostatic energy.

Beginner's Guide to Electric Potential

Let's demystify the energy that holds atoms together and powers our world. This guide uses a simple analogy to explain the concepts you'll see in the simulation.

Step 1: The Potential Energy Landscape

The most important feature of this simulation is the colored background. This is a map of the Electric Potential (often called voltage). It's an invisible landscape of energy that exists around electric charges.

Everyday Analogy: A Landscape of Hills and Valleys.

  • A positive charge (+) is like a big hill. It pushes other positive charges away.
  • A negative charge (-) is like a deep valley or well. It pulls positive charges in.

The color tells you the "elevation" of this landscape: red areas are high-potential "hills," blue areas are low-potential "valleys," and green areas are neutral ground.

Step 2: What is Potential Energy?

Now, imagine you have a small positive "test" charge (like a marble). The Electrostatic Potential Energy (U) is the energy your marble has because of its position in this landscape. If your marble is on top of a hill (near another positive charge), it has high potential energy—it "wants" to roll away. If it's in a valley (near a negative charge), it has low potential energy.

The simulation constantly calculates the Total Potential Energy of the entire system of charges you've built. Pushing two like charges together (pushing a marble up a hill) increases the total energy. Letting two opposite charges get closer (letting a marble roll into a valley) decreases it.

Step 3: What is "Work Done"?

In physics, "work" is the energy you expend to move something against a force. In our analogy, work is the effort it takes to push the marble around the landscape.

  • If you drag a positive charge towards another positive charge (uphill), you are doing positive work. The "Work Done" counter will increase.
  • If you drag a positive charge away from a negative charge (out of a valley), you are also doing positive work.
  • If you let a charge be pushed or pulled by the field (rolling downhill), the field is doing the work, and the counter for your work done will decrease (show negative work).

The key takeaway is that the Work you do is exactly equal to the change in the system's Total Potential Energy. This is a fundamental concept in electromagnetism.

Core Theory: Potential and Energy

The simulation is built on two key equations from electrostatics.

1. Electric Potential (Voltage)

The electric potential V at a point in space is the sum of the potentials from all charges:

V(point) = Σ (k * qᵢ) / rᵢ

Plain-English Translation: For every charge (qᵢ) in your system, you calculate its contribution to the potential at a specific point. This contribution is stronger if the charge is bigger (more q) and weaker the farther away it is (bigger rᵢ). The colored background is a plot of this V value at every point.

2. Electrostatic Potential Energy

The total potential energy U of the whole system is calculated by looking at every unique pair of charges:

U_total = Σ (for i < j) (k * qᵢ * qⱼ) / rᵢⱼ

Plain-English Translation: For every pair of charges, their contribution to the total energy is calculated. This energy is positive for two like charges (repulsive, adds energy to the system) and negative for two opposite charges (attractive, lowers the energy of the system). The simulation adds all of these pair energies up to get the total.

When you drag a charge, the "Work Done" is simply U_after_drag - U_before_drag.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. What are the units of energy and potential?
For simplicity, the simulation uses arbitrary units. The key is to look at the relative values and how they change, not the absolute numbers themselves. `k` is set to a convenient value for the display.
2. Why does the landscape color change when I move a charge?
Because the potential at every point in space depends on the position of ALL charges. When you move one, you change its distance to every point on the map, so the entire potential landscape has to be recalculated.
3. Why does the "Work Done" value reset to zero when I let go of a charge?
The "Work Done" counter is designed to show you the energy change during a single drag operation. Once you release the charge, that work has been "put into" the system and is now part of the new "Total Potential Energy".
4. The simulation is slow when I have many charges.
Calculating the potential landscape is computationally intensive. For each point on the background grid, we have to calculate its distance to every charge. With many charges, this can be slow. The simulation uses a coarse grid to maintain performance, but with 20+ charges, you may notice a slight lag.
5. How do I make a dipole?
Place one positive charge and one negative charge very close to each other. You will see the characteristic dipole potential field: strongly positive near the '+' charge, strongly negative near the '-' charge, and a neutral line halfway between them.
