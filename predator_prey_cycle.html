Guide: The Dance of Life and Death

The Cycle of Nature

In many ecosystems, the populations of predators and their prey are locked in a timeless dance. This simulation models that dance for a simple system of rabbits (prey) and foxes (predators). The cycle unfolds intuitively:

  1. With few predators, the rabbit population grows exponentially.
  2. More rabbits mean more food for foxes, so the fox population begins to thrive.
  3. The increased fox population eats rabbits faster than they can reproduce, causing the rabbit population to crash.
  4. With their food source gone, the fox population starves and its numbers plummet.
  5. With few predators once again, the rabbit population recovers, and the cycle begins anew.
This system doesn't settle on fixed numbers but instead finds a dynamic equilibrium, oscillating in a stable cycle.

Reading the Graphs

  • Population vs. Time: The graph on the right shows this story over time. Notice how the predator population peak (red) always comes slightly after the prey population peak (blue).
  • Phase Space Plot: The graph on the left shows the relationship directly, plotting the number of predators against the number of prey. The system endlessly traces a closed loop, called a limit cycle. Every point on this loop represents a specific state of the ecosystem (e.g., "many rabbits, few foxes"). The circular nature of the graph is the signature of this stable, oscillating equilibrium.

The Lotka-Volterra Equations

This classic ecological model is described by a pair of coupled first-order nonlinear differential equations:

dx/dt = αx - βxy  (Prey Population, x)
dy/dt = δxy - γy  (Predator Population, y)

Each term has a clear physical meaning:

  • αx: This is the natural growth rate of the prey population (x). Without predators, they would grow exponentially. α is their birth rate.
  • -βxy: This represents the rate at which prey are eaten by predators. It's proportional to the product of both populations because the rate of encounters depends on how many of each there are. β is the predation rate.
  • δxy: This is the growth rate of the predator population (y). It is also proportional to the encounter rate, but is modified by δ, the efficiency of converting prey into new predators.
  • -γy: This is the natural death rate of the predator population. Without prey to eat, they would die out exponentially. γ is their death rate.
