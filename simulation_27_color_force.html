Plain-Language Overview

The force that binds quarks together to form protons and neutrons is called the strong nuclear force. The theory describing it, Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD), introduces a new kind of charge called "color charge." This simulation visualizes the fundamental process of the strong force: the exchange of a force-carrying particle called a **gluon** between two quarks. You will see how this exchange changes the "color" of the quarks involved, while always keeping the overall particle color-neutral. This constant, frantic exchange of gluons is what creates the strongest force in nature.

Beginner's Guide — Swapping Colors

Step 1: Color Charge

In addition to electric charge, quarks possess a different kind of charge called **color charge**. This has nothing to do with visual colors; it's just a catchy name for a physical property. There are three types of color charge: Red, Green, and Blue. There are also corresponding "anti-colors" for antiquarks.

Step 2: The Rule of "Whiteness"

A fundamental rule of the strong force is that any particle that can exist freely must be "color-neutral" or "white." This can be achieved by combining colors. In our simulation, we show a proton, which is a **baryon**. A baryon is made of three quarks, one of each color: Red + Green + Blue = White. (Another way to be color-neutral is a meson, made of a color and its anti-color, like Red + Anti-Red).

Step 3: The Gluon - A Two-Colored Messenger

The strong force is carried by particles called **gluons**. The key feature of a gluon is that it carries color charge itself. A gluon carries one color and one *anti-color*. For example, a "Red-antiGreen" gluon. This is very different from the photon, which carries the electromagnetic force but has no electric charge itself.

Step 4: The Exchange

Click the "Exchange Gluon" button to see the process.

  1. A quark emits a gluon. To conserve color, the quark must change its own color. For example, a Red quark can emit a Red-antiGreen gluon. After doing so, the original quark is left with only the "uncanceled" color, so it becomes Green.
  2. The gluon travels to another quark.
  3. The second quark absorbs the gluon. For example, if a Green quark absorbs the Red-antiGreen gluon, its Green color is canceled by the antiGreen part of the gluon, and it takes on the gluon's remaining color, becoming Red.
The two quarks have swapped colors, and the overall proton remains color-neutral (it still has one Red, one Green, and one Blue quark). This continuous exchange is the source of the strong force.

Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD)

The theory of the strong force is called Quantum Chromodynamics. It is a "gauge theory" based on a mathematical symmetry group called SU(3). The "3" in SU(3) corresponds to the three color charges. The theory describes the interactions between the quarks (which are fermions) and the gluons (which are vector bosons).

Gluon Properties

Because the SU(3) group is more complex than the U(1) group of electromagnetism, there are 8 different types of gluons, not just one type of photon. They are complex linear combinations of color and anti-color pairs. For example, one gluon might be `(r anti-b + b anti-r) / sqrt(2)`. For this simulation, we simplify this greatly and represent a gluon as a simple `color-anticolor` pair to make the color-changing mechanism clear.

Color Conservation

The core principle is that color charge is always conserved at each interaction vertex.

  • Emission: When a quark emits a gluon, the outgoing quark's color plus the gluon's color must equal the incoming quark's color. This is why `Red → Green` requires emitting a `Red-antiGreen` gluon.
  • Absorption: When a quark absorbs a gluon, the gluon's anti-color annihilates the quark's color, and the quark takes on the gluon's color. `Green + (Red-antiGreen) → Red`.
