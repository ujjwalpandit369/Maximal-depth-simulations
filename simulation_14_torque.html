Plain-Language Overview

Why is it easier to open a door by pushing on the edge far from the hinges? Why does a figure skater spin faster when they pull their arms in? The answers lie in the physics of rotation: torque and moment of inertia. Torque is the rotational equivalent of a force—it's a "twist" that causes an object to change its rate of spin. Moment of inertia is the rotational equivalent of mass—it's a measure of an object's resistance to being spun. This interactive simulation lets you apply forces to a turntable to build an intuition for how these concepts work together.

Beginner's Guide — How to Spin a Turntable

In this simulation, you can apply a force to a turntable to make it spin. Click and hold to set the point of application, then drag to define the force vector.

Step 1: Force vs. Torque

A force is a simple push or pull that causes an object to accelerate in a straight line (`F=ma`). A torque (τ) is a twist or turn that causes an object to have an *angular* acceleration (`τ=Iα`). Torque depends not just on how hard you push, but also on *where* and in *what direction* you push.

Step 2: The Importance of "Where" (The Lever Arm, `r`)

Try applying a force near the center of the turntable, and then apply a similar force near the edge. You'll find the force at the edge is much more effective at making it spin. The distance from the pivot point (the center) to where you apply the force is called the lever arm vector (`r`). A longer lever arm produces a larger torque. This is why door handles are placed far from the hinges.

Step 3: The Importance of "How" (The Angle)

Now, try applying a force at the edge, but aim it directly towards the center of the turntable. Nothing happens! To be effective, the force must have a component that is perpendicular to the lever arm. The maximum torque is achieved when the force is perfectly perpendicular to the lever arm. This is why you pull a wrench at a right angle, not along its length.

Step 4: Resistance to Rotation (Moment of Inertia, `I`)

Just as mass resists linear acceleration, the moment of inertia (`I`) resists angular acceleration. It depends on an object's mass and how that mass is distributed. An object with more mass, or whose mass is farther from the center of rotation, has a higher moment of inertia and is harder to spin. Use the sliders to increase the turntable's mass and radius and see how it becomes more "sluggish" and resistant to your applied torques.

Torque (`τ`)

Torque is the rotational equivalent of force. It is defined as the cross product of the lever arm vector `r` (from the pivot to the point of force application) and the force vector `F`.

τ = r × F

The magnitude of the torque is given by `|τ| = |r| * |F| * sin(θ)`, where `θ` is the angle between the `r` and `F` vectors. In our 2D simulation, this simplifies to `τ = r_x * F_y - r_y * F_x`.

Moment of Inertia (`I`)

The moment of inertia is the rotational equivalent of mass. It measures an object's resistance to changes in its rotational motion. For a solid disk rotating about its center, it is:

I = (1/2) * M * R²

Where `M` is the total mass and `R` is the radius of the disk.

Newton's Second Law for Rotation

Just as `F = ma` relates force, mass, and linear acceleration, its rotational equivalent relates net torque, moment of inertia, and angular acceleration (`α`).

τ_net = I * α

This means the angular acceleration of the turntable is directly proportional to the net torque you apply and inversely proportional to its moment of inertia: `α = τ_net / I`.
