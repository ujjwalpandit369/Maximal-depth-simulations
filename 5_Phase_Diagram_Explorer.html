Plain-Language Overview

Water can be a solid (ice), a liquid, or a gas (steam). What state it's in depends on its temperature and pressure. A "phase diagram" is a map that shows you exactly what state a substance will be in under any given conditions. This simulation provides an interactive phase diagram. You can move a point around the map by changing the temperature and pressure with sliders. As you do, a second window shows you what the particles of the substance would look like at a microscopic level—from an ordered, vibrating solid to a disordered liquid to a chaotic, spread-out gas. By exploring the map, you can discover the conditions for melting, boiling, and even sublimating (going straight from solid to gas), and see special locations like the "triple point," where all three states can exist at once!

Beginner's Guide: A Map of Matter

Step 1: The States of Matter

We are familiar with the three most common states of matter:

  • Solid: Particles are locked into a fixed, orderly pattern (a crystal lattice). They vibrate but cannot move past each other. Solids have a fixed shape and volume.
  • Liquid: Particles are still close together but are disordered and can slide past one another. Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container.
  • Gas: Particles are far apart and move randomly and rapidly. A gas will expand to fill any container it is in.
The small "Microscopic View" canvas shows a simplified animation of these states.

Step 2: The Phase Diagram

The large canvas shows the P-T (Pressure-Temperature) phase diagram. Think of it as a map. The horizontal axis is Temperature, and the vertical axis is Pressure. Any point on this map represents a specific set of conditions. Depending on which region your point is in, the substance will be a solid, liquid, or gas. You can move the red "state point" by using the sliders or by dragging it directly on the diagram.

Step 3: Crossing the Lines (Phase Transitions)

The lines on the map are the phase boundaries. When you move the state point across a line, the substance undergoes a phase transition:

  • Solid-Liquid Line (Fusion Curve): Crossing this line from left to right is melting. Crossing from right to left is freezing.
  • Liquid-Gas Line (Vaporization Curve): Crossing this line from left to right is boiling. Crossing from right to left is condensation.
  • Solid-Gas Line (Sublimation Curve): Crossing this line from left to right is sublimation (like how dry ice turns directly into CO₂ gas). Crossing from right to left is deposition.

Step 4: Special Points on the Map

There are two very special locations on the phase diagram that are marked for you:

  • The Triple Point (TP): This is the unique point where all three phase boundaries meet. At this exact temperature and pressure, the solid, liquid, and gas phases can all exist in equilibrium at the same time!
  • The Critical Point (CP): This is the point where the liquid-gas boundary line simply ends. Beyond this point, at very high temperatures and pressures, the distinction between a liquid and a gas vanishes. The substance becomes a "supercritical fluid," which has properties of both.

Core Theory: The Clausius-Clapeyron Relation

The slopes of the phase boundary lines are not arbitrary. They are governed by the laws of thermodynamics, specifically the Clausius-Clapeyron relation. This equation relates the slope of a boundary line (dP/dT) to the change in enthalpy (latent heat, ΔH) and the change in volume (ΔV) during the transition.

dP/dT = ΔH / (T ΔV)

Plain English: The slope of the line depends on how much energy is needed for the transition (latent heat) and how much the volume changes.

  • For boiling, ΔV is large and positive (liquid to gas), so the slope is positive and relatively shallow.
  • For melting, ΔV is usually small and positive (solid to liquid), so the slope is positive and very steep.
  • Special Case - Water: Ice is less dense than liquid water, so for water's melting transition, ΔV is negative. This means the dP/dT slope is negative, which is why the solid-liquid line on water's phase diagram uniquely slopes to the left. This is why ice skates work: the high pressure from the blade melts the ice underneath it.
