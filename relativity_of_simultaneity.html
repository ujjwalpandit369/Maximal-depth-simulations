The Relativity of Simultaneity

The train and lightning thought experiment.

Plain-Language Overview

We usually think of "simultaneous" as an absolute concept: either two things happen at the same time, or they don't. However, Einstein's special relativity reveals that the answer depends on who you ask! This simulation shows his famous thought experiment: a person on a train and a person on a platform observe two lightning strikes that hit the front and back of the train. From the platform, the strikes look simultaneous. But because the person on the train is moving, they will see the light from the front strike before the light from the back strike, and conclude that the events were not simultaneous. This simulation lets you see the experiment unfold from both points of view to understand how two observers can be equally correct yet disagree on the timing of events.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Setup

We have a train moving along a track. There is an observer (T) in the exact middle of the train car and another observer (P) on the platform, who is also at the midpoint of the train's length *at the moment the lightning strikes*. Two lightning bolts strike the very front and very back of the train car.

2. The View from the Platform

Select the "View from Platform". This is the default view. When you click "Run Experiment," you see two lightning bolts strike the ends of the moving train at the same time. Light from each strike travels inwards towards Observer P. Since Observer P is exactly in the middle and is not moving, the two light pulses travel the same distance and reach them at the exact same time. Observer P rightfully concludes: "I saw both flashes at once, so the lightning strikes must have been simultaneous."

But what does Observer P see happening to Observer T on the train? Observer T is moving towards the light flash from the front of the train and away from the flash from the back. Therefore, the light from the front has a shorter distance to travel to reach Observer T than the light from the back. Observer P sees the front flash reach Observer T first, followed by the back flash.

3. The View from the Train

Now, select the "View from Train". According to the principle of relativity, Observer T is justified in considering themself to be at rest. From their perspective, the train is stationary and the platform is moving backwards. In their frame, the speed of light is still constant. Since they *observed* the front flash first, they must conclude that the lightning strike at the front of the train *actually happened* before the strike at the back.

Click "Run Experiment" in this view. The animation now shows the events from the train's perspective. You will see the front lightning strike happen first, and then a short time later, the back lightning strike happens. This is the only way for the physics to be consistent. Both observers' conclusions are correct within their own frame of reference.

4. The Conclusion: Simultaneity is Relative

There is no universal "now" across all of space. Whether two events are simultaneous depends on your state of motion. This is one of the most profound consequences of the two postulates of special relativity and resolves countless apparent paradoxes, including the "spaceship in the tunnel" problem from the previous simulation.

The Lorentz Transformation for Time

The relativity of simultaneity is a direct mathematical consequence of the Lorentz transformations. The transformation for the time coordinate is:

t' = γ ( t - vx/c2 )

Let's analyze this. In the platform frame (the unprimed frame), we have two events:

  • Back strike: (x1, t1)
  • Front strike: (x2, t1)
Notice they happen at the same time t1, but at different locations x1 and x2.

Now let's find the times of these events in the train's frame (the primed frame):
t'1 = γ(t1 - vx1/c2)
t'2 = γ(t1 - vx2/c2)

Since x1 ≠ x2, it is clear that t'1 ≠ t'2. The events are not simultaneous in the train's frame. The term -vx/c2 is the mathematical embodiment of the relativity of simultaneity.
