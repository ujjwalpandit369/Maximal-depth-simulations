Plain-Language Overview

The Standard Model of Particle Physics is our best description of the fundamental building blocks of the universe. It divides particles into two families: **fermions** (which make up matter, like electrons and quarks) and **bosons** (which carry forces, like photons). Supersymmetry, or SUSY, is a proposed extension to this model that suggests a deep, underlying symmetry between these two families. It predicts that every known particle has a "superpartner" with different properties. This interactive chart lets you explore the particles of the Standard Model and reveal their hypothetical supersymmetric partners.

Beginner's Guide — A Partner for Every Particle

Step 1: The Two Families of Particles

All known fundamental particles belong to one of two groups, based on a quantum property called "spin."

  • Fermions (Spin ½): These are the "matter" particles. They are the bricks and mortar of the universe. Quarks (which build protons and neutrons) and leptons (like the electron) are fermions. They obey the Pauli Exclusion Principle, which means no two fermions can occupy the same quantum state—it's why matter is solid and takes up space.
  • Bosons (Spin 0 or 1): These are the "force" particles. They are the messengers or carriers of the fundamental forces. The photon (carries electromagnetism), the gluon (strong force), and the W/Z bosons (weak force) are bosons. The Higgs boson is also in this family.
In the Standard Model, these two families are completely distinct.

Step 2: The Proposed Symmetry

Supersymmetry proposes a beautiful and profound new symmetry in nature: for every fermion, there should exist a corresponding boson, and for every boson, a corresponding fermion. They come in pairs. This hypothetical new particle is called a **superpartner**. If SUSY is correct, the number of fundamental particles is doubled!

Step 3: Meet the "Sparticles"

Physicists have come up with a simple naming convention for these hypothetical superpartners ("sparticles"). Click the "Show Supersymmetry" button to reveal them.

  • The superpartner of a **fermion** is named by adding an "s-" prefix. The partner of an electron is a **selectron**. The partner of a quark is a **squark**. These "sfermions" are bosons.
  • The superpartner of a **boson** is named by adding an "-ino" suffix. The partner of a photon is a **photino**. The partner of a gluon is a **gluino**. The partner of a Higgs is a **Higgsino**. These "bosinos" are fermions.

Step 4: Why Haven't We Found Them?

If every particle has a partner, where are they? If SUSY were a perfect symmetry, the superpartners would have the same mass as their standard counterparts (e.g., the selectron would have the same mass as the electron). We would have discovered them easily. Since we haven't, physicists conclude that SUSY must be a "broken symmetry," meaning the superpartners are much, much heavier than the known particles, and we need very powerful particle colliders like the LHC to have a chance of creating them.

Supersymmetry is an elegant and powerful idea, but it wasn't proposed just for its beauty. It also provides potential solutions to some of the deepest problems in modern physics.

  • The Hierarchy Problem: There is a huge, unexplained discrepancy between the scale of the weak force and the scale of gravity. Quantum corrections should make the Higgs boson's mass enormous, but it's relatively light. SUSY provides a mechanism with new particles that naturally cancel out these problematic corrections, stabilizing the Higgs mass.
  • Dark Matter Candidate: In most SUSY models, the superpartners can't decay into normal particles. This means the Lightest Supersymmetric Particle (LSP) must be stable. This LSP is predicted to be massive and only interact weakly—the exact properties required for the mysterious dark matter that makes up 25% of the universe's energy content.
  • Grand Unification: If you plot the strengths of the three fundamental forces (strong, weak, electromagnetic) against energy, they almost, but not quite, meet at a single point at very high energy. If you include the effects of the superpartners, the three forces meet perfectly. SUSY provides a path towards a Grand Unified Theory (GUT) where all three forces were once one.

Despite these powerful motivations, no experimental evidence for supersymmetry has yet been found at the Large Hadron Collider or other experiments.
