Plain-Language Overview

A pendulum's swing is one of the most classic images in physics. But not all pendulums are created equal. A "simple pendulum" is an idealized model with a heavy bob on a weightless string. A "physical pendulum" is any real, rigid object that can swing, like a meter stick or a baseball bat. This simulation compares these two types side-by-side. You'll see a simple pendulum and a solid rod of the exact same length released from the same angle. Which one do you think swings faster? The result might surprise you, and it reveals a deep concept about how an object's shape and mass distribution determine its motion.

Beginner's Guide: Why Shape Matters

Step 1: The Simple Pendulum

The blue pendulum on the left is a **simple pendulum**. This is the version you learn about first in physics class. It's an idealization where we assume all the mass is concentrated in a single point (the "bob") at the end of a completely massless string or rod of length L. For small swings, its period—the time it takes to complete one full back-and-forth swing—depends only on its length and the strength of gravity.

Step 2: The Physical Pendulum

The red pendulum on the right is a **physical pendulum**. This represents any real-world object that can swing. In this case, it's a solid, uniform rod, also of length L. Unlike the simple pendulum, its mass is not concentrated at the end; it's distributed evenly along its entire length. The small 'x' marks its center of mass, which is at the middle of the rod (L/2).

Step 3: The Race

Click the **"Release from small angle"** button. Both pendulums are released from the same starting angle at the same time. Watch them swing. You'll immediately notice that the **physical pendulum (the rod) swings faster** and completes a full cycle in less time than the simple pendulum. The timers in the readout panel below will confirm this measurement.

Step 4: Why is the Rod Faster?

This seems counterintuitive to many people. The reason lies in a concept called **moment of inertia (I)**, which is the rotational equivalent of mass. It's a measure of an object's "rotational laziness," or how hard it is to get it to spin or swing.

  • For the simple pendulum, all the mass is at the very end, as far as possible from the pivot. This gives it a large moment of inertia.
  • For the rod, the mass is distributed all along its length. On average, its mass is closer to the pivot than the simple pendulum's mass. This gives it a smaller moment of inertia.
Because the rod has less "rotational laziness" for the same length, gravity can make it swing more quickly. Its period is shorter.

Core Theory: The Period of a Pendulum

1. The Simple Pendulum

For small angles of oscillation, the period T of a simple pendulum depends only on its length L and the acceleration due to gravity g.

Tsimple = 2π √(L/g)

2. The Physical Pendulum

The period of any physical pendulum is given by a more general formula:

Tphysical = 2π √(I / mgd)

Where:

  • I is the moment of inertia of the object about the pivot point.
  • m is the total mass of the object.
  • d is the distance from the pivot to the object's center of mass.

3. Applying to a Uniform Rod

For a uniform rod of length L and mass m pivoted at one end:

  • The center of mass is at its geometric center: d = L/2.
  • The moment of inertia about one end is I = (1/3)mL².
Substituting these into the general formula gives:
Trod = 2π √((1/3)mL² / (mg(L/2)))
After simplifying by canceling `m` and `L`:
Trod = 2π √((2/3)L / g)

Comparing this to the simple pendulum's formula, you can see that the rod's period is shorter by a factor of √(2/3) ≈ 0.816.
