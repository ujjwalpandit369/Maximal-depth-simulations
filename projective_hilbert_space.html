Welcome to the Quantum World of Qubits!

In classical computing, a bit is either 0 or 1. A quantum bit, or qubit, can be in a "superposition" of both states at once. The Bloch Sphere is a beautiful way to visualize all possible states of a single qubit.

Step 1: The State on the Sphere

A qubit's state is represented by an arrow pointing from the center of the sphere to its surface.

  • The North Pole represents the pure state |0⟩.
  • The South Pole represents the pure state |1⟩.
  • Any other point on the surface is a superposition of |0⟩ and |1⟩.
Use the Polar Angle (θ) and Azimuthal Angle (φ) sliders to move the state vector around the sphere and explore different superpositions.

Step 2: The Hidden Dimension - Global Phase

In quantum mechanics, the overall "phase" of a state vector doesn't change any physical measurement. This is called a global phase. The equation for a quantum state is `|ψ⟩`, but the state `e^(iα)|ψ⟩` is physically identical for any angle `α`.

This is the most important part of the simulation. Use the Global Phase (α) slider. Watch the sphere closely.

Step 3: What Changed? (And What Didn't)

As you move the `α` slider, notice two things:

  1. The main arrow representing the state on the Bloch sphere does not move at all.
  2. A small "phase clock" or ring drawn on the arrow's shaft does rotate.
This is the key insight! The rotating clock represents the changing global phase `e^(iα)`. But since this phase has no physical consequence, the actual state on the Bloch sphere remains fixed. The Bloch sphere doesn't represent the raw state vectors in Hilbert space, but the "rays" of physically equivalent vectors. This space of rays is called a Projective Hilbert Space.

In essence, the Bloch sphere is a "quotient space" where we have "divided out" the un-physical global phase, showing us only the physically distinct states. All the vectors that differ only by a global phase are projected down to the same single point on the sphere.
