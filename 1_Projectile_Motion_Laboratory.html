Plain-Language Overview

From throwing a ball to launching a rocket, understanding how objects fly through the air is a classic physics problem. This interactive laboratory lets you become the scientist. You can control a cannon to explore the principles of projectile motion. Adjust the launch angle, initial speed, and even the strength of gravity to see how they change the projectile's path. For a more realistic challenge, you can add air resistance and observe how it makes the trajectory shorter and asymmetric. With real-time data readouts for range and height, you can run experiments to find the optimal launch angle and see firsthand how the laws of motion govern the world around us.

Beginner's Guide: The Two Simple Motions

Step 1: The Core Idea

The key to understanding projectile motion is to realize it's made of two separate and independent motions happening at the same time:

  1. Horizontal Motion: If we ignore air resistance, there are no forces acting on the projectile in the horizontal direction. This means its horizontal velocity is constant. It just cruises along at a steady speed.
  2. Vertical Motion: In the vertical direction, the projectile is constantly being pulled downwards by gravity. This means it has a constant downward acceleration. It starts by moving up, slows down, reaches a peak, and then speeds up on its way back down, just like a ball thrown straight up.
When you combine these two motions, you get the familiar curved path, a parabola.

Step 2: The Controls

This lab gives you control over all the important variables:

  • Initial Velocity (v₀): How fast the projectile is going the instant it leaves the cannon. More speed means it goes farther and higher.
  • Launch Angle (θ): The angle of the cannon. 0° fires horizontally, 90° fires straight up. You'll find the best angle for maximum distance is right in between.
  • Gravitational Acceleration (g): The strength of gravity. On Earth, it's 9.8 m/s². On Mars, it's only 3.7 m/s², so projectiles would fly much farther.
  • Cannon Height (y₀): The starting height of the cannon. Firing from a cliff gives you more time in the air, and thus more range.

Step 3: The Complication - Air Resistance

In the real world, the air pushes back on moving objects. This force, called air resistance or drag, always acts opposite to the direction of motion. You can add this effect using the **Air Resistance** slider. Notice what happens when you turn it on:

  • The trajectory is no longer a perfect parabola. It becomes lopsided.
  • The projectile doesn't go as high or as far.
  • The optimal launch angle for maximum range is no longer 45°; it's something lower.

Step 4: Running Experiments

Now you can be a physicist! Try to answer these questions using the simulation:

  • With no air resistance, what launch angle gives the maximum range? Is it the same for all initial velocities?
  • If you fire a projectile from a height, is the optimal angle still 45°?
  • How does adding air resistance change the optimal angle?
  • If you want to hit a target at a specific distance, are there two different angles you could use?

Core Theory: Equations of Motion

1. Ideal Motion (No Air Resistance)

The motion is decoupled into horizontal (x) and vertical (y) components. The initial velocity components are vx,0 = v₀ cos(θ) and vy,0 = v₀ sin(θ).

Horizontal: x(t) = x₀ + vx,0 t

Vertical: y(t) = y₀ + vy,0 t - ½gt²

2. Motion with Air Resistance

When air resistance is included, the acceleration is no longer constant. A common model for the drag force is Fdrag = -k|v|v, where k is a factor related to the drag coefficient, air density, and projectile shape. This leads to coupled differential equations:

ax = dvx/dt = -(k/m) √(vx²+vy²) vx

ay = dvy/dt = -g - (k/m) √(vx²+vy²) vy

These equations do not have a simple analytical solution and must be solved numerically, step-by-step. This simulation uses the Euler-Cromer method, a simple and stable numerical integration technique, to calculate the path when drag is enabled.
