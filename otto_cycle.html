The Otto Cycle

A simulation of the thermodynamic cycle of a four-stroke gasoline engine.

Overview

The Otto cycle is the idealized thermodynamic cycle that describes the functioning of a typical spark-ignition piston engine. It's the core process that converts the chemical energy in gasoline into the motion that powers most cars. This simulation provides a synchronized view of the two key aspects of the engine: the physical movement of the piston within the cylinder and the abstract changes in pressure and volume represented on a P-V diagram. By adjusting the engine's parameters, you can see how they affect the work done and the overall thermal efficiency.

The Four Strokes of a Gasoline Engine

The Otto cycle consists of four distinct processes, or "strokes." Follow along with the "Step" button to see each one.

  1. Intake (1 → 2 on P-V diagram): The piston moves down, increasing the volume in the cylinder and drawing in a mixture of fuel and air at roughly constant atmospheric pressure.
  2. Compression (2 → 3): The intake valve closes, and the piston moves up, compressing the fuel-air mixture. This is an adiabatic process, meaning no heat is exchanged with the surroundings, so both pressure and temperature rise significantly.
  3. Power / Combustion (3 → 4 → 5):
    • At the peak of compression, a spark plug ignites the mixture. This causes a near-instantaneous combustion, rapidly increasing the temperature and pressure at a constant volume (an isochoric process).
    • This high pressure exerts a massive force on the piston, pushing it down in the power stroke. This is another adiabatic process where the expanding gas does work.
  4. Exhaust (5 → 2 → 1):
    • At the bottom of the power stroke, the exhaust valve opens. The pressure drops instantly at constant volume as heat is rejected.
    • The piston moves up, pushing the remaining exhaust gases out of the cylinder at constant pressure. The cycle is then ready to begin again.

The P-V Diagram

The P-V (Pressure-Volume) diagram is a crucial tool for engineers. The path traced by the cycle shows how pressure and volume change. Most importantly, the area enclosed by the loop represents the net work done by the engine in one cycle. A larger area means more work and a more powerful engine for its size.

Parameters & Efficiency

The efficiency of an ideal Otto cycle is determined by a surprisingly simple formula that depends only on the compression ratio and the properties of the gas.

η = 1 - 1 / (r^(γ-1))
  • η is the Thermal Efficiency: The ratio of work done to heat energy supplied. An efficiency of 60% means 60% of the fuel's heat energy was converted into useful work.
  • r is the Compression Ratio: The ratio of the maximum volume to the minimum volume in the cylinder. As you increase this with the slider, you'll see the efficiency rise dramatically. This is why high-performance engines use high compression ratios.
  • γ (gamma) is the Specific Heat Ratio of the gas. It's a property of the working fluid (for air, it's approximately 1.4).
