Frame-Dragging: The Lense-Thirring Effect

An animation of spacetime being "dragged" by a rotating massive object.

Plain-Language Overview

General Relativity predicts that a rotating mass doesn't just curve spacetime; it twists it, dragging it around like a vortex. This "frame-dragging" effect will cause the axis of a nearby gyroscope to precess. This simulation shows a top-down view of a rotating star, a swirling spacetime grid, and a gyroscope whose spin axis slowly rotates due to this effect. The effect shown is enormously exaggerated for visibility.

Beginner's Guide

1. Spacetime is Sticky

A rotating mass drags spacetime around, like a spinning bowling ball in honey. You can see this as the "swirl" in the spacetime grid.

2. Watching the Precession

Press Play and watch the gyroscope orbit. The red line is its spin axis. Because of frame-dragging, the twisted spacetime causes its spin axis to slowly rotate, or precess. The display shows how much the axis has rotated. Increase the mass or angular velocity to see a stronger effect.

Core Theory

The angular velocity of the gyroscope's precession (`Ω_p`) is given by the Lense-Thirring formula: Ω_p = (2GJ) / (c²r³), where `J` is the angular momentum of the spinning star (`J ∝ Mass * Angular Velocity`) and `r` is the orbital radius. The `1/r³` dependence means the effect drops off very rapidly with distance. This effect was famously confirmed by the Gravity Probe B satellite experiment.
