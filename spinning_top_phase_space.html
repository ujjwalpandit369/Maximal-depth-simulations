Welcome to the Spinning Top Simulator!

A simple spinning top exhibits surprisingly complex and beautiful motion. This simulation explores this motion and connects it to a deep idea in physics: the geometry of phase space.

Step 1: The Two Views

You see two panels:

  • 3D View: This shows a familiar spinning top, which is spinning rapidly around its own axis. Under the influence of gravity, its main axis will also wobble. This wobble is a combination of two motions: precession (the slow movement of the axis in a circle) and nutation (the "nodding" or up-and-down bobbing of the axis).
  • Phase Space Projection: This is an abstract map of the top's state. The horizontal axis is the precession angle (ψ) and the vertical axis is the nutation angle (θ). As the top moves, it traces a path on this map, revealing the intricate pattern of its motion.

Step 2: Start the Simulation

Use the sliders to set the initial conditions—how fast the top is spinning and its initial tilt angle. Then click "Start/Reset". Watch the top precess and nutate in the 3D view, and see the corresponding path traced in the phase space view. The path is complex, but it's not random; it's a regular, repeating orbit on a higher-dimensional surface.

Step 3: The Symplectic Manifold and Liouville's Theorem

This is the deep part. The "phase space" of all possible states of the top is not just a simple graph, but a type of mathematical object called a symplectic manifold. Systems that evolve on these manifolds have a remarkable property, described by Liouville's theorem: phase space volume is conserved.

Let's see this in action! Click the "Draw Phase Space Area" button. Now click and drag on the phase space canvas to draw a small rectangle. This rectangle represents a "bundle" of slightly different initial states for the top.

When you release the mouse, the simulation will trace the evolution of all the points in this bundle. Watch how the rectangle gets stretched and sheared into a distorted parallelogram shape. It changes shape dramatically, but look at the "Current Area" readout in the info panel—its area remains constant! This is a fundamental feature of Hamiltonian systems (like our frictionless top) and is deeply connected to the conservation of information in physics.
