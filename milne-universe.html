The Milne Universe

A simulation of an empty, expanding universe in the context of Special Relativity.

Est. runtime: Continuous | Recommended device: Desktop | Approx. size: 30 KB

Overview

The Milne model is a uniquely simple and elegant cosmological model. It describes an expanding, empty universe using only the principles of Einstein's Special Relativity, without needing the full complexity of General Relativity. Imagine that the universe was born from a single point, with particles flying outwards in all directions at constant speeds. This simulation visualizes the "worldlines" of these particles in a spacetime diagram and demonstrates a profound result: an observer on *any* particle would see all other particles receding from them with a velocity proportional to their distance, perfectly recreating Hubble's Law in a "trivial" way.

Beginner's Guide to a Coasting Cosmos

Let's explore a universe without forces. The Milne model is a "coasting" universe, where everything moves in straight lines forever. It's a great way to build intuition about spacetime and relative motion.

Step 1: Spacetime Diagrams and Worldlines

Normally, we think of space (3 dimensions) and time (1 dimension) separately. A spacetime diagram combines them. In this simulation, we show two dimensions of space (the flat grid) and one dimension of time (the vertical axis). An object's entire history—its path through space and time—can be drawn as a single line on this diagram. This path is called a worldline.

  • An object that is stationary in space moves only upwards in time. Its worldline is a straight vertical line.
  • An object moving at a constant velocity travels through space as it travels through time. Its worldline is a straight, tilted line. The faster it moves, the more tilted its worldline.

Step 2: The Milne Universe Setup

The Milne model begins with a simple premise: at time t=0, an infinite number of particles (galaxies) are created at a single point (the origin). They fly outwards in all directions, each with its own constant velocity. The simulation shows the worldlines of these particles. Since they all start at the origin and move at constant speeds, their worldlines are straight lines fanning out from the bottom of the diagram, forming a "light cone" shape.

Everyday Analogy: A Perfect Firework Explosion. Imagine a firework that explodes at a single point. Thousands of sparks fly outwards in straight lines, each with its own speed. A slow spark travels a short distance before fading, while a fast spark travels far. The collection of all these spark-paths forms an expanding sphere. The spacetime diagram you see is like a long-exposure photograph of this explosion, tracing every spark's complete journey.

Step 3: The Surprising Part - Hubble's Law for Free

Edwin Hubble famously observed that distant galaxies are receding from us faster than nearby ones (v = H₀d). In most cosmological models, this is a result of the expansion of space itself, driven by complex physics. The Milne model shows how this same law can emerge "for free," just from the geometry of relative motion.

In the simulation, you can click on any worldline to select that galaxy as your "observer." The view will transform into that galaxy's reference frame. You will see that from its perspective, it is stationary at the center, and all other galaxies are moving away from it. More importantly, if you were to measure their distances and speeds, you would find that their speed is directly proportional to their distance. Every observer sees the same Hubble Law, no matter which galaxy they are on!

Step 4: Using the Simulation

  1. Observe the Spacetime Diagram: The default view shows the "master frame" with time on the vertical axis. You see all the worldlines originating from a single point at the bottom.
  2. Select an Observer: Click on any worldline. The view will instantly change. The selected worldline will become the new vertical "time" axis, as that galaxy now considers itself stationary.
  3. Observe Relative Motion: Notice how all the other worldlines now seem to fan out from the new observer. This is the Hubble expansion from their point of view. A panel will show data confirming that v ∝ d.
  4. Try Different Observers: Click on a slow-moving (steep) worldline and then a fast-moving (shallow) worldline. The qualitative picture is always the same. This is the "Cosmological Principle" in action: the universe looks the same to all observers.

Worked Walkthroughs

Walkthrough: Proving Hubble's Law

  1. Start with the default "View from Origin". You are stationary.
  2. Click on a worldline that is tilted, representing a moving galaxy. Let's call it Galaxy A. The view transforms, and Galaxy A is now the stationary vertical line.
  3. Now, look at the worldline of the *original* observer. It is now a tilted line moving away in the opposite direction.
  4. Observe all other galaxies. They are all moving away from Galaxy A. A data panel will appear showing the calculated distance and velocity for a few sample galaxies, confirming that `velocity` is proportional to `distance`. You've just discovered Hubble's Law.

Micro-Experiments

  1. Symmetry: Select a galaxy moving to the right. Note the apparent velocity of a galaxy on the far left. Now, reset and select that galaxy on the far left. Is its apparent velocity for the right-moving galaxy the same? (Expected: Yes, the relative velocity is the same).
  2. The Light Cone: The edge of the fan of worldlines represents particles moving at the speed of light. Can you select an observer moving at this speed? (In this simulation, no, as we limit galaxy speeds to be less than light speed).

Core Theory: Special Relativity

The Milne model requires no General Relativity. The position of any galaxy is given by simple motion:

x(t) = v * t

Where v is the constant velocity vector. The magic happens when we switch observers. If you are on a galaxy with velocity vobs, the apparent velocity of another galaxy with velocity vother is not just vother - vobs. It must be calculated using the Lorentz transformations from Special Relativity.

This transformation ensures that no relative velocity exceeds the speed of light and naturally produces the linear relationship between distance and recession velocity (Hubble's Law) for all observers.

Advanced Notes & Extensions

Relation to FLRW: The Milne universe is mathematically equivalent to the special case of the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric where the density of matter and energy is zero (Ωm = 0, ΩΛ = 0) and the curvature is negative (Ωk = 1). The spacetime is a portion of flat Minkowski space, but described in coordinates that make the spatial expansion obvious.

Hyperbolic Geometry: At any given moment of time `t`, a slice of the Milne universe has the geometry of a 3D hyperbolic space (a space with constant negative curvature), not flat Euclidean space.

Analogies & Intuition

1. Firework Explosion (Physical Analogy)

As described in the guide, this is a great analogy. Sparks fly out from a central point at constant speeds. An observer riding on any one spark will see all other sparks moving away from them. The further away a spark is, the faster its relative speed will be.

Limitations: A firework is a finite explosion in a pre-existing space. The Milne model describes the behavior of spacetime itself, and the "explosion" involves an infinite number of particles.

2. Runners on a Track (Visualization Analogy)

Imagine a group of runners starting a race at the same point at the same time, each running at a different, constant speed. From the perspective of the starting line, the fastest runner is always furthest away. From the perspective of a runner in the middle of the pack, the faster runners are getting further ahead, and the slower runners are falling further behind. Everyone is moving away from them, and the speed at which they move away is proportional to how far they are from them.

Limitations: This is a 1D analogy and doesn't involve the relativistic velocity transformations that are key to the full model.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. Is the Milne model a realistic model of our universe?
No. Our universe contains matter and dark energy, and its expansion is governed by General Relativity. The Milne model is a "toy model" that is extremely useful for building intuition about spacetime, relative motion, and why Hubble's Law is not as strange as it might first appear.
2. Why are the worldlines straight?
Because in a spacetime diagram, a straight line represents an object moving at a constant velocity. The Milne model assumes the particles fly apart and then "coast" forever with no forces to accelerate or decelerate them.
3. What does "clicking on a worldline" do mathematically?
It applies a Lorentz transformation to the entire spacetime diagram, shifting the reference frame so that the selected worldline becomes the new axis of time (i.e., it becomes the stationary observer).
