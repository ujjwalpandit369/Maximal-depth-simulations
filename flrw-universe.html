The Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) Universe

An interactive dashboard for the standard model of a homogeneous and isotropic universe.

Overview

The Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) model is the cornerstone of modern cosmology. It describes a universe that is, on large scales, the same in all directions (isotropic) and from every location (homogeneous). This simulation lets you explore how the universe evolves based on its fundamental ingredients: matter, which tries to pull everything back together, and dark energy, which mysteriously pushes everything apart. By adjusting these cosmic knobs, you can create universes that expand forever, collapse in a "Big Crunch," or hover in a delicate balance, and you can visualize the very shape of spacetime itself.

Beginner's Guide to the Expanding Universe

Welcome to your own personal universe! This guide will walk you through the key ideas of modern cosmology using this simulation. We'll use simple steps and analogies to understand the big picture.

Step 1: The Biggest Idea - Expanding Space

The most important concept to grasp is that the universe is expanding. But it's not like an explosion where galaxies are flying away from a central point into empty space. Instead, space itself is stretching.

Everyday Analogy: A Baking Loaf of Raisin Bread. Imagine a big lump of raisin bread dough. The raisins are like galaxies, and the dough is space. As the loaf bakes and expands, every raisin moves away from every other raisin. A raisin doesn't have to "do" anything to move; the dough (space) expanding between them carries them apart. From the perspective of any single raisin, all the other raisins seem to be rushing away from it. This is what we see in our universe.

Step 2: The Cosmic Ingredients

What drives this expansion? The fate of the universe is determined by a cosmic tug-of-war between two main ingredients, which you can control with the sliders:

  • Matter Density (Ωm): This represents all the "stuff" in the universe – stars, galaxies, gas, and dark matter. Matter has gravity, and gravity pulls things together. Think of it as the universe's anchor or brake, trying to slow the expansion down. The more matter, the stronger the pull.
  • Dark Energy Density (ΩΛ): This is one of the biggest mysteries in science. It's a form of energy that seems to be embedded in the fabric of spacetime itself, and it has the opposite effect of gravity. It acts like a cosmic accelerator, pushing everything apart at an ever-increasing rate.

The "Ω" (Omega) symbol represents a density parameter. It's a ratio: the actual density of a substance compared to the "critical density" needed to make the universe geometrically flat.

Step 3: The Shape of the Universe (Curvature, Ωk)

The total amount of "stuff" (Matter + Dark Energy) determines the overall shape, or geometry, of the universe. This is what we call curvature, and the simulation calculates it for you as: Ωk = 1 - Ωm - ΩΛ.

Visualization Analogy: A Universe You Can Walk On. Imagine you are a 2D being living on a surface. The geometry of your universe could be one of three types, which this simulation shows with the 3D grid:

  • If ΩTotal = 1 (i.e., Ωk = 0), the universe is Flat. Like a giant, infinite sheet of paper, parallel lines stay parallel forever. This is what astronomers believe our universe is like.
  • If ΩTotal > 1 (i.e., Ωk < 0), the universe is Closed or "spherical." Like the surface of a sphere, if you walk in a straight line, you eventually end up back where you started. This universe contains enough matter to eventually halt its expansion and collapse back on itself in a "Big Crunch."
  • If ΩTotal < 1 (i.e., Ωk > 0), the universe is Open or "hyperbolic." Its shape is like a saddle, curving away from itself at every point. This universe doesn't have enough matter to stop its expansion and will expand forever.

Watch how the 3D grid changes shape as you move the sliders above and below a total Omega of 1.

Step 4: Using the Simulation

Now you have all the concepts. Here's how to use the tools:

  1. Use the Sliders: Adjust Ωm and ΩΛ to define your universe. Notice how the "Total Density (ΩTotal)" and "Curvature (Ωk)" values change automatically.
  2. Watch the Graphs: The "Scale Factor a(t)" graph is the most important. It shows the size of your universe over time. A steep curve means rapid expansion. A curve that goes up and then down means a "Big Crunch."
  3. Check the Pie Chart: The "Dominant Era" chart shows which ingredient is "winning" the cosmic tug-of-war at any given time. In our own universe, matter dominated early on, but now dark energy is taking over.
  4. Try the Presets: The easiest way to start is to click the preset buttons like "Our Universe" to see a simulation of the cosmos we live in.

You are now ready to explore the fate of the cosmos. Happy universe-building!

Worked Example

The expansion rate of the universe is governed by the Friedmann Equation. A simplified version is: Expansion Rate² ∝ (Ωm / Size³) + (Ωk / Size²) + ΩΛ. Let's plug in the default values for "Our Universe" (Ωm=0.3, ΩΛ=0.7, so Ωk=0) when the universe is at its current size (Size=1):

Rate² ∝ (0.3 / 1³) + (0 / 1²) + 0.7 = 0.3 + 0 + 0.7 = 1.0. The expansion is driven by both matter and dark energy.

Walkthrough 1: The Power of Matter

  1. Start with the "Empty Universe" preset (Ωm=0, ΩΛ=0). The expansion is a straight line.
  2. Now, increase Ωm to 1.0. The total density is now 1, so the universe is flat. Notice how the expansion curve is no longer straight; it's bending downwards. Gravity is putting the brakes on! The expansion rate slows down over time.
  3. Analogy: This is like throwing a ball in the air. At first it moves fast, but Earth's gravity constantly slows it down. More matter means more gravity.

Walkthrough 2: The Mystery of Dark Energy

  1. Start with the "Our Universe" preset (Ωm=0.3, ΩΛ=0.7). Notice the expansion curve starts bending down (matter-dominated) and then starts bending up (dark energy-dominated).
  2. Now, keep Ωm at 0.3 but increase ΩΛ to 1.5. The curve becomes much steeper, much faster. The initial matter-dominated era is shorter, and the final acceleration is extreme.
  3. Analogy: Dark energy is like having a rocket engine attached to the ball you threw. Not only does it counteract gravity, it pushes the ball away even faster over time.

Micro-Experiments

  1. Create a "Static" Universe: Can you find a balance that makes the scale factor graph almost flat? Try setting Ωm high and ΩΛ high. You'll see this is very unstable (this is related to the Einstein Static Universe model).

  3. The "Hesitating" Universe: Set Ωm = 0.3 and ΩΛ = 0.3. The universe expands, but very slowly. It's in a long "hesitation" before it commits to expanding forever.
  4. Maximum Age: Which universe lives the longest before collapsing? Try different values for Ωm > 1 with ΩΛ = 0. Does a universe with Ωm = 1.1 live longer or shorter than one with Ωm = 3.0? (Expected: Higher density leads to a faster collapse).
  5. Dominance Transition: Using the "Our Universe" preset, pause the simulation and step through time. At what time (approx.) does the Dark Energy slice of the pie chart become bigger than the Matter slice? (This marks the transition to accelerated expansion).
  6. Curvature's Effect: Compare two universes with the same Ωm = 0.3, but one with ΩΛ = 0 (Open) and one with ΩΛ = 0.7 (Flat). How does adding dark energy to "flatten" the universe affect its expansion?
  7. The Coasting Point: For the "Our Universe" preset, find the point on the scale factor graph where the curve stops bending down and starts bending up. This is the "inflection point" where acceleration began.
  8. Extreme Dark Energy: Set Ωm = 0.1 and ΩΛ = 2.5. Observe the incredibly rapid, exponential expansion. This is known as a "de Sitter" like universe.

Core Theory: The Friedmann Equation

The heart of this simulation is the Friedmann Equation. It's derived from Einstein's theory of General Relativity and describes the expansion of spacetime. A simplified form of the equation for the Hubble parameter, H (the expansion rate), is:

H(t)2 = H02 [ Ωm,0 / a(t)3 + Ωk,0 / a(t)2 + ΩΛ,0 ]

Plain-English Translation

  • H(t)2: This is the "Expansion Speed" squared at any time 't'.
  • H02: This is the initial "Expansion Speed" squared, at the beginning of the simulation. It sets the overall pace.
  • Ωm,0 / a(t)3: This is the Matter term. As the universe expands (a(t) gets bigger), the density of matter dilutes very quickly (by a factor of the volume, or size cubed). Its gravitational pull gets weaker over time.
  • Ωk,0 / a(t)2: This is the Curvature term. It represents the initial geometry of the universe and dilutes less quickly than matter.
  • ΩΛ,0: This is the Dark Energy term. Crucially, it's a constant! As the universe expands, the density of dark energy stays the same. This is why it eventually dominates everything else.

This simulation solves this equation step-by-step to find the scale factor a(t) at each moment in time, then uses that to draw the graphs and the grid.

Advanced Notes & Extensions

Numerical Stability: The simulation uses a simple Euler or RK2 integrator to solve the Friedmann equation. This is fast and works well for most parameter values. However, for extreme values or near the "Big Crunch" singularity (where a(t) approaches 0), the steps must be very small to maintain accuracy. A real cosmological simulator would use an adaptive step-size integrator.

Edge Cases: We have restricted Ωm and ΩΛ to be positive. In some exotic theories, "phantom energy" corresponds to ΩΛ < -1, which could lead to a "Big Rip." Negative matter density is generally considered unphysical.

How to Extend This Simulation:

  • Add Radiation: A complete model would include a term for radiation, Ωr, which dilutes even faster than matter (as 1/a(t)4). This is only significant in the very early universe.
  • Symplectic Integrator: For better long-term energy conservation in the calculations, one could replace the current integrator with a symplectic one like the Verlet method, though it's more suited to n-body simulations than ODEs.
  • More Precise Age Calculation: The age of the universe is calculated by integrating 1/H(t) with respect to a(t). This simulation does it numerically, but for specific cases (like Ωm=1, ΩΛ=0), there is an exact analytical formula: Age = 2 / (3H0).

Analogies & Intuition

1. The Expanding Balloon (Physical Analogy)

Imagine drawing dots on the surface of a balloon and then inflating it. The dots (galaxies) move apart from each other not because they are moving on the surface, but because the surface (spacetime) itself is stretching. This is the best simple analogy for the expansion of space.

Limitations: The balloon is a 2D surface expanding in a 3D space. Our 3D universe isn't expanding "into" a 4D space. Also, the balloon has a center, whereas the universe does not.

2. Raisin Bread Dough (Visualization Analogy)

As mentioned in the guide, raisins in an expanding loaf of bread all move away from each other. This correctly models the idea of homogeneity – the universe looks the same from any "raisin." It also helps visualize how expansion can be faster for more distant objects (raisins on the far side of the loaf move away faster).

Limitations: The bread has edges, the universe (as far as we know) does not. The expansion of the dough is also caused by chemical reactions, not a fundamental property of the dough itself like dark energy.

3. The Zooming Spreadsheet (Computational Analogy)

Imagine a giant spreadsheet where each cell is a point in space. The distance between cells is initially 1 unit. The simulation's "scale factor" a(t) is like a global zoom setting. At each time step, we update the zoom. If a(t) doubles, the distance between every cell doubles. The "galaxies" in the cells don't move from cell A1 to A2; the entire grid just gets bigger. The rules for updating the zoom factor (the Friedmann equation) depend on the sum of values in all the cells (matter) and a fixed "background energy" (dark energy).

Limitations: This analogy treats space as a discrete grid, whereas it is continuous (a continuum). It's good for thinking about the computation, but less so for the physical geometry.

FAQ & Troubleshooting

1. What is "critical density"?
It's the exact amount of matter and energy needed to make the universe geometrically flat (ΩTotal = 1). If the actual density is higher, the universe is closed; if lower, it's open.
2. Where is the center of the universe? Where did the Big Bang happen?
There is no center. The Big Bang wasn't an explosion in space, but an explosion *of* space. It happened everywhere at once. Every point in the universe can be considered the "center" of its own observable patch.
3. How can Ω be greater than 1? Isn't that more than 100%?
The Ω values are not percentages of a whole in the traditional sense. They are ratios of a measured density to a *different*, theoretical density (the critical density). So, if your universe has twice the critical density of matter, Ωm will be 2.0.
4. Does the universe expand into anything?
No. The universe is, by definition, all there is. Spacetime itself is expanding; it doesn't require an "outside" to expand into.
5. Why does the pie chart change over time?
Because the densities of matter and dark energy change differently. Matter dilutes as space expands, but dark energy's density remains constant. So, even if you start with lots of matter, dark energy's influence will inevitably grow and take over as the universe gets bigger.
6. The simulation is running slow or freezing.
Try toggling the "Performance Mode" on, which may reduce the number of points calculated for the graphs or the grid resolution. If it hangs, reloading the page with default settings is the best option. If the problem persists, open your browser's developer console (F12) and report any errors you see.
