Gravitational Lensing Simulator

An interactive tool to see how gravity bends light.

Plain-Language Overview

According to General Relativity, the gravity from a massive object, like a galaxy or a black hole, curves the spacetime around it. Light, just like any other object, must travel through this curved spacetime. From our perspective, this makes it look like the light's path has been "bent" by gravity. This effect is called gravitational lensing. If a massive object (the "lens") lies between us and a distant light source (like a quasar), the light can be bent in such a way that we see multiple, distorted images of the source. This simulation lets you set up your own cosmic alignment to see how this amazing phenomenon works.

Beginner's Guide

1. The Setup

The main panel shows a top-down view of a simplified universe:

  • The Observer (You): Represented by an eye symbol on the far left. This is your fixed position.
  • The Lens (Orange Circle): This is a massive object like a galaxy cluster. Its gravity will bend the light rays. You can drag this object.
  • The Source (Blue Star): This is a distant object like a quasar, emitting light in all directions. You can also drag this object.

Lines are drawn from the source, representing rays of light. Notice how they bend as they pass by the lensing mass.

2. What the Observer Sees

The smaller panel on the right shows what the observer actually sees. Because the light paths are bent, the source doesn't appear to be where it actually is. Instead, you see one or more lensed images. These images are formed by tracing the bent light rays that reach your eye backwards in a straight line to their apparent origin.

3. Creating Cosmic Illusions

Try dragging the objects to create different lensing effects:

  • Multiple Images: When the source is slightly off-center behind the lens, you will typically see two distorted images. One image forms from light that passed "above" the lens, and the other from light that passed "below."
  • Einstein Ring: The most dramatic effect occurs when you align the Observer, Lens, and Source in a perfect straight line. When this happens, the lensed image is smeared into a perfect circle around the lensing mass. This is called an Einstein Ring, and they are one of the most beautiful confirmations of General Relativity.
  • Gravitational Arcs: If the alignment is very good but not quite perfect, the images will be stretched into bright, curved arcs. Astronomers observe these arcs frequently when looking at distant galaxy clusters.

The Deflection of Light

General Relativity predicts that a light ray passing a massive object M at a closest distance `b` (the "impact parameter") will be deflected by an angle `α`.

α = 4GM / (bc2)

Where `G` is the gravitational constant and `c` is the speed of light. This simulation uses a simplified version of this formula where the deflection angle is proportional to `Mass / distance`. By tracing multiple light rays and finding which ones reach the observer, we can reconstruct the lensed images. The "Observer's View" is constructed by taking the angle of each incoming light ray that hits the observer and plotting a point at that angle on the viewscreen.
