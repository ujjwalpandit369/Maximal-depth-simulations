Plain-Language Overview

While we often imagine atomic nuclei as perfect little spheres, the reality is that many are not. Depending on the number of protons and neutrons they contain, nuclei can be stretched like a football or squashed like a doorknob. The "electric quadrupole moment" is the physical quantity that measures this deformation. This simulation visualizes the shape of a nucleus as you change its composition, showing how nuclei with "magic numbers" of protons or neutrons tend to be spherical, while others become deformed.

Beginner's Guide — Spheres, Footballs, and Doorknobs

Step 1: The Spherical Ideal

The simplest model of a nucleus treats it like a drop of liquid, which naturally pulls itself into a sphere to minimize surface tension. Furthermore, the Nuclear Shell Model tells us that nuclei with filled energy shells are exceptionally stable. These nuclei, which have a "magic number" of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126), are indeed found to be nearly perfect spheres.

Step 2: Deformed Nuclei

However, when a nucleus has a number of protons and neutrons that is far from a magic number, its outer nucleons are in partially filled shells. In this situation, the nucleus can often find a lower, more stable energy state by deforming its shape as a whole. The collective interactions of the outer nucleons cause the entire nucleus to stretch or squash.

Step 3: Prolate vs. Oblate

There are two primary types of this deformation:

  • Prolate (`Q > 0`): The nucleus is stretched along its spin axis, like an American football or a rugby ball. This is the most common type of deformation.
  • Oblate (`Q < 0`): The nucleus is squashed along its spin axis, like a doorknob or a spinning top.
The **electric quadrupole moment (`Q`)** is the value that tells us the type and magnitude of the deformation. A positive `Q` means prolate, a negative `Q` means oblate, and a `Q` of zero means the nucleus is perfectly spherical.

Step 4: The Simulation

Use the sliders to change the number of protons (Z) and neutrons (N). The simulation uses a simplified model based on experimental data to determine the shape. Try setting Z or N to a magic number (like 20, 28, 50, or 82) and observe how the nucleus becomes spherical (`Q` ≈ 0). Then, move to a region halfway between magic numbers (like Z=66, N=100 in the rare-earth metals) to see a strong prolate deformation.

Measuring Deformation

The electric quadrupole moment `Q` is a measure of the deviation of the nuclear charge distribution from spherical symmetry. For a nucleus with its spin axis along the z-direction, it is defined by the expectation value of the operator:

Q ∝ ⟨ Σ (3z_i² - r_i²) ⟩

Where the sum is over all protons. This is a complex quantum mechanical calculation. Intuitively, it measures the difference between the extent of the nucleus along the spin axis (`z`) and its extent in the perpendicular plane (`r`).

  • If the nucleus is stretched along z (prolate), `z²` is large, and `Q > 0`.
  • If the nucleus is squashed along z (oblate), `z²` is small, and `Q < 0`.
  • If the nucleus is spherical, `⟨z²⟩ = ⟨x²⟩ = ⟨y²⟩`, and since `r²=x²+y²+z²`, we have `⟨3z² - r²⟩ = 0`, so `Q = 0`.
The unit of quadrupole moment is the "barn" (b), where 1 barn = 10⁻²⁸ m².
