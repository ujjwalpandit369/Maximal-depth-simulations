What is the Straightest Path?

On a flat piece of paper, the shortest, straightest path between two points is a straight line. But what about on a curved surface, like the Earth? You can't draw a "straight" line in the usual sense. The equivalent concept is a geodesic.

Step 1: Geodesics on a Sphere

Make sure "Sphere" is selected. Click on two different points on the sphere. A red line is drawn between them. This is the geodesic! For a sphere, the geodesics are always arcs of a great circle (a circle whose center is the center of the sphere, like the Earth's equator). This is the path a plane flies to save fuel, and it's the path light would take across the surface.

Step 2: Geodesics on Other Surfaces

Now try the "Torus" (a donut) or the "Hyperboloid" (a saddle). Click two points again. The paths look much more complex! The simulation is numerically solving a complex set of "geodesic equations" to find these paths. They might spiral around the torus or fly off the hyperboloid. In each case, this is the "straightest" the path can possibly be while being confined to the surface.

Step 3: Parallel Transport and Curvature

How can we "feel" the curvature of the surface? By using a concept called parallel transport. Once you have a geodesic, click the "Animate Parallel Transport" button.

An arrow will appear at the start of your path. As it moves along the geodesic, it will try to stay "as parallel to itself as possible." On a flat surface, it would never change its direction. But on a curved surface, its direction changes!

Try this experiment on the sphere:

  1. Click a point on the "equator."
  2. Click a point on the "north pole." A geodesic is drawn.
  3. Click "Animate Parallel Transport." An arrow starts on the equator, pointing, for example, east.
  4. As the arrow moves to the pole and then comes back down another longitude line, watch its direction. When it returns to the equator, it will no longer be pointing in its original direction!
The amount the arrow has rotated after returning to its starting point is a measure of the total curvature enclosed by the path. This is a profound idea in geometry and is central to Einstein's theory of General Relativity, where gravity is not a force, but the curvature of spacetime itself.
