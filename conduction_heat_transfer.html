Heat Transfer: Conduction

An interactive simulation of heat flow through a solid object.

Overview

Conduction is one of the three main ways heat is transferred. It's the process by which heat energy is transmitted through collisions between neighboring atoms or molecules. When you touch a hot stove, the heat you feel is transferred through conduction. This simulation visualizes heat conducting through a solid rod placed between a hot reservoir and a cold reservoir, allowing you to explore the factors that determine the rate of heat flow, as described by Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction.

The Law of Heat Conduction

The rate at which heat flows through an object is described by a simple and elegant formula known as Fourier's Law.

P = k * A * (Tₕ - T꜀) / L

Let's break down each part of the equation, all of which you can control with the sliders:

  • P: The Power, or rate of heat flow, measured in Watts (Joules per second). This is what the simulation calculates and visualizes. A higher value means heat is moving more quickly.
  • k: The Thermal Conductivity of the material. This is an intrinsic property of a substance that measures how well it conducts heat. Metals like copper have a very high 'k', while insulators like wood or styrofoam have a very low 'k'.
  • A: The Cross-Sectional Area of the rod. A thicker rod provides more pathways for heat to travel, increasing the overall rate of flow.
  • (Tₕ - T꜀): The Temperature Difference between the hot end and the cold end. Heat flows from hot to cold, and a larger temperature difference provides a stronger "push" for the heat to move.
  • L: The Length of the rod. The longer the path the heat has to travel, the slower the rate of flow will be.

Analogy: Water Flowing Through a Pipe

You can think of heat conduction like water flowing through a pipe. The rate of water flow (gallons per minute) is analogous to the heat flow rate (Watts).

  • The temperature difference (Tₕ - T꜀) is like the pressure difference between the two ends of the pipe. Higher pressure difference pushes more water through.
  • The cross-sectional area (A) is like the pipe's diameter. A wider pipe lets more water flow.
  • The length (L) is the pipe's length. A longer pipe creates more resistance, reducing the flow.
  • The thermal conductivity (k) is like the smoothness of the inside of the pipe. A very smooth pipe (like copper for heat) offers little resistance, while a pipe clogged with gravel (like wood for heat) offers a lot of resistance.

Parameters & Worked Examples

ParameterDescriptionWorked Example
Thermal Conductivity (k) A material property indicating how well it conducts heat. Units: W/(m·K). For Copper (k=401), with A=0.01, ΔT=100, L=0.5, the Power is P = 401 * 0.01 * 100 / 0.5 = 802 W. For Glass (k=1.0), the Power is P = 1 * 0.01 * 100 / 0.5 = 2 W.
Cross-Sectional Area (A) The thickness of the rod. Units: m². With default values (k=401, ΔT=100, L=0.5), if A is doubled from 0.01 to 0.02 m², the Power doubles from 802 W to 1604 W.
Length of Rod (L) The distance the heat must travel. Units: m. With default values (k=401, A=0.01, ΔT=100), if L is doubled from 0.5 to 1.0 m, the Power is halved from 802 W to 401 W.
Temperature Difference (ΔT) The "driving force" for heat flow. Units: °C or K. With default values (k=401, A=0.01, L=0.5), if the temperature difference (Tₕ - T꜀) is halved from 100°C to 50°C, the Power is halved from 802 W to 401 W.
