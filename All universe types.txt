4. The Milne Universe
Create a simulation of an empty, expanding universe in the context of Special Relativity.
Scene Description: A 3D spacetime diagram (1 time, 2 space dimensions).
Interactive Elements:
Guide: The Milne model is a special case of the FLRW universe where the density is zero. It can be fully described by Special Relativity without needing General Relativity. It represents a universe where a ball of particles was created at a single event and has been expanding outwards at constant velocity ever since.
Visual Guides:
Particle Worldlines: The simulation shows the worldlines of the "galaxies" as straight lines emanating from the origin of the spacetime diagram.
Hubble's Law: An observer on any one of the worldlines will perceive all other galaxies to be receding with a velocity proportional to their distance, thus satisfying Hubble's Law in a "trivial" way without curved spacetime.

Part 2: Cyclic and Bouncing Universes
6. The Cyclic Universe (Steinhardt-Turok Model)
Create a conceptual animation of a universe that undergoes cycles of expansion and contraction.
Scene Description: A timeline showing multiple "aeons" or cycles. Our 3D universe is represented as a "brane."
Interactive Elements:
Guide: This model, based on M-theory, proposes that our universe is a 3D brane that collides periodically with another parallel brane in a higher dimension. Each collision is a "Big Bang" that fills our universe with hot matter and radiation. The branes then pull apart (expansion), cool, and are eventually drawn back together by a force for the next collision.
Visuals: An animation shows the two branes moving in a hidden dimension, colliding (Big Bang), pulling apart (expansion, dark energy era), and then re-collapsing for the next cycle. This avoids the singularity problem.

7. The Conformal Cyclic Cosmology (Penrose)
Create a visualization of Penrose's theory linking the end of one universe to the beginning of the next.
Scene Description: A timeline of a single "aeon."
Interactive Elements:
Sliders:
"Time" slider that goes far into the future.
Guide: Penrose's CCC model proposes that in the far future, after all black holes have evaporated, the universe will contain only massless particles. At that point, the universe "forgets" its sense of scale. A mathematical trick called a conformal transformation can map the infinitely large, cold future of one aeon onto the infinitely small, hot Big Bang of the next.
Visuals: The simulation shows the universe expanding and becoming empty. Then, a "conformal squish" animation transforms this cold, empty space into the hot, dense state of a new Big Bang. It can also show how supermassive black hole collisions from a previous aeon might leave detectable signatures ("Hawking points") in our CMB.

8. The "Big Bounce" Universe (Loop Quantum Cosmology)
Create a simulation of the universe bouncing instead of starting from a singularity.
Scene Description: A plot of the scale factor a(t) vs. time, centered around t=0.
Interactive Elements:
Buttons: Toggle between "Classical Big Bang" and "Loop Quantum Cosmology Bounce."
Guide: Loop Quantum Cosmology (LQC) applies quantum gravity principles to the early universe. It predicts that as the universe is traced back in time, quantum geometric effects create a repulsive force at extremely high (Planck) densities, preventing the formation of a singularity. Instead, a preceding, contracting universe "bounces" into our current expanding one.
Visuals:
Classical: The a(t) curve goes to zero at t=0.
LQC: The a(t) curve is shown reaching a minimum, non-zero value and then "bouncing" back out. An animation can show the universe contracting, bouncing, and re-expanding.

9. The Tolman Cyclical Universe
Create a simulation of an oscillating universe with growing entropy.
Scene Description: A plot of the scale factor a(t) vs. time over several cycles.
Interactive Elements:
Guide: This was an early cyclical model proposed by Richard Tolman. It involves a closed universe (Ω > 1) that undergoes cycles of expansion and re-collapse. Tolman showed that due to the second law of thermodynamics, the entropy of the universe would have to increase with each cycle.
Visuals: The a(t) vs. time graph shows a series of "bounces." Crucially, the maximum size and the duration of each successive cycle are shown to be larger than the previous one, reflecting the increase in entropy. A bar graph for entropy is shown increasing with each bounce.

Visuals: Before the measurement, a single timeline is shown. When the button is pressed, the timeline is animated splitting into two branches. In one branch, the particle is measured "spin up," and the observer sees that outcome. In the other, parallel branch, the particle is measured "spin down."
