Black Holes and Escape Velocity

An interactive simulation on the formation of a black hole.

Plain-Language Overview

If you throw a ball up, it falls back down. If you throw it hard enough—at the "escape velocity"—it would never fall back. A black hole is an object so dense that its escape velocity is greater than the speed of light. Since nothing can travel faster than light, nothing can escape. This simulation lets you design your own planet. By changing its mass and radius, you can see how the escape velocity changes. If you make the planet dense enough to cross its "Schwarzschild Radius," you will create a black hole.

Beginner's Guide

1. Escape Velocity

Escape velocity is the minimum speed needed for an object to "break free" from gravity without further propulsion. For Earth, it's about 11.2 km/s. In this simulation, a projectile is fired at a fixed speed. Whether it escapes depends on the planet's properties.

2. Mass and Radius

Escape velocity depends on mass and radius. Increase the Mass, and escape velocity increases. *Decrease* the Radius (making the planet denser), and escape velocity also increases.

3. The Schwarzschild Radius

The Schwarzschild Radius is the radius at which the escape velocity equals the speed of light. Try to create a black hole: increase the mass and decrease the radius until the planet's radius is less than its Schwarzschild Radius. The planet will turn black, and the projectile will no longer be able to escape. The edge of this black sphere is the event horizon.

Core Theory

The escape velocity vₑ is derived by setting kinetic energy equal to gravitational potential energy: vₑ = √(2GM/R). The Schwarzschild Radius `Rₛ` is found by setting `vₑ = c` and solving for R: Rₛ = 2GM/c².
