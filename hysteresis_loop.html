A simulation of the magnetization of a ferromagnetic material.

Guide: The "Memory" of Magnets

Magnetic Domains

A ferromagnetic material like iron is composed of tiny regions called magnetic domains. Within each domain, the atomic magnetic moments are aligned, making it a tiny, powerful magnet. However, in an unmagnetized piece of iron, the domains are randomly oriented, so their magnetic effects cancel each other out. The animation on the left shows a simplified view of these domains.

The Hysteresis Loop

The graph on the right plots the material's overall Magnetization (M) versus the External Field (H) you apply with the slider.

  1. Initial Magnetization: Starting from H=0, as you increase H, the domains aligned with the field grow and others flip to align. M increases rapidly.
  2. Saturation: Eventually, nearly all domains are aligned. The material is saturated, and M no longer increases much with H.
  3. Remanence: Now, decrease H back to zero. Notice that M does not go back to zero! The domains are "sticky" and tend to stay aligned. This leftover magnetization is called remanence. The material is now a permanent magnet.
  4. Coercivity: To force the magnetization back to zero, you must apply a negative field. The strength of the reverse field needed to wipe out the remanent magnetization is called coercivity.
As you sweep the field back and forth, you trace out a complete "hysteresis loop." The "fatness" of this loop is characteristic of the material. "Hard" magnetic materials (for permanent magnets) have a wide loop, while "soft" materials (for transformers) have a very thin loop.

Energy Loss

It takes energy to change the orientation of the magnetic domains against their "stickiness." This energy is dissipated as heat in the material. The area enclosed by the hysteresis loop is directly proportional to the energy lost per cycle. This is why transformers, which rapidly cycle the magnetic field in their iron cores, are designed with materials that have very "thin" hysteresis loops to minimize energy waste.
