Plain-Language Overview

Osmosis is a fundamental process in biology and chemistry, responsible for everything from how plant roots absorb water to how our kidneys filter blood. It's the spontaneous movement of a solvent (like water) across a special kind of barrier—a "semi-permeable membrane"—that lets the solvent pass but blocks other dissolved substances (solutes). This simulation gives you a microscopic view of this process. You'll see small water molecules moving freely across a membrane, while larger solute molecules are trapped on one side. This leads to a net flow of water towards the side with the solute, which in a U-shaped tube, causes the water level to rise, creating a pressure difference known as osmotic pressure.

Beginner's Guide: Why Water Moves Toward Salt

Step 1: The Semi-Permeable Membrane

Imagine a fence with holes that are just big enough for a cat to squeeze through, but too small for a dog. This is a "semi-permeable membrane." It's a barrier that is selective about what it lets pass. In our simulation, the vertical dashed line is a membrane that allows the small blue water molecules to pass through, but blocks the larger red solute molecules.

Step 2: The Setup

The simulation shows a container divided in two. The left side contains pure solvent (water). The right side contains a solution—water with a solute dissolved in it. Both sides start at the same water level. The particles are all in random motion.

Step 3: The Flow of Water (Osmosis)

Watch the particles closely. Water molecules from both sides will occasionally hit the membrane and pass through. However, on the right side, the solute molecules also bump into the membrane, effectively "blocking" it some of the time and reducing the rate at which water can leave that side. Because water can leave the pure solvent side more easily than it can leave the solution side, there is a **net flow of water from left to right**. This net movement of a solvent across a semi-permeable membrane is called **osmosis**.

You can think of it as nature trying to equalize the *concentration* of water on both sides. Since the right side has solute taking up space, its water concentration is lower, so water from the high-concentration side (left) moves in to try and dilute it.

Step 4: Osmotic Pressure

In a U-shaped tube, this net flow of water would cause the water level on the right side to physically rise. The extra height of water creates a downward pressure due to its weight (hydrostatic pressure). This pressure pushes back on the water, making it harder for more water to flow from left to right. The flow continues until the hydrostatic pressure created by the height difference is exactly equal and opposite to the "osmotic potential" driving the flow. The pressure at this equilibrium point is called the **osmotic pressure (Π)**. In the simulation, you can see this represented by the rising water level in the U-tube diagram.

Core Theory: The van 't Hoff Equation

The osmotic pressure created by a solute in a solution can be calculated, for dilute solutions, using the van 't Hoff equation, which bears a striking resemblance to the Ideal Gas Law.

1. The van 't Hoff Equation

Π = iMRT

Where:

  • Π (Pi) is the osmotic pressure in atmospheres.
  • i is the van 't Hoff factor, a dimensionless number representing the number of particles the solute splits into in solution (e.g., i=1 for sugar, i=2 for NaCl). We assume i=1 here.
  • M is the molar concentration of the solute (moles per liter).
  • R is the ideal gas constant (0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K)).
  • T is the absolute temperature in Kelvin.

2. Hydrostatic Pressure

The osmotic pressure is balanced at equilibrium by the hydrostatic pressure from the difference in fluid height (h) in a U-tube.

Phydrostatic = ρgh

Where ρ is the density of the solvent and g is the acceleration due to gravity. Equilibrium is reached when Π = ρgh.
