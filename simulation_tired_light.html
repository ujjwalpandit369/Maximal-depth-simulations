The Static "Tired Light" Universe

A historical (but disproven) alternative to the Big Bang.

Overview

When we observe distant galaxies, their light is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum. The standard explanation is that the universe is expanding, stretching the light waves as they travel. The "Tired Light" hypothesis was a historical competitor that proposed a different idea: the universe is static, and photons simply lose energy—getting "tired"—on their long journey through space. This simulation visualizes the core idea of tired light and explains one of the key observations (the Tolman surface brightness test) that demonstrates why this model doesn't match reality.

Why Tired Light Fails: The Tolman Test

In a static "tired light" universe, a distant galaxy would appear dimmer but would have the same apparent size as a nearby one. This means its surface brightness (brightness per unit area) would decrease dramatically with distance.

In an expanding universe, the galaxy also appears dimmer, but its apparent size also gets smaller due to distance, and time dilation reduces the rate we receive photons. These effects cancel out in a specific way.

Observation: Real galaxies' surface brightness remains nearly constant with distance, which matches the expanding universe model and refutes the tired light hypothesis.

Beginner's Guide to Tired Light

1. The Mystery of Redshift

In the 1920s, astronomers like Edwin Hubble made a startling discovery: the light from almost all distant galaxies is "redshifted." This means the wavelengths of their light are stretched out, making them appear redder than they should be. The farther away the galaxy, the greater the redshift. This was a cosmic mystery that demanded an explanation.

2. Two Competing Ideas

Two main hypotheses emerged to explain this observation:

  1. The Expanding Universe: This idea, which became the basis of the Big Bang model, suggests that space itself is expanding. As a photon travels through this expanding space, its wavelength gets stretched along with it. A longer wavelength means redder light.
  2. Tired Light: Proposed by Fritz Zwicky in 1929, this hypothesis suggested the universe was static and infinite. It proposed that photons lose energy as they travel over vast cosmic distances, perhaps by interacting with other particles or through some unknown physical process. Since a photon's energy is related to its frequency (`E = hf`), a loss of energy means a lower frequency and thus a longer, redder wavelength.

While intuitive, this idea has been ruled out by multiple lines of evidence, most famously the Tolman surface brightness test described in the panel next to the simulation, as well as the existence of the Cosmic Microwave Background and observations of time dilation in distant supernovae.
